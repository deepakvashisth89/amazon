ONCE UPON A CRIME: HISTORICAL MYSTERIES FROM ELLERY QUEEN'S MYSTERY MAGAZINE (New York, 1994), edited by Janet Hutchings, is a very uneven anthology of 13 crime stories—4 novelettes and 9 short stories—set in time periods ranging from the 1st century B.C. to the 1930s. Three of these works utilize real historical people as investigators in fictional cases, and 2 other works offer "solutions" to the mysterious deaths of real historical people.In order of their appearance in this anthology, the 13 stories are——1. THE LEMURES by Steven Saylor (first published in ELLERY QUEEN'S MYSTERY MAGAZINE, Oct. 1992); a Roman detective named Gordianus solves two unrelated cases involving the appearances of ghosts in the 1st century B.C.—2. "The Prince Who Was a Thief" by Theodore Dreiser (first published in 1927 in Dreiser's CHAINS; reprinted in EQMM, Mar. 1945); in medieval Baghdad, a kidnapped prince lives by his wits and gains love, wealth, and high rank as a result.—3. A LIGHT ON THE ROAD TO WOODSTOCK by Ellis Peters (pen name of Edith Pargeter; first published in 1985 in WINTER'S CRIMES 17, ed. George Hardinge; reprinted in EQMM, Apr. 1992); in the autumn of 1120, series detective Cadfael (not yet Brother Cadfael) saves the life of his military superior and ensures that justice is done in a lawsuit.—4. "Witch Hunt" by Terry Mullins (EQMM, Oct. 1992); with the help of world traveler Sir John Mandeville, Alain Schram solves who has organized a rather inept coven of witches in 14th century Flanders.—5. "Gather Not Thy Rose" by Miriam Grace Monfredo (EQMM, July 1992); while some historians have thought that Robert Dudley (a supposed lover of Queen Elizabeth I) murdered his wife, this story presents an alternative solution.—6. "Death of a Noverint" by William Bankier (EQMM, Apr. 1992); the stabbing death of playwright Christopher Marlowe is provided an alternative solution.—7. "The William Shakespeare Murder Case" by George Baxt (EQMM, Oct. 1987); during rehearsals for HAMLET, an actor is found stabbed, and Shakespeare himself solves the mystery, clearing his wife in the process.—8. "The Earl's Nightingale" by Lillian de la Torre (EQMM, May 1989); James Boswell narrates how Dr. Samuel Johnson solved the murder of an earl's beautiful mistress in David Garrick's theater in the late 18th century.—9. CHINOISERIE by Helen McCloy (EQMM, July 1946; reprinted in at least 10 other anthologies); a beautiful Russian woman's death is closely linked to a priceless ancient Chinese painting.—10. "The Notorious Snowman" by James Powell (EQMM, Oct. 7, 1981); series detective Ambrose Ganelon goes to St. Petersburg, Russia, in 1875 and solves who is behind a series of jewel robberies.—11. "A Statesman's Touch" by Robert Barnard (EQMM, Mar. 1993); during the Paris Peace Conference of 1919, the murder of a man in a shabby hotel is "dealt with" by the British Prime Minister so that a minimum of the hotel's guests find their "affairs" disrupted.—12. SMILING JOE AND THE TWINS by Florence V. Mayberry (EQMM, Sep. 1993); feuding between gambling casino associates in Reno, Nevada, leads to the murder of one.—13. "The Problem of the Leather Man" by Edward D. Hoch (EQMM, Dec. 1992); series detective Dr. Sam Hawthorne solves who killed a man who was slightly injured in a minor car accident.Editor Janet Hutchings provides this book with a very good 3-page Introduction as well as brief Head-notes for each story (and a brief End-note to Robert Barnard's "A Statesman's Touch").In my judgment—valuing kindness, compassion, ingenuity, and intelligence—the 4 best stories in this anthology are Ellis Peters' A LIGHT ON THE ROAD TO WOODSTOCK, Miriam Grace Monfredo's "Gather Not Thy Rose," Helen McCloy's CHINOISERIE, and Steven Saylor's THE LEMURES. Respectively, they received from me letter grades of "A", "A-", "A-", and "B+".The 4 weakest stories in my view are George Baxt's "The William Shakespeare Murder Case," James Powell's "The Notorious Snowman," Robert Barnard's "The Statesman's Touch," and Florence V. Mayberry's SMILING JOE AND THE TWINS. Baxt's story received a "C-" grade from me because of its huge number of preposterous bad jokes, while the other 3 works all earned "D-" grades from me for other faults. Powell's story is flawed by the fact that LUCK alone—luck, luck, and yet MORE luck—provides Ganelon with the solution to a case that stumbles across HIM as he is leaving Russia. Barnard's story attempts, in a very heavy-handed and sophomorically smutty way, to satirize Woodrow Wilson, David Lloyd-George, and other world leaders. And—SPOILER ALERT—Florence V. Mayberry's novelette is a very unsatisfying and unpleasant story of EVIL TRIUMPHANT in which a relatively decent man is murdered and his killer's life is somewhat "blighted" by the total destruction of his favorite daughter's future happiness and his loss of her love; ALSO the narrative point of view in this story goes off the rails in its final 2 pages.With the exception of Lillian de la Tour's "The Earl's Nightingale," which received a "C" grade from me, the remaining stories of this anthology all received either a "B" or "B-" grade from me.
