Judith C. Brown's "Immodest Acts" rises above its salacious title.  It is the small history of Benedetta Carlini, a nun in a Tuscan convent in the early 1600s who rises to the role of abbess on the strength of her visions and mystical ecstasies, which are then discredited by Church authorities, leading to her spending the rest of her life in prison.  While these officials are conducting their inquiries, they uncover Benedetta's affair with another nun, which only adds to her troubles.  Brown does an admirable job sifting through meagre historical records to examine laws against homosexuality in Early Modern Europe and the the societal role of women, including within convents, which housed an admixture of "discarded patrician daughters" and women driven by a truly religious vocation. Benedetta was undoubtedly the latter and readers are left to guess at the motives behind her fantastical religious visions, perhaps part self-induced ecstasies, part cunning manipulations and part wish fulfillment.
