First of all, this review contains spoilers. Fair warning.This is one that's hard. The entire book is dated by it's racial aspects that is at the heart of the story. It's gotten a lot of flak for being "racist", but I don't think it is really. For the record, I am white. The book takes an (anything but stereotypical) family of the 1950's, with a black "houseboy", on an adventure through time to a future where the roles are reversed and whites are slaves to blacks. The one thing that stands out and may be the reason it's gotten so much flak is that the blacks actually eat their white slaves, and some are bred for that very purpose. I understand that this sort of thing has happened many times in history, so is not unprecedented. It is questionable, perhaps, that Heinlein actually "needed" to use this device to make his point, which is slavery is (very simply, arguably too simply) bad no matter what. It drove the point home, but also made the black slave-masters in the story far worse, in a very primal way, than the white slave-masters of more modern times. So while the book is an adventure story with the context of slavery, and ostensibly was written as and intended as a denouncement of slavery, it comes across as yet another denouncement of blacks, that they would be this horrific. Heinlein was a champion of racial and sexual equality, but he was also a product of his time. And frankly -- and as far as I know, I have read every published word of Mr. Heinlein -- his take on sexual "equality" was to put the female in charge... and his arguments for that are actually fairly compelling and rational. But to do that, he also goes to extremes in several ways. Perhaps, and we will never know at this point, at the time he felt he must be extreme to make his points. Both in gender and in racial equality. Perhaps, and again we will never really know, he didn't realize that he didn't need to make his slave-masters quite so nasty and was, in fact, hurting his stated cause by going to an "extreme beyond extreme". To be fair, the idea of cannibalism is something Heinlein addressed several times in his works. I won't presume to say I understand his thoughts on it; he has presented it as evil, an occasional necessary evil, and, as in this book, a simple social convention. And I will state I have done no particular research for this review... I strongly suspect there are better ones out there, and urge you to seek them out. My own personal take, which may be without value, is... he did it for, essentially, the shock value. He had written these black slave-masters as, quite honestly, "too nice". He had to, to have the story he wanted to tell; he had to give his main characters a chance, and for that he needed their slave-masters to be "nicer" in many ways that traditional slave-masters were not. So he then had to turn them into "monsters" at almost the last second -- the cannibalism is only hinted at very very vaguely until the climax and resolution -- so they were truly evil in the end. In that, this may be the "sloppiest" writing by a master wordsmith who abhorred sloppy writing in his career. Technically it isn't a deus ex machina... but in a literary way it's pretty darn close. To the detriment of the overall message he was trying to give. It's an exciting story, with many thought-provoking ideas and strong characters. It does have it's flaws.
