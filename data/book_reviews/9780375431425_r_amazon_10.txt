Jubilo is born with a smile on his face and an immense zest for life and for love.  His grandmother, a Mayan Indian, teaches him her language and, although only a small boy, Jubilo is able to translate his grandmother's words into Spanish, so that his mother can understand them.  However, Jubilo does not just have a gift for the spoken language, he has also been gifted with being able to hear what is in people's hearts, so he does not always translate literally; he adds a kind word here and there or changes the tone, in order that the 'true' message - as he sees it - will be given and will be received more favourably. As Jubilo grows older, he puts his talent to good use in his job as a telegraph operator in Mexico in the 1930s, sometimes changing messages slightly in his desire for everyone to be as happy as he is.  And Jubilo is a happy man; he is married to the beautiful Lucha whom he worships and adores, making love to her at every available opportunity and working long hours to be able to provide for her and for his children.  Jubilo almost feels he is in Paradise, but it is a Paradise that does not last, for a tragic event befalls him and Lucha which causes an almost irreparable rift between them.  It takes many years and the intervention of Jubilo's daughter, Lluvia, before Lucha and Jubilo can try to repair the damage caused by past events. (Please note that I am not giving away too much information here, the reader knows from the first few pages that Jubilo and Lucha have been estranged for years).I was keen to start this novel from the author of the bestselling: 'Like Water for Chocolate' (which I have not read, but which has been recommended to me) and enjoyed the first few chapters where the reader learns a little about the Mayan Indians and their close connection to the sun and the universe. However, if I am entirely honest, I have to say that I found the writing a little too cloying and, at times, a little moralistic for my taste. I also found certain aspects of the story repetitive, especially when reading over and over again about how much Jubilo passionately loved his wife; how he wanted to be in bed with her all the time, stroking and sucking and licking her (and lots more besides). To be fair, I am sure the author intended a wider message with this novel - about how unhappiness can be avoided if lines of communication are kept open - but I am afraid I just found the language and the content too sentimental. I also felt the tone was a little sermonizing at times - it was surprising to read suddenly in the last few pages where Lluvia is in the middle of mourning the loss of a loved one, that telegraph operators played an important and essential role in the history of telecommunications and how it makes her sad that few people remember this before they get on their computers, which seemed entirely out of place and could have been added as an afterword if the author felt strongly about this.
