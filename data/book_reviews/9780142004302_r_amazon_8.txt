Last Christmas I read Sansom's alternate history spy novel "Dominion" and enjoyed it hugely.  I decided to take a gamble on the Shardlake series. I wasn't disappointed in the first volume "Dissolution". Matthew Shardlake is an English lawyer at the time of Henry VIII. He comes from the rural middle classes (his father owns some land) and he is clever and sensible. He is close to Thomas Cromwell, the King's Minister who has recently morphed from a villain (in "A Man for All Seasons") to a hero (in Hillary Mantel's yet unnamed trilogy-in-progress), who often assigns him confidential work.  Though initially a reformer, Shardlake is losing his high hopes for Reformation: the violence and brutality required seem to him excessive to build a Christian commonwealth.  Shardlake is also a hunchback and at times is filled with self-loathing, although this makes him more sensitive to others in peripheral positions. In this novel Shardlake must travel with his idealistic sidekick to investigate the murder of a king's commissioner in Scarnsea monastery.  All Gothic tropes turn up: huge, decaying churches, marshes with quicksand, secret passages and oubliettes, lecherous (either sodomitic or otherwise) and covetous monks.  This makes the novel slightly stereotypical, particularly in the set-up of a series of murders in an isolated monastery, which is familiar to everyone from Eco's "Name of the Rose".  While some may have thought it risky for a new author to set himself up for comparisons with a master, Sansom rises to the occasion.  The story is a good one, the characters are attractive (although most of the monks are grotesques- I don't really believe things were as bad in the monasteries as Sansom makes them out), in particular Shardlake himself who is a deeply sympathetic character.  The action alternates with rumination and the historic setting is well achieved. As an English writer, Sansom is quite attuned to differences in social class and speech, and does a good job in showing how the emerging middle classes supported the King and Cromwell in the dissolution of the monasteries to appropriate the accumulated wealth of centuries, while also remarking on the immense human costs for the former monks and their dependents.Based on my liking of Dissolution I moved on to Dark Fire, which I liked even more, because Sansom seemed to have found his stride.  This is a highly amusing series, well written and likeable.Read moreRead less
