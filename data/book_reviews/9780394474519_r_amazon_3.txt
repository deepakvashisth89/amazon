In spite of the fact that Alvarez knew Sylvia Plath, that he attempted suicide himself, that he gives us a comprehensive history of attitudes about suicide among the Greeks, the Romans, the Inquisition, the Romantic Era, the Victorian era, the modern world of technology and weaponry, I don't think that he really puts his finger on the essential impulse that leads to suicide.The closest he comes, in my opinion, is when he cites Freud's death instinct. Freud believed that we all have a homicidal, concupiscent, completely self-centered and aggressive force within us (unconscious, of course), which boils under our civilized surface and is constantly trying to get out. The evidence of this is obvious: the use of technology as a means of mass murder in the modern world. The ubiquity of violence as a condition of modern life. This could be thought of as a gigantic attempt at suicide by the entire human race.When the ego strength of the individual, due to outside stress, a change in life circumstances or, as Freud brilliantly suggests, a tipping of the inner forces of the mind at a particular time of life, suicide suddenly seems to be the only logical course of action.Freud also says that the Ego could not possibly kill itself unless the aggression is originally pointed outside, toward someone else, and then is turned back upon the self. This is borne out by the fact that the families of suicides feel profoundly wounded by the loss. The aggressive intent of the suicide is felt by family and loved ones.Alvarez sees this, but does not draw the proper conclusions. He doesn't see the hidden link between suicide and the constitution of the individual. His final attitude is puzzlement, bewilderment, like every other social commentator who has written about suicide.So, Alvarez looks at suicide through a telescope, not a microscope.
