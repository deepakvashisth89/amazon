At the age of 14 in 1965 I came across this book in my local library and was instantly captivated. The narrative challenged me to confront the confusions and divided loyalties of a civil war; the biggest threat to the hero, Jeff, was often his scheming captain, some of his strongest relationships were with the apparent foe,that war was often dirty and deaths were not always instant and caused by a single convenient bullet. In those far off days works that treated war in such a way were rare for the average adolescent reader. From that time on I would bemuse friends and relatives about this book, which I eventually consigned to history, assuming it out of print.Then last year through the wonders of on-line bookstores I found to my joy that Rifles for Watie was still in print, naturally I babbled this to my patient spouse and much to my delight I was given a copy for my 53rd birthday.What a joy to read again and discover that this book has lost none of its magic and to have the impressions of long ago reawakened. The tale of a youth's coming of age in a turbulent time while still retaining his own convictions and morality is an enthralling as ever. Now I am able to appreciate even more the minor characters that move in and out of the narrative with a rationale that does not jar while enriching the tale with their own stories.I can forgive and overlook the minor military inconsistencies if only because the author set the work in an often overlooked theatre of the American Civil War, and because many of the events are based upon the experiences of survivors that he interviewed in the 1940s.I recommend this to readers of all ages both American and British, even if you have had no previous interest in the American Civil War, this is simply a very good story.
