This ambitious retelling of Arthurian romance relies on the Celtic rather than English or French legends (think Gawain rather than Lancelot, freedom-fighting rather than pageantry). Arthur and his foster-brother, with a band of faithful comrades, struggle to bring peace to a war-torn Keltia at great personal cost. For me, it captures the force and pathos that all great Arthurian tales have, and its heroes are complex and imperfect enough to love. The relationship between the two men, which you see from their childhood fostering, has the same depth and authenticity as that between Frodo and Sam (in Tolkien's books, not the movies), and it is the foundation of all that comes after.The stories are set in an imagined Keltic world on planets far from Earth. When I first saw this, I thought it was a gimmick. In Kenneally-Morrison's hands, though, the new setting works. For one thing, the world doesn't get in the way of the story or its characters--and as the tales progress, the multi-planet setting becomes crucial to the final crises. The books' real strength is not in the fantasy-world genre elements but in its characters and their complex, utterly believable loves and fears. The novels also feature plot twists that satisfy--you feel rewarded rather than tricked--and unexpected but authentic reworkings of the original legendary material.The author really did her homework on Keltic culture and language, at least as far as I can tell. Names and practices bear a distinctively Celtic flavor (some are so tough to pronounce that there's a guide to help you). However, the books are not trapped within some sort of Irish-itis; Kelita has existed long enough in a new place to have evolved its own distinctive culture. If you enjoy Tolkien's linguistic flare, you will enjoy something similar in Kenneally-Morrison.I have read this trilogy several times, which I can say of only ten or twelve books of any type, and have given or loaned them to friends and students (especially those who dabble in writing fantasy).
