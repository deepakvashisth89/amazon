This book is 19 chapters, written by different scholars, describing the history and theology of Christianity from its origins in the 1st Century CE to the late 20th Century CE.Christianity emerged in the first half of the 1st Century AD among the Jews of Judea, then quickly spread into Gentile communities. By the middle of the 4th Century AD, Christianity had become the state religion of Rome. Esoteric theological debates flowed throughout the early Christian community, as Christians tried to decide exactly who Jesus was and what Jesus's relationship was to God. By the middle of the 5th Century AD, Christian leaders had developed the basics of orthodox theology-- there was one God, made up of three persons (the Father, the Son [Jesus], and the Holy Spirit), and Jesus was both fully human and fully divine. The Church of the East (based in modern-day Iraq, Iran, and India) thought this formulation deemphasized Jesus's human nature and split off, eventually becoming a tiny sect as Islam became the dominant force in the region. Meanwhile, the Oriental Orthodox Church (based in modern-day Armenia, Egypt, and Ethiopia) thought this formulation deemphasized Jesus's divine nature and split off.From the 5th Century AD through the 12th Century, Christianity spread throughout Europe, eventually becoming the dominant and almost sole religion of the continent. Christianity formed the basis of European social and political life, but European Christianity split in the 11th Century. Catholics argued that the Holy Spirit came from the Father and the Son, but Orthodox Christians thought this made the Holy Spirit a subordinate entity in the Trinity and argued that the Holy Spirit came from the Father. And more concretely, Orthodox Christians felt that the Bishop of Rome was first among equals, and the other major bishops (of Constantinople, Antioch, Alexandria, and Jerusalem) also deserved equal standing. Catholics disagreed, and the churches split in the 11th Century, with Orthodox Christianity dominating in Eastern Europe and Catholicism dominating in Western and Central Europe.But the Catholics eventually faced their own schism, with Protestants breaking off in the 16th Century. Protestant Christianity itself splintered into innumerable denominations. Further, the Scientific Revolution and the Enlightenment weakened Christianity's hold on the European intellect. Secularization, tolerance, liberalism, and socialism all served to weaken Christianity, and by the 20th Century Europe had moved far from its Christian roots.Yet European imperialism ensured that Christianity would become a global religion.In the nearly empty lands of North America, European settlers established an array of denominations.In Latin America, the Spanish and Portuguese Empires had established a powerful role for the Catholic Church, but Latin distance from Rome, nationalism, liberalism, and Marxism led to unresolved tension between church and state, with the precise contours of the relationship in constant flux.In Africa, missionaries spread Christianity throughout sub-Saharan Africa, and Africa has become a major center of Christianity.In Asia, Christianity struggled to gain a foothold in a land home to already well-established faiths: Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Shinto, Confucianism, Taoism. But in some regions-- namely Korea-- Christianity became a vehicle for anti-imperial resistance (specifically, resistance to the Shinto Empire of Japan) and Christianity developed a widespread following. Christianity also gained a deep foothold in the Spanish colony of the Philippines.The weakest chapters are the Reformation (the author is a poor writer) and the concluding chapters, which examine the future of Christianity and are outdated now that the book is 30 years old.
