I enjoyed every minute I read this book.  The main crux of the book is that in the 19th century, up to the Civil War, the Senate was the great hall of the republic.  That our nation's great statesmen from the immediate founding, to the Civil War all came from the Senate.  The opening passages entitled "The Desks of the Senate" encapsulates this.  Caro shows that our political heritage as a nation once held men like Clay, Webster, and Calhoun as giants in the running of the nation.  From the Civil War until the 1950's the Senate was a body of obstruction instead of action.  Instead of thoughtful compromise, the compromise tended to water down all liberal change, indeed all change was bottled up, especially in the waning of the power of the Radical Republicans after the Reconstruction period.The South used the Senate to stop all attempts to change their way of life.  That is until Lyndon Johnson came along.  He used the South to change the Senate.  Johnson had the attaining of power as his chief goal.  Caro argues that that power was intended to be used for the good of our society, but there are questions regarding that.  Johnson was a difficult figure to read.  He lied when it suited him.  He used his work ethic and his ability to understand what moved people to gain power for the office of majority leader.  He frustrated liberals for years, but got a Civil Rights Bill passed in 1957, despite the arguments by conservatives in the Democratic Party.The passages on Richard B. Russell are enlightening.  That Lyndon Johnson intuitively knew that he was the power of the body, and became cozy with his is an explanation of his unbridled understanding of power.  Lyndon Johnson used the southern Democrats against their own interests, but in favor of his own power.  I understand the titanic struggle for Civil Rights was important, but one must also remember the level of ends-justifies-the-means actions that Johnson had undertaken to achieve this largely meaningless legislation.  Johnson ruled the Senate, because Johnson knew more.  Johnson proved that knowledge is power, and lying to a colleague will not be found out by the ignorant.  He knew the Senators as well.  The picture section of the hardback edition is really and truly worthwhile to see the Johnson treatment being applied to members.Flagrant uses of power is something that Americans tend to deplore.  Lyndon Johnson is rightly called to task for the odious treatment of Leland Olds, but this consummate politician, a man who looked to the end result instead of recognizing any type of moral boundaries, is the man who got a lot done in a short amount of time.  For liberals out there that think all the left is sweetness and light, read the Olds chapter and know what your side is capable of doing.  For the conservative, one needs to recognize the power and its uses, but are those uses legitimate?  Was the reign of people like Lyndon Johnson, with his lying and manipulation such that it began the politics of distrust in the 1950's?  If ends justifies the means is the norm, then the left cannot argue that those tactics are impermissible to the right.This is an important book to understand our political system.  It is also a fine character study on one of the singular most important lives in 20th Century America.  If you are a student of political science, I highly recommend this book.
