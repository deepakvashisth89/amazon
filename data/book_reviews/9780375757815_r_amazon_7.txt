“Be honest and poor, by all means — but I shall not envy you; I do not much think I shall even respect you. I have a much greater respect for those that are honest and rich.” (loc 3070)The first half of this iconic novel fascinated me. It examines the forces that shape the person we become – the early experiences, hardships, and setbacks that form our character and worldview, and the cumulative effect of seemingly small matters. It does so through a story of a well-to-do family that voluntarily and enthusiastically adopts a niece from a decidedly less privileged home. The surrogate caregivers are largely well-intentioned, but they are painfully blind, sometimes willfully so. Their generosity has limits – “come this far but no further”. They insist on maintaining distance and distinction: we love you, but you are not one of us. Austen tells the story with penetrating insight and dry humor. She employs extended set pieces (especially a brilliant, convoluted, multi-chapter saga of the preparation for a family theatrical production) that serve to highlight relationships, character flaws, conflicts, and changing allegiances.The second half of the novel frustrated me a bit: I expected a nuanced account of the niece’s adult life, but I encountered instead a series of lengthy slogs involving little more than romantic machinations: genteel triangles; heavy-handed flirting; jealousies; marriage intentions, offers, acceptances, refusals, and consequences. But Austen eventually comes through: one extended storyline thoughtfully contrasts life in a chaotic, lower-class household with that in a calmer but overly structured, privileged one. More importantly, a series of late-breaking surprises opens the story to a somewhat larger agenda. Austen meets the moment: a subtle, easy-to-miss twist on the opening page of the final chapter (tantalizing and not entirely explained; fulfilling an isolated throwaway line earlier in the book) sets the tone for a fitting if not entirely satisfying finishing stretch.Early nineteenth century prose can be hard on modern readers, and Austen is sometimes opaque even by the norms of the day. I often had trouble keeping the characters straight, and I occasionally struggled to figure out who was speaking. I especially found difficult the challenge of keeping track of who was romantically attracted to whom.
