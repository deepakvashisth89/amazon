Please note: there are already some excellent, thoughtful, reviews of this book, but I feel obliged to put in my five-cents-worth.I am delighted to have come across this book.  As a Kerouac nerd, I found it really interesting because it is the only account that I have read that attempts to give a real sense of how Kerouac actually spoke and interacted with others, reproducing, as much as the author can remember, conversations and specific meetings.  This manages to give an immediacy and authenticity that is difficult to appreciate in reading the biographies.It is worth reading also because David Amran first got to know Kerouac in 1956 before the avalanche of notoriety that descended on Jack with the publication of “On the Road” in 1957.  Amran was already well established in the jazz world, knowing Dizzy Gillespie, Charlie Mingus, Thelonious Monk among others, and could easily sense the common ground that linked Kerouac’s writing and approach to life with the spontaneity and improvisation of jazz.  He used to regularly provide musical interplay with Jack as he scat sang and improvised his spontaneous prose and stories at bars and parties, and as a musician Amran can vouch for the creative skills that he witnessed.At the time of their first meeting in 1956  Amran was twenty five and Kerouac thirty four, and they remained friends and in regular contact, if only by phone in the last years, until Jack’s death in 1969.  Clearly Jack’s spontaneity, compassion, ability to listen to others, and whole approach to life had a significant effect on the younger Amran, and in this book he attempts to correct what he sees as the disservice done to Jack by a number of biographers, while emphasising the coherence and essential validity of Jack’s whole approach to life.  This determination to set the record straight make this an important book to read for anyone seriously interested in Kerouac.  The book is just over 300 pages long and the first 174 pages concern Amram’s own memories of his contact with Jack and his circle of friends, for instance Amram was present at the filming of “Pull My Daisy” and goes into detail about the whole process, describing impromptu behaviour by Corso, Ginsberg, and Orlovsky (behaviour that I’d probably have found hilarious in my twenties, but seems distinctly tedious now I’m in my sixties: still, you had to be there.).  The remainder of the book describes his contacts with others after Jack’s death and his attendance/ participation in various events commemorating Jack.  In addition to getting a better perspective on Jack, I was also pleased to read about Amran’s contact with others, such as Carolyn Cassady and Gregory Corso.A brief extract:“There’s been a lot of grim judgmental scowls and negativity when people write about us.  But whatever our shortcomings, we were all, in our own way, purveyors of joy, energy, hope and sharing.  We didn’t whine and put down others, but searched for moments of beauty.  We dug every precious moment of life.”All in all: essential reading.Read moreRead less
