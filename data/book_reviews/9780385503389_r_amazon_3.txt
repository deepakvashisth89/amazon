I read a lot of books about World War II, including in German. I had several times read German-language accounts by survivors of the Holocaust in southern Germany and Austria, as well as by German civilians who were children at the time, that they had been liberated by black soldiers. I had never forgotten one particular account in which concentration camp survivors, who had fled a train of box cars from Poland to the south when the Nazi guards abandoned it, suddenly saw tanks coming and thought they would all be killed. Instead, the turret opened and a black American’s head came up, and they knew they were safe. People in Dachau and Munich also described seeing the arrival of black American soldiers as the moment of knowing that they were no longer in danger.Americans I mention this too, especially other WW II buffs, were usually skeptical. Surely we’d have heard about this in English-language books too (this was back in the 1980s).Someone recently told me about this book, which I was fascinated to read. It confirms those German stories and brings back into the light these unusually brave and dedicated men. Besides the danger from the enemy, they also faced contempt and mistreatment at the hands of their own countrymen, from the time they first trained in segregated southern Louisiana till the end of the war and afterwards. Letters commending them were “lost,” and honors due them were only conferred long afterwards, when most of them had died.The 761st was trained to operate tanks but was never actually expected to go into combat. Despite their outstanding record in training, the units were held back in the States while white units that arrived later were shipped overseas to fight. Many white officers of the time believed black troops were stupid and cowardly, and some even called the 761st “Mrs. Roosevelt’s n*****s” because it was widely believed that the only reason for their training was her demand for black troops to be treated equally—until then, most black men in the military had been limited to service as cooks, servants, quartermasters and other support roles. Prejudiced whites in towns near the army camps caused constant problems for the black soldiers (the book contains several shocking stories of this kind, including one about Jackie Robinson). But because of the grave need for good tank units, the 761st wound up being sent into combat as pioneers in General Patton’s army.This account of the remarkable men of the tank battalion begins with Kareem Abdul-Jabbar’s childhood when he got to know a New York City cop who was a friend of his father’s. Much later, as an adult, he comes to realize that Leonard Smith was an authentic war hero who had not talked about his experiences because they were so shattering. As he  learned more about his friend’s history and that of the forgotten men of the 761st (neglected in part because unlike today’s tank battalions, they were constantly being switched around to different army units so that their story was not told as a whole), he decided that their story should be told, and that resulted in this very readable book.Because of this wish to document, there is a lot of discussion of which armies, divisions, battalions etc. were in combat near which specific villages and towns. However I never found that this distracted from the essential story, which revolves around Leonard Smith and his friends and the action they saw—more than most US soldiers in World War II.In short, a good read but also an important book to remind us that those times are not so long ago when brave and highly capable members of the U.S. military were treated as inferior solely because of the color of their skin.Read moreRead less
