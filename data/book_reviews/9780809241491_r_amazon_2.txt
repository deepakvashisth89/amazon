(This review is based on the Contemporary Books edition of 1988.)Janet Levine was born in South Africa in December, 1945. From her youth her white liberal activism made her part of a distinct and isolated minority. Her anti-apartheid  positions were public and forceful, but she and her family left South Africa in '84 when she came to the conclusion that white liberalism was impotent in the face of entrenched racism.When writing this book in '88, she believed that white rule would soon be replaced by violent black nationalist domination. She recalled the words of a black trade unionist who said of the white government: "It is a dog that knows it is going to lose the fight in the end, so it fights like a mad dog." She could only conclude that "the future reality would not be my humanitarian dream, but the nightmare of racial war in South Africa."Fortunately for all concerned, a markedly different future was in store. In 1989, many African National Congress (ANC) activists were freed from detention. Then, in a stunning development, the ANC itself was unbanned and Nelson Mandela was released in '90. After a new interim constitution was enacted in '93, Mandela was elected president in '94. While the demise of apartheid was certainly not without bloodshed and disturbance, on the whole the country and its population (black, white, colored, Indian) got off fairly lightly--somehow.The failure of the author's predictive powers does not devalue this book. What she did (resourceful and courageous) and what she saw and experienced (gut-wrenching and arguably genocidal) constitute an important insider account of one of colonialism's most shameful, horrendous, and degrading misadventures.Read moreRead less
