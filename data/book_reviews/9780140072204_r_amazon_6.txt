Perhaps cartoon humor has become more literal and down to earth and less anarchic, surrealistic and nonsensical over the past few decades? Or perhaps it was always straight-laced and those who played with and morphed reality were always on the fringe? How else to explain the now very obscure reputation of cartoonist B.Kliban? His drawings and the concepts behind them were nearly always profound, mind-bending and sometimes hilariously stupid and juvenile. Maybe such humor is doomed to remain unpopular, as it often takes a mental leap to appreciate. Not that people have become dimmer, but maybe the expectations of comics, or perhaps general humor, now calls for an "instant laugh." One should "get it" on the first try and turn the page to the next instantly digestible gag. And once the laughs cease, the reading ceases. Kliban's best works at a different level than this, though many of his cartoons do elicit immediate laughter. Like the kid who doesn't get the dirty joke for six hours and suddenly bursts out laughing in an inappropriate place, some things require time to digest or brew in the mind until a eureka moment arrives. But of course some types of humor will just not appeal to certain types of people. Kliban remains the ultimate in acquired taste. Not to mention he mainly wrote for adults. The vast majority of his work is not for children and deals with what we now know ominously as "adult themes." Cartoons, and particularly gag cartoons, today seem mostly geared towards children, or they at least imbue the mentality of children or the worst that sit-coms have to offer. Obviously exceptions exist and humor at the child level definitely has its place, but the adult themes now seem largely phased out. Maybe because of this, though Kliban passed away in 1990, his work still has a unique freshness today."The Biggest Tongue in Tunsia and Other Drawings," from 1986, was the final Kliban collection published before his death. It contains some of Kliban's best, stupidest and funniest adult work. No newspaper would have published most of this stuff, but Kliban often sold his work to Playboy. A few classics appear here, most notably the one with a monk or a nondescript ascetic saying "It only hurts when I exist" while sitting on a bed of nails. Elsewhere, two turkeys talk, one of them sits in front of a typewriter and the standing turkey says "I know we're stupid, but is that any reason to give up on your novel?" They just keep coming: "Anachronism person"; "Mime Chow"; "Turkish Vibrating Soup"; "Espanish por Turistas"; "Great weather for ducks out there"; "Height Watchers"; "An Angel of the Lord Smites A Careless Jaywalker"; "Clouds Turning into Iron and Falling on Everything"; a toaster from God; "The only show in town"; "People without television watching liver sliding down a wall"; "Jesus changes water into urine"; "The bookburning festival was called off because nobody had any." Many others remain ineffable or simply unmentionable due to those devious innocence-killing "adult themes." "Humor of the World: The Balkans" in which a man receives pills that the doctor tells him "will cure you but will also make you very ugly" might not sink in right away. It represents the height of absurdity for this volume, which is quite a claim.Those who enjoy Kliban's not quite so esoteric work will probably find this collection one of his strongest. It's not quite as "out there" as some of his earlier works, though it's plenty "out there." Of course, like most of his work, it remains sadly out of print, though the online marketplace has rendered that once despised phrase almost impotent. In other words, anyone can find a copy online. Hopefully someone will lead an e-revival of his work, as his influence upon almost all subsequent single panel comics has become more obvious. Gary Larson, Dan Piraro and others definitely drew on Kliban's humor and style for their considerably more popular, and far more toned down, work. Perhaps the internet, with all its glory and horror, will see a revival of Kliban-speak, at least in some tiny corner where few dare to type?
