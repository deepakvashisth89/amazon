As a nine year old at the time of the Kennedy assassination I have never thought to question why that singular, awful eventshould have left such a lasting impression on my generation.Now, fifty years after the fact, I have to wonder how anyone who wasn't there could possibly understand why we, Baby Boomersand Greatest Generation alike, can never seem to move past that moment. Why every one of us remembers where we were,what it felt like and how the world changed over that long weekend of grief.Having just reread William Manchester's The Death of a President  I am painfully reminded of why that is.Most works written within close proximity to the  events they describe suffer from the passions of the moment and are oftensubject to extensive revision as other perspectives and new sources of information become available. It is a testament toWilliam Manchester's reportorial roots and his historian's rigor that so little of the  factual basis of this work has ever been challenged.Told from the inside of the event, from the days leading up to the trip to Dallas to the period immediately after JFK's intermentat Arlington, Manchester's exhaustive investigation tames a potentially overwhelming mass of facts and testimony into a veryhuman narrative.Rereading it, I was amazed at how much I was transported back to that time. Little details like the constant smoking as well as reminders of the ever present threat of the cold war and how it became a pretext for the most outlandish beliefs and behaviors, particularly by the Far Right of the period.I was equally amazed at the vivid narrative flow. Don't let the length fool you. Once begun, this is a very hard book to put down.I recommend this book to my graying cohort to read, or reread, that they might revisit and remind themselves of what was andhow losing it changed us so much. But I particularly recommend it to those who are inheriting the sum of my generationssuccesses and failures. This book says a lot about how we came to be the generation we are.Read moreRead less
