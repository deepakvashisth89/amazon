The subtitle of "The Underdogs" could be:  The more things change, the more they stay the same.  This novel of the Mexican Revolution (1910-1920) shows the tragedy of power struggles, vacuums and abuse in politics.  When the simple mountain peasants of central Mexico revolt against the tyranny of the abusive & corrupt Federal government, Azuela shows how they quickly become even more tyrannical, murderous and destructive than the dictator they are opposing.  "We have met the enemy and it is us" comes to mind, as does Lord Acton's famous quote:  "Power corrupts, absolute power corrupts absolutely."  Azuela wrote this novel in 1915, at the height of the revolution when rebel movements turned upon each other like rabid dogs -- underdogs that have gone from abused to abuser.  Azuela was a physician who at first was loyal to the revolution but soon realized that his compadres were actually worse than the power they were opposing.  The novel went unnoticed until it was published in 1925 by a gringo publisher in Texas.  Azuela's prose is simple, but with great impact, to coincide with the simplicity of his characters.
