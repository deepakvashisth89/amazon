Perhaps one of the better example of colonialist literature, "King Solomon's Mines" is fascinating less for its story than its characters and the colonialist beliefs they openly share with us. The protagonist, Allan Quatermain, belongs to the "Robinson Crusoe" line of literary heroes, which is to say that they would undoubtedly be villains were they to be written about today. Crusoe, of course, was a slaver washed ashore after being shipwrecked, and Quatermain is a poacher - and proud of it. Indeed, one of the book's most trying sequences comes early on when Quatermain and his associates decide to halt for a day or two in their quest to reach King Solomon's legendary diamond mines purely in order to go elephant hunting. The trio of British colonialists, armed with rifles, pursue a pack of elephants and end up trapping them against a hill, butchering several members of the pack. A few, we're told, "unfortunately" escape. As for the rest, they are gunned down and their great ivory tusks cut off. The carcasses are then left to rot. My greatest disappointment was that the elephants didn't manage to turn back and successfully stampede these savage butchers.So yeah... that made our trio of heroes rather hard to like, right from the get go. They come close to death numerous times later on but, remembering the elephants, I found it rather hard to care. I do believe it important however, not to judge past characters based on the moral standards of today, as this was indeed the mindset - however wrong though it was - that dominated in 19th century England.That is by far not the only area sure to offend modern sensibilities.Much later on, after a kind native woman has saved the men - at the cost of her own life - Quatermain remarks on how her death is actually a good thing, as Good, a member of his party (and race) was somewhat smitten with her."I am bound to say, looking at the thing from the point of view of an oldish man of the world, that I consider her removal was a fortunate occurrence, since, otherwise, complications would have been sure to ensue. The poor creature was no ordinary native girl, but a person of great, I had almost said stately, beauty, and of considerable refinement of mind. But no amount of beauty or refinement could have made an entanglement between Good and herself a desirable occurrence; for, as she herself put it, "Can the sun mate with the darkness, or the white with the black?"'Ok, you get it. Colonialist ideas and values = bad! But, as I mentioned, it's important not to let this ruin the book for you. There I'm many books written pre-20th century that won't offer at least one offensive paragraph. And "King Solomon's Mines" is valuable and enlightening for this very reason - to see how far we've come... or at least, most of us.As I mentioned, the story itself is rather unremarkable when read today, but it appears to have left quite a mark. Many films and subsequent books take from "King Solomon's Mines", the first adventure novel of its kind. The most famous of these is no doubt Indiana Jones, and Steven Spielberg has spoken of the influence Quartermain and co. had on the crafting of the legendary Dr. Jones.There are some wonderful bits of writing here as well. Take this for example:"As those who read this history will probably long ago have gathered, I am, to be honest, a bit of a coward, and certainly in no way given to fighting, though somehow it has often been my lot to get into unpleasant positions, and to be obliged to shed man's blood. But I have always hated it, and kept my own blood as undiminished in quantity as possible, sometimes by a judicious use of my heels. At this moment, however, for the first time in my life, I felt my bosom burn with martial ardour. Warlike fragments from the "Ingoldsby Legends," together with numbers of sanguinary verses in the Old Testament, sprang up in my brain like mushrooms in the dark; my blood, which hitherto had been half-frozen with horror, went beating through my veins, and there came upon me a savage desire to kill and spare not."A "judicious use of my heels"? Verses from the Old Testament springing up in the brain like "mushrooms in the dark"?
