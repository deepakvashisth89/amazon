William Dean Howell's tenth novel chronicles the life--family, business, and social--of Silas Lapham, formerly a colonel in the Civil War. Readers learn much expository information about his Rise to wealth and business success in the first chapter, in which a highhanded Boston reporter embellishes the protagonist's blunt and boastful account of his reinvented lifestyle. Set in the late 19th century this deeply psychological novel presents the enigma of a moral business man struggling to navigate the social climate of two disparate realms: one of aristocratic Boston (where true gentlemen DO nothing but enjoy their inherited wealth) and the maze of capitalistic Immorality, (where rogues of all kinds are alert to pluck naive investors.)  Can a simple, plain spoken man from the Vermont backwoods survive such metropolitan snares?Owing their wealth to the enterprising development of a special mineral paint which his father had discovered on the family homestead, the Laphams have moved to the Back Bay area (admittedly not Beacon Hill) where they enjoy many luxuries while lacking the education and breeding to fit gracefully in with true Old Money.  Silas, Persis and their two daughters are not socially ambitious, yet both young ladies are instantly attracted to Tom Corey, whose mistaken preference for the wrong sister one causes soap operatic suspense.  Despite Lapham's insistence of  advertising his product in garish colors on local rocks, the family gains its own modest degree of respectability.  Priding himself on earning honest money (not speculating in stocks and wildcat patents) Lapham remains approachable, plain-spoken and a shrewd businessman. If only the sisters can sort out their romantic triangle.Finally bitten by the desire to promote his daughters in real society, the Laphams decide to build a lavish home in the Back Bay region and adorn it with an impressive library.  A big dinner party, given by the Coreys to introduce the Laphams as honored guests, proves the turning point of the novel; the country-bred family must consult etiquette books to know  how to dress and behave at table.  The evening proves a disaster for Lapham who, unaccustomed to wine, overindulges and speaks too openly and boastfully.  The next day at the office he pathetically asks Tom, his new employee and a shy suitor, if he was drunk last night.Behind the Laphams' ill advised social climbing lurks the ubiquitous character of Rogers, a somewhat Dickensian figure with his skulking reappearances, which cause marital strife between the Laphams and ultimately tempt Silas into shaky business schemes.One of the author's secondary themes is a warning against the evil effects of novels, at least on untutored female minds, where the current trend of heroines who are hell bent on self-sacrifice in the name of Love, may lead some earnest maidens astray, causing great needless grief to all parties. Indeed, the romantic triangle of the Lapham sisters (Penelope and Irene) over the attentions of Tom Corey provides a lighter though interesting subplot, offering literary breaks from the serious, long-term issues of financial and social ruin which eventually overwhelm the once unified family and result in the protagonist's inevitable, downward spiral.Silas' marriage is firm but circumstances once consigned to the Past threaten both domestic as well as fiscal stability.  He grapples for decades with personal issues unrelated to his paint business: How much gratitude does a man owe the family of the dead man who saved his life during battle? Was Lapham morally responsible for the fate of a man who once was his business partner--an assumption which Persis felt deeply, one which eroded their faith in each other's scruples and ultimately their marital trust.  Must a man's desperate attempt to right a questionable moral wrong be permitted to cloud his judgment and propel him to the brink of bankruptcy?  Why should a man who tries earnestly to behave in a moral manner be punished so severely? We gradually  realize that the title refers to more than Lapham's financial and social Rise; that his grasp of personal morality is also elevated--even as he suffers ignominy.
