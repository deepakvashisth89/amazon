One night in 1975, an up-and-coming FBI agent and an up-and-coming mobster met on a deserted beach just outside of Boston.  It wasn't their first meeting; the two had grown up in the same neighborhood, and the agent had been the childhood friend of the mobster's younger brother (now a prominent figure in state politics).  Now adults on opposite sides of the law, they formed a partnership that night: James "Whitey" Bulger would serve as an FBI informant, passing along information to Agent John Connolly that could help the federal authorities take down Boston's prominent Italian Mafia.  After all, Bulger wanted the rival gangsters out of the way as much as the FBI did, if not for the same reasons - and surely it couldn't hurt to have a friend in law enforcement.  It wasn't long, however, before Connolly fell entirely under the sway of the brash, charismatic criminal he had idolized as a child, and soon the flow of information was going both ways. . . .It sounds like the plot of a Dennis Lehane novel, but it's a true story, and Dick Lehr and Gerard O'Neill have untangled the knots of neighborhood loyalty, sifted the truth from the lies, and wallowed in ream after ream of legal documents to lay it bare in "Black Mass: The Irish Mob, the FBI, and a Devil's Deal."  In painstaking detail, supported by 35 pages of source notes, they show how this unusual partnership began, how the outgoing and persuasive Connolly drew a number of his FBI colleagues (and other members of law enforcement) into the conspiracy to various degrees, and how it all fell apart in the end.  Time and again, Connolly passed along to Bulger and his sidekick Stevie Flemmi information about an open investigation by another agency, a pending indictment, even another informant who might be about to rat on them - and time and again, Bulger and his crew managed to "outwit" the cops . . . and reason was found to remove their names from the list before the indictments were handed down . . . and "rats" conveniently disappeared before they got the chance to squeal.  Connolly's support of his informant/friend extended to active advocacy as well, exaggerating the importance of the information Bulger provided in order to convince his more scrupulous colleagues that letting the gangster slide under the radar now and then was serving a greater good.Boyhood ties, manly friendship, crime on the streets, corruption in high places, and an exploration of the vast and dangerous (yet necessary) moral gray area of the use of criminal informants in law enforcement: it's probably impossible to ruin a story this inherently interesting.  And in fact, it's hard to imagine a more precise and straightforward account of Connolly's "devil's deal" with Whitey Bulger than Lehr and O'Neill have served up here.  Unfortunately, the authors' excellence as journalists doesn't translate exactly to the kind of skill needed to compose a book-length narrative, and "Black Mass" has the sort of bloodless, matter-of-fact quality one tends to expect from a longform newspaper article, in which the purpose is to inform, perhaps to engage, but never to entertain.  Of course, one doesn't generally read nonfiction for entertainment alone, but there are a number of nonfiction writers - and the true-crime genre has more than its share - whose books are exhaustively researched and accurate with the facts, yet are at the same time well-told stories, a pleasure to read.  Lehr and O'Neill's style is rarely engaging, and there's a lot of repetition, as well as a combination of chronological and topical organization that can be mildly confusing.  Fortunately, the drama inherent in the facts themselves is enough to keep the pages turning, but I can't help wishing the authors had done their part to make them turn just a little bit faster.P.S.
