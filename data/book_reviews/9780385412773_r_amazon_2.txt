This is quite an eye-opening book.  I agree with one reviewer in that one thinks:  that could happen to me.  This is always a useful realization.  The stories of the two ladies---the main characters, I guess, are interesting.  But to me, the most interesting parts are the segments about how helping the homeless (and I suspect others) was a local undertaking.  People in communities got involved, and the results were much better.  Also, the story of the man who took over the XXXXXX house, who was himself homeless at one point, was interesting.  He believed in charging people for a night's room (a very minimal amount) so that the person did not feel like they were on welfare (the book states it more eloquently than me).  He also believed that these houses (night houses) should be non-judgmental.  Just provide the room and be supportive; do not criticize or condescend.  There were other really interesting parts about the history of homelessness and what people have tried to help these folks; e.g., how the New Deal actually *hurt* these shelters' ability to help the homeless.  There were a lot of interesting parts, but I finished it a month ago and cannot recall the details.  I do recall, however, that I frequently said to myself "wow," or "isn't that interesting" while reading the book.  The only reason I did not give it 5 stars is because the book rambles back and forth between the lives of the two ladies (another others), and the sociological history of the homeless, and I felt this disjointedness made the book more difficult to process than it should have been.  However, that may well be too picky.Another interesting aspect to this work is that the Atlantic--great magazine--had a story on the homeless, and mentioned some recent books.  Also, very interesting.  But what I found most interesting is that some of the most important insights from the article and the newer books that it was reviewing, were already in this this book.
