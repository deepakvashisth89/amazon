Nelson DeMille once again shows his versatility in the art of story-telling in this very, very good novel. Despite it's somber tone, DeMille's occasional light touches of humor enliven the story in ways that will be familiar to his followers. Believe me, this is one you won't want to put down, folks.Other reviewers have described the story line very well, so I have little to add about that. Suffice to say that it is engaging to the point of obsessive fascination, especially to those readers who lived through the Vietnam years - whether at home or in "The Land of Bad Things" - and who recall the sturm und drang of the My Lai massacre sensation that so dominated the news during that time.To me, the best parts of the story revolve not around the well developed characters, their personal issues, or even the oddities of the military justice system, but rather concern the inherent tensions built into the officers' code of the Army. An officer is required to control his men under any and all circumstances, even at the risk of his own life. But in a milieu where his men are exhausted, hardened to the realities of war as it really is, with morale virtually non-existent, how can an officer overcome outright mutiny, however brief?The answer, of course, is that he can't. He could die trying, but that would be a pointless sacrifice. The UCMJ doesn't specifically recognize being held hostage during a mutiny as an "extenuating or mitigating" circumstance, but covers itself by requiring that the officer report the mutiny - and all that occurred during it - after the fact. Even that is an  object of tension within the Code. Reporting such an incident raises the charge of "failure to control your men", which translates to absolute failure as an officer. So the officer may thereby escape a court martial, but he would be held in egregious contempt by others in the officer corps, to say nothing of his career being at an end. Added to that would be the element of disloyalty to his own men: men who were required to be loyal to him under the rules of military discipline.Many of those who were "there" understand how the natural barbarism of war was terribly exacerbated by the nature of the Vietnam War... a civil war into which we threw our young men in great numbers and with little to no understanding of what they were fighting for. In the view of many of those men - mostly teenagers in the later stages of the war - they were surrounded on all sides: by the NVA, by the Viet Cong guerillas who were merely cold if not friendly during the day but vicious enemies by night, and even by their own officers. Mr. DeMille makes it clear that the only thing which distinguished the hospital massacre from scores, perhaps hundreds, of similar breakdowns in discipline throughout the war was the raw number of civilian killings in one place at one time, and that some of the victims were Europeans.This is a somber and disturbing novel, but "the ring of truth" prevails throughout. We may not want to think about what our young men are expected to endure and what they might be reduced to doing under the extreme stresses of war, but Mr. DeMille requires that of us, like it or not.
