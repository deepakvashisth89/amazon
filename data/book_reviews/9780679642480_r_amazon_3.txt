Faulkner wrote three unquestioned great novels: As I Lay Dying, The Sound and The Fury, and Light in August, plus one that many consider great, Absalom, Absalom!.Light in August centers on a man named Joe Christmas who considers himself part black and thus in his mind, and that of his Southern society, tainted beyond redemption. That premise makes for a story full of agony, despair, and tragedy–needless tragedy to our modern sensibilities. The term for mixed race is a word invented in America in 1863: miscegenation. The timing is not a coincidence, being dead center during the American Civil War. The premise for the book seems impossible today, but history tells us that not only was miscegenation a term, it was against the law. Being of mixed race could also get you killed back then and up through the times of this novel. Indeed, the protagonist anti-hero of this book dies because he essentially goes mad with rage, murders a woman, and in return is murdered by his society.The structure of time in Light in August zigzags from the near past to the present, with a good deal of the distant past included as remembrances. It makes for difficult reading at times. A gothic tone hangs over the book like an evil fog. The story unfolds as a mystery, a tale hidden within the larger story. It takes time for the horrors to arrive, but arrive they do.It took me two cracks at the book to get past the first 50 pages, but it proved worth the time and effort. That said, I recommend this book with hesitation due to its twisted subject matter and mournful tone. Light in August is not as difficult as The Sound and The Fury, but it is challenging. I read an in-depth commentary on the book as I went along, Reading Faulkner: Light in August by Ruppersburg, which dramatically increased my understanding and my pleasure.
