Le Carre's novels are a rosetta stone for how a jaded cynic views the world. Tinker, Tailor, Soldier, Spy was a watershed event in how the public perceived spycraft, not as an exciting lark of dames and fisticuffs, but as a dreary slog. Gathering information, reams upon volumes of data, never knowing whether it will be enough - and if it is, will the answer even matter to the course of a nation?The Russia House goes further, beyond that story of a traitor in MI6, to a Russia undergoing change. The new openness of Russia seemed inspiring to most. But then, the US, UK, the former Soviet Union were always ruled by the men in grey. A scientist persuades a woman to smuggle out to the West a manuscript that tells the truth. What truth? The military ability of the Soviet knight, dying in his armor. This pure desire to end the lie that keeps the arms race going seems a no-brainer to any normal person with ethical moorings. So it goes that the Truth causes much vexation to the powers that be, who would rather not know.It feels plotless, as The Russia House is more concerned with character than machinations of standard stories. Barley Blair, an alcoholic publisher from Britain, has no heart for spycraft. He wants to publish the manuscript, which is out of the question as far as the grey men of MI6 and the CIA are concerned. So how do you do the right thing, in an utterly compromised position? Le Carre revisits this theme time and again, of men forced to compromise in the face of an utterly corrupt world.This novel excels at the difficult choices one faces in trying to push the world into a just direction, lacking the strength required to do so, one pushes nonetheless.Read moreRead less
