A Prayer for Owen Meany is a charming, funny, mostly entertaining--and yes, somewhat long--historical and mildly philosophical novel. I would argue that its length is not all that painful, however; and also that its length is justified. First, the narrative has a consistent and compelling rhythm that carried me along. Second, there's no law anywhere that a novel must never be long and meandering. (On the contrary....) Third, the political stuff in here is NOT in fact long and meandering; it mainly consists of a few direct statements of historical fact. What's going then is simply this: John Irving imagined a truly fantastic tale, then perceived (wisely) a need to ground that tale in the mundane. He did so by (a) including lots of nostalgic little bits that help to convince us these characters really did grow up in a certain time and place; and (b) including a few threads of historical trivia--things that were reported in our newspapers, and on TV, all along the novel's timeline. Since this novel spans at least forty years, those events serve us as stabilizing markers on that timeline--plus, the main characters go through some significant (very often surprising) changes as they mature, and that, too, requires (for believability) strong and plausible ties to familiar things. For example, one of the characters grows up to enter the military: what that means to his friends and family is inextricably tied here to the Vietnam war and the contemporaneous Anti-war protests; this story could not have been tied to another modern war and still had the same effect, or even a vaguely similar effect.One reviewer here comments that the narrator, John, lacks real depth and seems to have no purpose, other than to relate Owen's story. Hmm.... Sort of like Matthew, Mark....Anyway, consider this: buried within all that well-contrived nostalgia in the novel's opening chapters are the devices and signs that Irving needs to pull together in order to forge his big climax. I'm pretty sure that, if the telling of this tale were any less convoluted, were it more expedient, then all those devices and signs would stand out far too plainly--as plainly as, for example, the fact that the three little pigs can build their own homes. Or, to put that another way, I laughed out loud more than once while reading Owen Meany, and I'm sure I would not have, had I been less convinced that its absurdities were happening right here in the same "real world" that I inhabit. One cannot stage a miracle in the world of Three Little Pigs, because everything those pigs do--talk, etc.--is miraculous right from the start: nothing in a fairy tale can have much impact on adult sensibilities--adults, on the contrary, require a more thoughtful seduction--and every novel is, after all, more than anything else a string of too-convenient lies--so, if you like to rush straight to your climax--or, if foreplay truly disturbs you--then Owen Meany is probably not the book for you.
