Uwe Timm calls this charming book a novella. At over 200 pages, it might seem long for this description, but the book's format is scarcely larger than the palm of my hand, and the story itself is a simple one, without complications or subplots. Curried sausage apparently -- sliced sausage with curry sauce --  is a German food truck specialty. The author knows the woman who claims to have invented it, a Frau Lena Brücker, whose stall he used to visit on his occasional trips back to Hamburg. Now, many years later, he makes a final trip to the Northern port city, where he finds Frau Brücker, now completely blind, living in an old people's home. She remembers him, and in a week of afternoon visits bearing gifts of cake, he hears her story.It is something of a shaggy dog story, in that the narrative seems to be leading up to something, only to switch tracks a couple of times just before the end. If she were merely explaining the circumstances in which the idea of curried sausage came to her, Lena Brücker could have told the author in a single visit: basically the serendipitous combination of whatever ingredients could be found on the black market in the months following the war in 1945. But the old woman has a longer story to tell, one that is going to be of considerably greater interest to non-German readers than the origin of a strange street food. Like the stories of Scheherazade, it is the curried sausage that keeps him listening.For what Frau Brücker offers is a glimpse into conditions in Germany in late April and early May 1945, the days immediately before and after the German surrender. Much of it is her relationship with a naval petty officer called Bremer who, rather than return to the navy, has been ordered to report to the anti-tank unit mounting a last-ditch defense of Hamburg. Lena bumps into him by accident, takes him home with her, and invites him to stay. He thus becomes a deserter -- one of thousands, one would imagine, as military morale is crumbling everywhere. While most people seem to want a peaceful surrender, the ranting of hold-to-the-last-man diehards is echoed on the local level by fervent middle-managers and block wardens; both Lena and Bremer face real danger if he is discovered by the wrong person. Meanwhile, the British take over the city and impose a curfew which lasts several days before people begin to go out again into a world where everything has changed. It is the glimpse of that confused new world -- suspicion and instantly changed allegiances, food shortages and the black market -- that feels so utterly authentic and is the real reason for reading this delightful but otherwise slight little book.Read moreRead less
