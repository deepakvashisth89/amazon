Just to be clear, this is a brief review of Diane Solway’s “Nureyev, His Life”, 1998, published by Morrow, ISBN 0-688-12873-4. My copy, an ex-USA library hardback, possibly a publisher’s proof with ragged edges, did not disappoint. I had recently read extensive extracts from Julie Kavanagh’s detailed “Nureyev” and seen Ralph Fiennes’ “The White Crow” to add to my previous reading and viewing.Trying to capture this enigmatic, many-faceted, tempestuous, personally demanding but loyal to his few friends, his “druks” (Russian for friends you can trust”) was a challenge to which Solway rose with academic and journalistic panache. This is a weighty, six hundred page tome, filled with the detailed research one expects from an academic and the writing skills expected from a skilled journalist - a great combination, especially when themed with such a deep and passionate interest in ballet.Nureyev single-mindedly lived his life for and on the stage from early years but it meant the practicalities of his personal life had to be marshalled and organised in support, a task his “druks” performed to perfection in thrall to his genius and personality. His almost “Jekyll and Hyde” nature did little to aid their demanding tasks she illustrates on many occasions.Solway does not content herself with the performances and rehearsals - his professional life - she takes us behind the curtain into his intimate life in a valiant attempt to perceive and understand what it was that drove him so ferociously and relentlessly from an early age to develop and extend his innate skills as a dancer, to desert the Kirov, defect from Soviet Russia, create a stunning career in the free West and re-ignite an already great career of another, older dance. “I believe there is something in me still to be found,” he said regularly and Solway taps deeply into this life-long search.Solways records clearly how, in the 1970s, in New York, Nureyev met Jerome Robbins “at once ‘moody, demanding, hurting’ in the view of Leonard Bernstein, a frequent collaborator.” Nureyev wanted to prove to Robbins (and continue to prove to himself), that he could rise to any challenge. Like Bernstein and Robbins, Nureyev saw the demarcation lines between artistic styles but ignored them and, like them was successful, despite being shunned by the arch-conservatives in each style; even the arch-conservatives had to admire and respect them - but from a distance. “The staging of ‘Dances at a Gathering’ was one of the happiest collaborations in Rudolph’s career, for it offered him the chance to be seen as part of an ensemble, not an outsider. So fixed was he in the public mind as a visiting superstar , one associated with flashes of temperament and technical brilliance that choreographers never considered him for any group pieces. His success in ‘Dances’, however, owed everything to his ability to blend into the work’s overall conception, without forsaking his individuality. ‘Nureyev conforms,’ wrote Mary Clarke, though the phrase seemed an oxymoron, ‘but he is also the catalyst that brings an extra ounce of performance out of his colleagues.’” He also agreed to a lower fee. (P 367)This short extract illustrates clearly Solway’s perceptiveness, detailed research and journalistic ability to bring out so many facets of Nureyev’s character so quickly.For anyone interested in ballet and Nureyev, this book is a page turner; page by page she reveals the inner demands and drives which made him such a danseur of genius and still make him such a fascinating man, even after all these years.Read moreRead less
