This book was a disappointment, but not a waste of time. First the good points. It is a humorous (at times) revision of the standard history of the American Revolution. Our famous forefathers in this book are not the mythical figures who we know so well. Their numbers include the bright, the dull, the refined, the crude, the principled, the self-interested, the courageous, the cowardly. They smell. They fornucate. They get drunk. They fart. They fight over just about everything. They bumble their way to victory for a Revolution they never desired or understood, let alone intended. This book reminds us that the true Revolution did not take place on the battlefield, or even in the halls of Congress, but in the hearts and minds of average individuals. Now the bad points. The main fictional story line and characters are confusing and unconvincing. John Lawrence is variously an assistant and confidant of a number of American, British and French major-players. These positions gives him  unique opportunities to help shape events. Unfortunately, his role in this regard was never clear to me, at least in part because it was never clear to him. He simply takes his directions from Deborah, a prostitute-turned-revolutionary, with whom he also happens to be in love. Deborah flits in and out of his life as she, seemingly singlehandedly, directs the course of the Revolution from way, way behind the scenes. Deborah is apparently the only person who really understands what the Revolution is about and how it can be won. Unfortunately, she keeps all this a secret. She speaks of it only criptically, almost mystically. Deborah is where Lussier really lost me. Unfortunately, she is a major figure in his book and a significant distraction from what could otherwise have been a good story. I have one other complaint.
