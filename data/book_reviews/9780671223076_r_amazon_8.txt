With JeriLee Randall, the main character of "The Lonely Lady," Harold Robbins attempts a female perspective. Given that in a Harold Robbins novel a strong female character usually means a mistress who's good at oral sex, it should come as no surprise that the author's take on women's issues is more in line with Bob Guccione's than Betty Friedan's.The strongest storytelling is in the book's first part, focusing on JeriLee's growing up in a small east coast town in the post World War II era. Smart and independent, JeriLee aspires to be a writer, though writing eventually takes a backseat to -- you guessed it -- exploring her sexuality. This exploration ultimately leads to her being sexually assaulted by a friend of a famous playwright's son. Her stepfather wants to report the attack but it's JeriLee's uptight mother who insists they keep the matter private for fear of what the townspeople will say, never mind that the townspeople talk anyway, their gossip having little bearing on the truth. However, this unpleasantness does lead to JeriLee becoming the muse -- and wife -- of Walter Thornton, the playwright whose son was an accessory to her assault. Hey, when life hands you lemons....From there "The Lonely Lady" becomes a series of ups and downs -- mostly downs -- as JeriLee, after winning acclaim (and a Tony) as an actress, divorces Walter and attempts to make it on her own. The first play she writes bombs. The few acting gigs she gets turn sour, largely because she's particular about the roles she plays and with whom she'll have sex (that Tony award just doesn't open doors like you'd think). Her finances become dire. She needs a job, any job. Though she can type she doesn't even consider secretarial work, instead taking a job as a hostess at a nightclub and taking up with the coke-snorting gangster who owns it. That doesn't end well. In fact, nothing ends well for JeriLee. It seems the only chance she has of getting ahead is by using her body, yet even that route seldom pays off. There is a light at the end of the tunnel, kind of, but JeriLee's redemption is fleeting. Our heroine gets the last laugh in an Oscar® ceremony finale so outrageously over the top that it almost eclipses the hilarity that is the entire movie adaptation of this novel (and oh, how I wished the book's finale was filmed as written, but alas even a Pia Zadora vehicle has its moments of restraint).JeriLee makes for a pretty schizophrenic heroine, simultaneously rebelling against a patriarchal society (refusing to become a homemaker; speaking her mind even if it costs her jobs) while allowing herself to be used sexually to get her screenplays produced. As a story of female empowerment, "The Lonely Lady" is pretty laughable, but no one picks up a Harold Robbins novel for insight on the human condition. As trash, "The Lonely Lady" fares better, though on that level it's also pretty schizophrenic, with Robbins taking time to develop JeriLee's story in the first half only to give up and just put her in a series of degrading situations. It's not the smelliest offering on the Robbins trash pile, but it does get pretty ripe by the final third.
