It's an odd title for a novella about civilian life in Hamburg, Germany at the end of and shortly after World War II.  Is "the invention of curried sausage" a metaphor of sorts for how life in Germany was transformed after the demise of the Nazis?  I have to leave that question to those who know post-War German history and literature much better than I.  For me, THE INVENTION OF CURRIED SAUSAGE was a change of pace, charming and clever but a tad short of being great.For years after the War Lena Brücker had a stand in Grossneumarkt, in Hamburg, at which she sold curried sausage.  It was a huge success -- sliced and browned veal sausage (without casings), served on a paper plate in a sauce of ketchup, curry powder, nutmeg, aniseed, black pepper, and fresh mustard seed.  Frau Brücker stumbled (literally) upon the recipe shortly after World War II when, through a complex chain of bartering, she acquired a steady supply of veal sausage, ketchup, and curry powder.By the 1980's curried sausage has swept Germany as a low-brow culinary treat.  The anonymous narrator is curious as to its origin.  The first he ever ate, and the best he ever had, was from Frau Brücker's stand in Hamburg.  He tracks her down, now eighty-seven and living in a nursing home.  She admits that she invented curried sausage, and, over the course of seven meetings, she tells him how that invention came about.  Her story begins April 29, 1945, as the Wehrmacht is crumbling, when she befriends Bremer, a 24-year-old deserter, and hides him in her garret apartment in Hamburg.  Lena Brücker is then forty-three and she has been alone for six years (her husband, a heel who looks like Gary Cooper and plays the comb, had disappeared).  She likes Bremer's company and she likes the sex.  So when Hamburg surrenders, she does not tell Bremer.  For a month she keeps him in her apartment, deceiving him about the last days of the Third Reich.  (There is a faint echo of John Fowles's "The Collector".)Much of the novella is devoted to that month, with Bremer locked in the apartment, Lena Brücker going out each day to her work in a canteen, and the two of them at night sleeping and copulating on a mattress on the kitchen floor so that the creaking springs of her bed don't tip off what's going on to the woman in the apartment below.  For how Bremer's captivity came to an end, you will have to read the novella.  Ditto, for how Bremer provided the treasure that was the source of the chain of barter that secured Lena Brücker her supply of veal sausage, ketchup, and curry powder and for how she ended up inventing curried sausage.For modern fiction (it was written in 1993), THE INVENTION OF CURRIED SAUSAGE is written rather straightforwardly.  It does employ a rather deft narrative technique, transitioning back and forth among a first-person account of the anonymous narrator interviewing Frau Brücker in 1989, her occasional first-person reminiscences, and an omniscient third-person account of what happened back in 1945.  There are patches of humor, some of which consists of slyly giving the finger to the Nazis.
