True to form, Joanna Trollope shines her torch into all the hidden corners of the mind and emotions, as her characters face their individual and shared challenges in the novel "Brother and Sister." There is no doubt that the subject of adoption is a delicate and difficult one, but we have an extremely gifted and experienced author here, who does not shy away from the painstaking research required to present this most complicated of issues with raw honesty and sometimes heart rending believability. The story is about two adoptees, Nathalie and David, brother and sister, who decide to seek out their individual, natural mothers. They have always told themselves, each other and the world that being adopted meant they were free to be truly themselves without having the constraints of knowing their blood lineage. The truth is they were in that place in Egypt called "De Nile," and as Nathalie becomes aware of this charade she persuades David that the time has come to seek out the details every human being has the right to know regarding their origins. The ripple effects of their quest to discover their natural parentage are felt by their partners, children and the parents who adopted them. There is an awareness of shifting sands, as all that is comforting and secure in the family dynamics suddenly feels cast aside and abandoned to the winds of change. Also deeply affected are the natural mothers who have carried their burdens around with them like a backpack for so long. They have their lives, and families or relatives who have to deal with the sudden appearance of the baby who was given up for adoption in the past and is now an adult. Suppressed feelings are stirred, and long forgotten events brought back to life. From the reader's point of view, when a story goes this deeply into the truth of human frailties, it is impossible not to identify at some level, and feel a connection with these personalities and their universally common desire to be wanted, needed and loved. The dialogue is often deeply moving and the prose, whilst never overly sentimental or mushy, hits the spot just right with its compassion and insight.
