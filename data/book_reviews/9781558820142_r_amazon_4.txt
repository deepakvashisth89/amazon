This is one of the classic line of works in the story of the unsolved murder of Sir Edmund Berry Godfrey in 1678. The first criticisms of the verdict of the three men unjustly convicted came shortly after by Sir Roger l'Estrange and Madame Cellier who claimed it was suicide, made to look like murder so his relatives could inherit. In the 19th century Alfred Marks also claimed it was suicide & the extensive bruising to his chest was a result of post mortem lividity (where the blood collects at the lowest point). Carr however suggests that Godfrey waws murdered by Philip Herbert the Earl of Pembroke in revenge for Godfrey being the foreman of a jury that convicted him of murder (which he got off on account of being an Earl. Carr also gives some insight to Godfrey's life and his preference for mixing with working-class people rather than social climbing. The opinion of Home Office pathologist Keith Simpson in the appendix is useful - in it he says that Godfrey cannot have hanged himself due to the position of the marks on his neck.Later works are by Stephen Knight in the Killing of Justice Godfrey and Alan Marshall, The Strange Death of Edmund Godfrey.Knight concurs with Carr, but traces Godfrey's membership of a group of republican plotters called the Peyton Gang and suggests Pembroke killed him on behalf of his fellow conspirators and also suggests an illegitimate son (?).Marshall says it is not possible to know but feels that suicide is more likely, given Godfrey's  "melancholia " (his own father killed himself in 1664) by twisting a rope connected horizontally to a tree, as has been shown possible in prison suicides. However I doubt this, since Godfrey's neck was broken so that "one could as easily lay his head on one shoulder as the other" but in the prison suicides only the hyoid bone is broken.Carr's style is rather strange since he posits the reader's membership of a Murder Club and addresses us as if in a meeting.
