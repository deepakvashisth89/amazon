As a fan of Andrew Kaplan’s Scorpion series, I was particularly interested in tracing his main character’s history, which is revealed in Scorpion, first published in 1986. What I most admire about his later books is setting Scorpion up with an impossible mission, like in Scorpion Betrayal, where he has weeks to track down and stop an unknown terrorist, and watch the agent do the impossible. Here, he must track down a senator’s daughter, the victim of white slavery in an Arab world where women just disappear.  So, who does the CIA call on: an American who was raised by the desert Bedu, or how the Arabs refer to themselves, and the real treat is the flashbacks to Nick’s rescue as a child and his education under the watchful eye of Sheikh Zaid of the Mutayr. (He got his nickname little scorpion by cutting the sheikh’s hand with his pocketknife.)  But, as in the later novels, all is not what it seems, and here in the mid-1980s there’s one last cold-war standoff between the Soviets and their traitorous Arab ally—the woman’s kidnapper—and the West.
