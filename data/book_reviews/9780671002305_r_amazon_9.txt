I've argued for a while now that it takes an American author to write a really good book about the British Royal Family.  British authors always seem too in thrall to the Monarchy as a whole, or seem frightened of shaking the order of things.  There has been the occasional notable exception in recent years, such as Norman Baker's 'And What Do You Do?: What The Royal Family Don't Want You To Know'.  Otherwise it tends to be the same old glib rubbish and tired, breathless Gyles Brandreth-y anecdotes.  'The Decline And Fall Of The House Of Windsor' is a truly epic-sized work, covering the years from Queen Victoria's accession to 1995.  At nearly 500 densely-plotted pages long, it is a book that will require quite a bit of your time.I found the Victorian/Edwardian era and the early half of the 20th century more interesting, simply because the personalities involved seemed to be more complex and colourful.  By the time we get to the 1950s and the era of the present Queen I began to lose interest a bit.  This might simply be because from then on it was more familiar territory, but I don't think it was just that.  I think the Royal Family themselves became less interesting.  As the author points out, by the time we get to the modern era of royalty, the characters themselves really don't have much about them worth getting excited about.  As early as the mid-1950s the public were questioning what a royal family were for.The author unashamedly has his favourites and non-favourites amongst the gallery of characters.  It made a refreshing change to see someone sticking up for Queen Mary, who all too often gets pushed aside as a dull, emotionally repressed woman obsessed with Duty.  I've long felt that there was rather more to her than that, and Mr Spoto makes it clear he has quite a soft spot for her, in spite of her obvious failings as a parent. Queen Mary was that classic sort who made a rubbish parent, but a good grandparent, going out of her way to broaden the education of the little princesses when their parents really didn't seem that bothered.  By the time we get to the 1980s and 90s, Spoto clearly doesn't have much time for the then younger generation at all, apart from Diana.  And I can't say I blame him.  He touches on the dubious side of Prince Andrew long before the current unsavoury revelations.  I remember in the early 1980s, post-Falklands War, that Andrew became a tiresome, embarrassing menace, a boorish lout chucking paint over photographers, and frequenting kinky nightspots in London.  I swear that's why they married him off in a hurry, hoping it would calm him down.  Of course that didn't happen, and the author (rightly) portrays Andrew and Fergie as a pair of over-exuberant wastrels, living the high life at tax-payers expense.  There is a telling bit about the Windsor Castle fire in November 1992, when the then British Government made the heinous mistake of announcing that the tax-payer would pay for the repairs.  There was a huge outcry, and apparently staff at Buckingham Palace said they were shocked by the lack of sympathy shown.  Which somewhat suggests a load of people all living in a fluffy, pink bubble.The book stops before Diana's untimely death, and her notorious 'Panorama' interview.
