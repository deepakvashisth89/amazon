In the first half of this book we watch Orpha Chase grow from a socially awkward schoolgirl into a twice married young woman. The action – and there’s plenty of it – takes place in northern Kentucky mostly during the 1920s. This part of the story includes a couple of suicides, a couple of murders, some incest, adultery, man-boy sex, and plenty of other juicy material. But the best part of the book is the second half. In 1932, still a relatively young woman, Orpha moves to California and eventually pursues a writing career. This part of the book, which delineates Orpha’s professional rise, is very reminiscent of James M. Cain’s great novel “Mildred Pierce.” Both Orpha and Mildred have to overcome disastrous marriages. Both Orpha and Mildred eventually become huge successes in their chosen fields (restaurateur for Mildred; novelist for Orpha). Both women are sexually betrayed by their own daughters. Both must deal with the deaths of loved ones. The second half of “The Life I Really Lived” also includes a gripping courtroom drama, a passionate love affair with an up-and-coming movie star, and a detailed account of the rise of one of California’s first mega-churches (clearly patterned after Aimee Semple McPherson’s Foursquare Church). In the hands of another writer, this might have all been nothing but melodrama. But Jessamyn West infuses it with wit, intelligence and a number of fascinating philosophical meditations, including: What does it mean to be a Christian? What does it mean to be a Californian? What is the role of women in society? What do children owe to their parents, and vice versa? Can adultery ever be moral? This is a book that spans practically the entire lifetime of a woman who lived through some of the greatest societal changes ever to roil the American experiment.
