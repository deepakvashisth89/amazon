Firstly the edition: as can be seen from the photo, the Pan/Macmillan book is almost twice the size of the old Pan paperbacks I grew up on. Why every publisher in the world of today insists on making them so unwieldy is beyond me. Seeing as they all harp on about their use of (supposedly) environmentally-friendly forestry sources, one would expect them therefore to use LESS paper and not MORE (the font size is correspondingly huge – all definite no-nos in my world). And the copy I got was yet another “print on demand”, so the paper and especially cover are horrible.Now for the contents (if anyone is interested): I can’t imagine anyone buying and reading this who hasn’t watched the film at least once or twice (I’ve seen it dozens of times over the decades). But the novel does bring a good deal of extra focus to the character of Howie: his betrothal (we see his Mary in the opening scenes of the book on a picnic with him), his politics (he is "a convinced socialist" p. 126), his hobby of birdwatching, and most importantly his sympathetic nature – the opening scene has him fighting off an armed poacher who has just killed an eagle (Howie bravely saves the mate and egg), and the final scenes inside the wicker man has him making a superhuman effort to release caged birds, thus saving them from an atrocious and sadistic  death. All this is contrasted to the very one-dimensional characterisation of him in the film, where he comes across as a narrow-minded religious bigot who has no interest in anything whatsoever outside the laws of God and man.In fact I find his attitudes in the book far more humanistic and endearing than the pagan cult of Summerisle, with its continual cruely to animals – not only are many burnt alive in the wicker man (as in the film version), but many animals are also hanged by the inhabitants from an oak tree in the sacred grove as a form of votive offering (p.196 has Howie musing that whereas the Catholic church lights candles, the islanders instead "clearly offered up the sufferings of animals"). And obviously a human sacrifice is hardly a sign of an enlightened and progressive religious outlook: Howie is reminded in fact of the Nuremberg rallies, whose participants had “expressions just like this congregation” (p.281). In his last moments before being burnt alive, Howie himself “simply discovered that his manhood and his faith were indivisible”. He is also able to “convert” the intriguing character Beech (no sign of him in the film version), who acts as a kind of self-imposed guardian of the aforementioned sacred grove (and in assuming such a role the author is maybe forcing the Golden Bough analogies too far).So from this novel version of the story, ultimately Howie wins in my book, contrasting the ugliness (cf. especially the “Hand of Glory” – a hand severed from a fresh corpse, the fingers of which are burnt – and how it conveniently appears just in time is left unsaid) and cruelty of the islanders’ beliefs, combined with their embarrassingly exhibitionist displays of sex, with the restraint, dignity and humility of his own Christian faith.Read moreRead less
