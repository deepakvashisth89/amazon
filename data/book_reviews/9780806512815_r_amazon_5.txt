I first read Dalton Trumbo's "Johnny Got His Gun" back in 1990, en route from Japan to Bahrain, to meet my ship for Desert Shield and Desert Storm. I was 22, so sometimes when you're that young you don't make good decisions, as this was not the kind of book one should be reading if heading to a combat zone (I also made the mistake of reading Ron Kovic's "Born on The Fourth of July" during the same time period). Trumbo's novel is about a young man name Joe Bonham, who joins up to fight in World War I in order to protect democracy -- at least that's how he understands his involvement. While in combat Joe is horifically maimed, requiring the amputation of his arms and legs, while also leaving him blind and deaf with, as he puts it, just a hole for a face. The book is a series of flashbacks to his idyllic life in Colorado, while the scenes set in the present have Joe desperately attempting to first connect himself with the passing of time, and then later, due to loneliness, communicate with the nurses he can feel via vibration come into his room -- trying to place himself as a living being within his current reality. Trumbo's prose is simple, to the point, and angry -- there are long stretches with no punctuation, which fit the theme that we're hearing Joe's thoughts, as he desperately wants someone to "listen" to him via the Morse Code he's tapping out with what remains of his body. All he has is his mind, living in an existence where he no longer can touch with his hands or walk with his legs. If you've seen the music video for "One" by Metallica, you already know how the story ends. I won't spoil that here. Needless to say, Trumbo's words from 1939 are just as relevant today. To quote the Fallout video games: "War -- war never changes." My fellow combat veterans to this day are sacrificing their limbs and their lives for the sake of democracy -- even in conflicts where it's not abundantly clear why the sacrifices are being made in the first place. I was lucky -- my involvement in an armed conflict did not result in any physical injuries to me. I think all of us, though, who do participate are left with emotional scars. Sometimes at night I think back to that conflict and wonder how many innocent civilians I had helped cause the deaths of, albeit indirectly. I am proud of the six years I served my country, and would do it again if I could go back in time.
