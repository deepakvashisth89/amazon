Dennis McDougal penned this riveting, compelling true crime masterpiece nearly 30 years ago, detailing the horrific crimes committed by a truly evil monster against at least 65 young men and boys - for no good reason other than satisfying that monster's own wicked, twisted, perverted desires.I have read this book several times over the last 3 decades, and it never fails to terrify. McDougal does a masterful job pointing out the early failures of law enforcement bureaucracies to charge and prosecute Kraft for his early crimes.Many lives would have no doubt been spared had LE done a better job investigating Kraft's earliest known and only known living victim - young Joey Fancher had been raped and assaulted by Kraft in 1970, and of course the poor lad did not volunteer all that had happened to him at Kraft's apartment. Better investigative techniques would have gotten the poor kid to be more forthcoming, and would have secured a search warrant for Kraft's home. Kraft would have been prosecuted for his crimes against Fancher, and would have at least been watched closely by authorities from then on.Likewise, the investigation of Kraft for the murder of Keith Crotwell years later was stymied by a local LE bureaucracy. This murder by Kraft was also likely to have been assisted (at least after the fact) by his late former lover Jeff Graves. Again, many more lives would have been saved had the detectives in charge of the case been allowed to vigorously investigate and charge Kraft.McDougal also does a thorough job detailing Kraft's childhood, adult progression through college, a short Air Force stint where he revealed to his superiors that he was homosexual, and an eventual career as a successful  computer programmer by day... as he became a heinous serial sex slayer by night - a true monster whose torture and grizzly murdering of dozens of young men ranks him among the most prolific and twisted killers of all time.McDougal also does not fail to honor the victims and their families, despite the fact that his book must detail Kraft's torture and murder tally in painful detail. McDougal manages to accomplish this delicate balance quite well.As well, the author details two other serial killers operating in the same general area and targeting similar victims during the early years of Kraft's killings. McDougal distinguishes important differences between the murders of Kraft and fellow homosexual serial slayers Patrick Kearny and the team led by William Bonin. Kraft's murders were far more brutal, of course.Of course the sociopath Kraft has always claimed innocence. He even dismisses the score card of 65 victims written in his own shorthand as a list of friends and acquaintances. But when one considers that personal belongings of at least 16 of the victims were found in Kraft's residence, he had a nearly dead body in the passenger seat of his car the night he was pulled over for DUI, and that same seat was soaked with the blood of previous victims - his claim of innocence and no evidence against him rings hollow.Consider as well the credit card and other records placing him in the area of many of the victims when they disappeared (among other things McDougal recounts), and it's safe to say that Kraft is guilty of at least 16 murders beyond any reasonable doubt.It is unfortunate that Kraft has never been executed, and that he seems to have been having at least some fun behind bars - playing bridge with fellow serial killers Douglas Clark, William Bonin, and the late Lawrence Bittaker.
