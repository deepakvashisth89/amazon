One might think that the only people interested in reading Galileo would be flat-earthers (that bizarre sect who make it into the news for believing the earth is not spherical) or those who debate them.  Instead, this book is a epochal work, at least as important as the dialogues of Plato.  If one goes beyond the well-known story of Galileo and the Inquisition one discovers a superb work of art that tries, fairly successfully, to redirect the minds of humanity.The book can be divided into three parts:  An attack on the scholastic approach to nature, an exposition of a heliocentric universe and an effort to explain tides as a result of the earth’s motion.  While the second part is well known and the third part can be dismissed as historical arcana, Galileo’s acute insights into the proper methods to study nature are as important as his heliocentrism. One can see the craftsmanship behind the seemingly simple dialogue in first clearing the mind of Aristotelianism, then expounding his central theory and then a (supposed) result from the same theory.By using the method of dialogue, Galileo, like Socrates, tries to get the reader to think through their answers so that they come to see the truth of the matter for themselves.  Unlike Socrates however, Galileo doesn’t discuss the essence of virtue but the proper study of nature.  His expositor continually attempts to pull the Aristotelian listener away from relying on authorities and deep readings of the Aristotelian corpus to a mathematically guided perception of nature.In other words, if one is looking for a similar text, it most resembles the classic physics lectures of Richard Feynman. Feynman too tries to convey to undergraduates not only the results of physics but the excitement of using math and the senses to discover the fascinating world of nature—a world much more interesting than the deepisms of the Peripatetics.Modern heliocentrism may have been started by Copernicus but Galileo is by no means merely a popularizer. He is one of the first to argue from a turn away from a medieval mindset into the modern love of the scientific method.  If one likes reading popular science writing like Feynman or Richard Dawkins which actually engages the reader in the why beyond scientific facts this book will be a delight.
