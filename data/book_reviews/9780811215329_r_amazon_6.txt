To English-speaking readers "nineteenth century European literature" (with a few exceptions, such as Ibsen) generally means "nineteenth century French and Russian literature. We are familiar with Balzac, Flaubert and Hugo, with Tolstoy, Dostoyevsky and Chekhov, but much less so with their German, Spanish and Polish counterparts such as Fontane, Galdos and Sienkiewicz. Even Manzoni is not particularly well-known, although his "I Promessi Sposi" is virtually regarded as a national institution in Italy. (This division has little to do with "big" and "small" languages; German and Spanish both have more native speakers than French).The Portuguese author José Maria de Eça de Queiroz (the spelling he himself used) is another nineteenth century novelist who is venerated by his countrymen but little-known outside his native land.  His "The Crime of Father Amaro", was first published in 1875, but this translation is based upon his third version from 1880, rewritten to remove some of the more controversial elements from the original text, which had scandalised public opinion when it first appeared.Even so, there is still plenty in this third version which would have caused controversy in the moral climate of the 1880s. The “Father Amaro” of the title is a parish priest in the provincial town of Leiria who falls in love with, and has a sexual relationship with, Amélia, the beautiful daughter of his landlady. As one might expect, such a topic was bound to be deeply shocking in a devoutly Catholic society like Portugal.Eça clearly had no sympathy with the idea of compulsory celibacy for the clergy. Amaro has been persuaded to enter the priesthood by a condition attached to a legacy from an aristocratic patron, but lacking any true religious vocation struggles with his vow of celibacy. Eça’s objections to Catholicism, however, went further than this single issue. He is not, I think, suggesting that all would have been well if Amaro had been permitted to marry Amélia, as would have been the case in a Protestant country. Amaro’s flawed character, in particular his selfishness and amorality, would have made him a bad husband, and it is these same flaws which make him a bad priest.The novel is subtitled “Scenes of Religious Life”, a possible reference to George Eliot’s “Scenes of Clerical Life”, but Eça’s view of the Church is far more critical than Eliot’s. Amaro’s fellow-priests, with one exception, are treated equally harshly. Canon Dias is complacent and lazy, interested more in food than the Church, and is having an affair with Amélia’s mother. Father Brito is a coarse, barely educated ruffian, whose answer to any criticism of the Church or himself is to lash out with his fists. Father Natário is more intelligent, but vindictive, spiteful and a master of the dark arts of intrigue. The one exception is the relatively liberal Father Ferrão, who provides comfort to Amélia when she finds herself pregnant and abandoned by Amaro.Eça, however, can be just as critical of free-thinkers, whose attacks on religion are often portrayed as self-serving. João Eduardo, a lawyer’s clerk and Amaro’s rival for Amélia’s affections, pens a viciously anti-clerical diatribe, motivated more by sexual jealousy than by principle.  Doctor Godinho, a liberal lawyer and newspaper proprietor, prints this diatribe in his paper, ostensibly in the interests of free speech but really in the hope of increasing circulation. When this stunt backfires, however, he changes his mind about free speech and forbids any more attacks on religion. Doctor Gouveia, the atheistic local medical practitioner, is less hypocritical, but nevertheless comes across as self-satisfied and dogmatic.“The Crime of Father Amaro” can therefore be seen as a satirical portrait of19th-century Portuguese small-town society. Yet there is more to it than this.Eça achieves the difficult task of combining some biting, and often very amusing, satire, with a deeply serious psychological novel. The most sympathetic, and most completely realised, character is Amélia, portrayed as torn between her sexuality and her religious beliefs which, unlike Amaro’s, are quite genuine. Even Amaro, at least in the early part of the story, is treated with some understanding; he may be a bad priest and a bad man, but Eça is interested in understanding the process whereby he became one.It is not always easy to work out what Eça’s own solution to Portugal’s problems would have been; in the last chapter he seems to be advocating either a revolutionary bloodletting along the lines of the Paris Commune or the deeply reactionary idea of a return to the values of Portugal’s “heroic age” during the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Nevertheless, we should not judge him too harshly for that; great writers are not always great political thinkers.When I reviewed “The Maias”, possibly his best-known work, I said that Eça was a writer who ought to be better known outside his own country. That opinion has been strengthened by my reading of “The Crime of Father Amaro” , which I would rate more highly than “The Maias”. Certainly his main characters, especially Amaro and Amélia, are better realised than any in that work. In Margaret Jull Costa’s translation, the prose is highly readable and fast-moving; unlike many 19th-century novels this is one which will keep the reader turning the pages to the end.
