Modern historians have at their disposal the findings of disciplines such as epigraphy, papyrology, and numismatics, which enable them to chart the course of events more accurately than Gibbon could.  In his day, these  sciences were in their infancy, and with rare exceptions he depended on the  primary works of the ancient historians, who themselves were prone to error  and bias.  Thus, it is possible today to write a history of the Roman  Empire that is significantly more accurate than Gibbon's.  All of this is  largely beside the point, as Gibbon is a giant upon whose shoulders the  pygmies stand.  I think it quite probable that he is the greatest writer of  non-fiction English prose to ever live (I'll accept arguments on  Churchill).  He also had an incredible feel for the sweep and grandeur of  his subject, and a passionate involvement with his material that is sadly  lacking in many works of history (a refreshing recent exception is the  ringing defense of Stilicho in "Theodosius: the Empire at Bay,"  by Williams and Friell).  "The Decline and Fall" is one of the  greatest works of history and one of the greatest works of literature.
