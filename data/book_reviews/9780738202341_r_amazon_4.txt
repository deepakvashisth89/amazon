The unique feature of this book is that the author combines a personal lost-love story with masterly accounts of quantum physics and neuroscience.  The author has real literary talent and manages to weave together the different storylines into a coherent whole. His treatment of the history to quantum physics has a poetic feel to it, as he leads the reader through the theories and experiments in the early 1900's where science moved from a billiard-ball conception of the atom to the mysterious wave function.  Walker then gives fresh accounts of the leading interpretations of quantum physics from the Copenhagen interpretation (don't worry about reality, just read your instruments) to the bizarre many-worlds interpretation (each of the uncountable wave states represents a different world.) He then takes on the difficult question of how is it that under quantum theory, the observing consciousness collapses the wave function? He devotes many pages to Bell's theorem and the finding that the world indeed is not made of independent objects but is in some sense entangled. He then makes a detour to Zen Buddhism and notes Zen's insights into the nature of consciousness.  (Along the way, we see that Walker's memory of his teenage romance has caused him to reject extreme materialism and to search for something more; hard to take issue with that.)  Using quantum physics and Bell's theorem, Walker then proposes that the source of consciousness is the "collection of potentialities" that develop as electrons and the brain's structure interact. He then plows new ground by proposing a way to measure the "consciousness stream" of the brain, along with the "will stream," based upon the firing speed of brain synapses. Here he finds the "quantum mind," the one consciousness that we all share, emerging from the brain. "Reality is the observer observing," he writes, and "our observation -- our coming together -- created matter." Although this book is worth reading because it explores new ground and stretches the intellect, in the end the theory may appear self-contradictory. If there is no independent world outside of consciousness and if the mind is primary, then would not that also hold true for the human body and the brain?  He frames the question, "where is in Mind in the Brain?" but maybe the radical question really is, "where is the brain in the Mind?"
