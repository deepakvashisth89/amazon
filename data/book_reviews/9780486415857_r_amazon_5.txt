I must preface my review by admitting that I am hardly an Austen aficionado (this being my second read from her), although I have watched many film adaptations of her works. It appears through looking at criticisms and reviews that many are divided on this novel because of the novel’s protagonist, Fanny Price.I have criticisms of the novel, but they were not because of Fanny, who I felt was a redeeming figure in the novel in a sea of rather unpleasant characters. While not exactly the most engaging character, at least she was a character could readily root for, has some substance, and does have moments where she sticks to her convictions. I understand, however, that Austen was purposeful in how we as readers respond to her characters.I tended to sympathize with Fanny because I looked at the book through the lens of her background and situation vs. what she experiences when she arrives at Mansfield Park and how others treat her. Austen does an apt job examining and comparing the different class structures and stations through her characters and their situations and conflicts. (Although I will readily admit some of the intricacies probably went over my head). Fanny, from the point she arrives, must work through these differences both internally and externally.So, there is a good deal of complexity within the novel in terms of prevalent themes of the day (class, marriage, gender roles, social constructs), and criticizing and putting these social conventions under a microscope. And yet, I still cannot say this is novel I found very enjoyable.This is because of the other issue I had with the novel, and that is its structure and length. The novel is structured into three volumes, and it is only in the final volume that events move at a decent pace, and we see how things will pan out, conflicts resolved. The two volumes felt extremely overwritten, and we are hit with a preponderance of characters who are not very memorable. I just felt like the first two volumes could have been condensed, but I realized that Austen was writing in serial format.There is, curiously enough, a chunk of the book dedicated to many of the characters trying to cast and put on an extravagant play (“Lover’s Vows”). Some complain about this section and wonder where it comes from or why it is in the book. I didn't mind this section at all, and the failed aspects of the play seem to signify and parallel the failed aspects and dynamics of so many romantic ideals and relationships contained within the plot.Overall, I felt divided, as some parts were just difficult to wade through in the first volumes.
