As a general reader not obsessed with the JFK assassination, I had always wondered how the various conspiracy theories would hold up to a meticulous, objective analysis, and why so many seem to persist. This book answers both questions. With painstaking detail, the author works backward from Oswald's arrest to explore not only how but why he would become a presidential assassin, and does the same for his assassin in turn, Jack Ruby. The level of patience and meticulousness involved in this effort is astounding: Posner not only seems to have reviewed all existing literature but has personally interviewed witnesses and verified facts down to the level of phone records and cross-referencing the testimony of anyone remotely involved in the case. Such attention to detail might have been numbing if applied to a less dramatic subject, but here it brings vividly to life a story whose basics are already planted firmly in the collective consciousness of most readers.My only criticism is that the author's meticulousness extends to the extensive use of footnotes, which seem to pop up at the bottom of almost every page. Although they usually provide significant background information, I found they bogged down the flow of the story and often had a smug tone in dismissing some detail of a particular conspiracy theorist's conclusions. My suggestion would be to read the main text of each chapter in continuity first, then go back to the footnotes if you are in the mood for more detail. Personally, I would have preferred they had been gathered into endnotes, a possible improvement for any future edition.The inherent appeal of the simplest explanation for all the facts, which is also consistent with the detailed personality analyses he provides, should indeed close the case for any reasonable person. Yet what I found most satisfying about Posner's work was that his rebuttal to conspiracy theories went beyond presenting monumental amounts of research and evidence. The very nature of conspiracy plots makes trying to disprove them with evidence alone futile, as yet another layer of conspiracy can always be invoked as a counterargument, no matter how incredible. Posner understands this and takes time near the end of the book to explore the psychological underpinnings of such theories - how there seems to be a cultural need for epic explanations for epic events, and how the memory of eyewitnesses is malleable with time and suggestion, if not ulterior motives.
