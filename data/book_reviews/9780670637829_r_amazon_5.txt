19th Century Russian history poses a difficult question:  How did the nation able to defeat Napoleon in 1814-15, the nation that was the "policeman of Europe" in 1825, become outdated and weak by 1855, a stew of discontent and revolution by 1881, a beached whale by 1917, and the dominion of a new, red Czar by 1935?  This book addresses the puzzle of Russian history, the great early modern empire that failed to modernize, having been paralyzed by its own history of absolutism.  Other reviews give summaries of content, and include some of the author's insights.  I was impressed with the author's knack for putting me into the mind of a Russian ruler, and a Russian citizen, in ways I have not perceived before.  Among others, he points out that the vast Russian domains that frightened the relatively small nations of Europe, to the czars represented vast indefensible borders that stretched the precious Army to the breaking point; that 19th century Russian history appeared to be playing out the endgame of the boyar capitulation, who in turn played out the endgame of the Mongol conquest and dominion of Russia; and that Russian history focuses on intellectuals and artists so much precisely because, in the autocracy, there was no actual political party that could be the "action arm" of a philosophy or idea.  While various theories of government and society appeared in Britain in the form of different parties and leaders, in Russia all of these ideas remained only theoretical, existing in the pages of writings and almost nowhere else.I learned a lot about Russia from this book, and recommend it to almost anyone.
