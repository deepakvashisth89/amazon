A new edition of a classic history raises many questions, foremost among them: Assuming "The French Revolution" told as much of the truth about the past as it was humanly possible to tell in 1837, how much of the book is still true? All of it, according to the Introduction:"His new friend John Stuart Mill sent him a library of books on the French Revolution, and in a letter of 1833 acknowledging the gift, Carlyle announces that the 'right History' of the Revolution would be the 'grand Poem of our Time.  . . .  [The] man who could write the truth of that, were worth all other writers and singers.' 'The French Revolution' is just that, one of the grand poems of his century, yet its poetry consists in being everywhere scrupulously rooted in historical fact." (xvii)So, "The French Revolution" is “everywhere scrupulously rooted in historical fact” (Points 1-3) and retains 100% accuracy into the present (Point 4). These are bold claims. But, are they true? Here are four reasons to doubt it:1. Proving the book is all true would require a prodigious research project to verify the reliability and rigor of myriad statements from hundreds of sources. The results would then need to be corroborated by scholars expert in those sources. If the claim of 100% accuracy was subjected to this verification process (or something like it), why doesn’t the Introduction say so and present the proof?2. Perhaps, “rooted” refers to Carlyle’s scholarly apparatus providing an irrefutable factual basis for his history. If so, this is questionable on two grounds. First, Carlyle’s apparatus is thin: about one footnote per page—a paltry number for a serious non-fiction work. Second, even if Carlyle’s footnotes were as abundant and thoroughgoing as “everywhere” and “scrupulously” indicate that still wouldn’t be enough to prove total certainty because “footnotes have never supported, and can never support, every statement of fact in a given work. No apparatus can prevent all mistakes or eliminate all disagreements.” (Anthony Grafton, "The Footnote: A Curious History" [Cambridge: Harvard University Press, 1997], 233.)3. Even were "The French Revolution" every bit as accurate as the Introduction claims, complete verisimilitude would still not be guaranteed because, depending on the validity of the inferences, Carlyle’s conclusions might be partly true, half true, mostly false, or wholly false. Thus, the implication that since every statement is “rooted in “historical fact,” the book’s conclusions must also be true is invalid. (Tracy Bowell and Gary Kemp, Critical Thinking: A Concise Guide, 3d ed. [London: Routledge, 2002; 2010], 62-66.)4. Finally, even if "The French Revolution" was “everywhere scrupulously rooted in historical fact,” it could only have been so at the time of its writing (1833-1837).  For historians not to have discovered new evidence—after more than 150 years of historiography—that challenges at least some of Carlyle’s facts would be nothing short of miraculous.Given these reasons to doubt, the Modern Library might want to consider supporting the Introduction’s extraordinary claims with extraordinary evidence or replacing them with a more plausible assessment of the book’s veracity.Read moreRead less
