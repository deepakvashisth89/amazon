Even the best of authors have their off days, and sadly, their off novels. Like this one. Only Heyer  completists should bother, and even they might want to think twice before plunging into this well-written (from a prose point of view) but ultimately unsatisfying morass.Most of Georgette Heyer's novels stand the test of time brilliantly, and her historical novels especially can hold their own with, and usually rise head and shoulders above, anything anyone trying to write Georgian or Regency romance novels is producing today. (If you can stomach the occasional glimpses of Heyer's unfortunate antisemitism.) Her Murder Mysteries don't fare quite so well as a modern reader will normally figure out who the murderer is fairly quickly (in one book, it was clear to me from the first line).  Still, her mysteries are usually entertaining and provide marvelous snapshots of the times in which they were written.  Her dialogue normally sparkles and her characters are usually well-drawn.But Penhallow isn't really a Murder Mystery. You walk down the hall with the murderer, two-thirds into the book, as they go to commit their act of crime (or possibly self-defense) and you are probably rooting them on because the "victim" has been victimizing the murderer and the rest of his household for decades. The murderer, by-the-way, has been driven by sleep-deprivation and the unrelenting daily mental cruelty of the "victim" to a state of nervous exhaustion where "temporary insanity" might apply. As the police never actually identify whodunnit, the legal viability of such a defense is never put to the test. However, some unintended consequences of the victim's death truly appall the murderer, who thought they were setting other family members free, only to find that the circumstances for those persons worsen, and they are more firmly entrapped than ever. The murderer blames themself for the second death, but the reader knows that the second death had little to do with the "victim's" murder, and stems from the absolute cruelty of a life-long lie and family secret the "victim" gleefully reveals to them the morning of their death. Neither the murderer nor anyone else in the house save for one elderly servant is privy to said secret, so the second death preys on the murderer's mind and nerves. One wonders how long it will be before the murderer has a complete mental breakdown and confesses their crime, upon which they will probably be carted off to a nice mental sanitarium somewhere.This is a bleak story filled with unlikable characters, where there is neither justice nor catharsis, nor solved mystery to pay the reader back for the time invested in reading this work.
