Dom Manuel, the absent hero of Cabell's `Jurgen' and the `Silver Stallion' is now introduced to us; a simple, self-centred and introspective fellow who rises from a  pig-keeper to a minor monarch, somewhere in southern Europe in the early middle ages, by a mixture of luck, simplicity and physical gifts, especially with ladies; and by taking literally his mother's dying wish to make himself a figure in the world; but his efforts never satisfy.  But apart from being an excellent tale, the book is full of the most delightful prose with at least 17 passages of what Cabell called `contrapunctal prose' in effect poems written in prose. (For details of this , see the excellent monograph by Warren A McNeill called `Cabellian Harmonies.' There is also, like in Jurgen a lavish but bewildering sprinkling of deities and other mythological figures from all over the world; many are explained in `Notes on Figures of Eath' by Cranwell and Cover, but you don't need to know them. Through this book and others runs a rather pessimistic attitude to married life, exemplified by the wizard who has carried off a princess but has arranged for the hero to rescue her: `She has a strong will in her white bosom, and a tireless tongue in her glittering head, and I do not equally admire all four of these possessions.'Note; beware of the Kindle collected Cabell, quite a few of his books are there but it's all centre-justified and so difficult to read.Read moreRead less
