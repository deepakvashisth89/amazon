The fourth of the five novels that comprise Doris Lessing's Children of Violence series, Landlocked (1965) find's Lessing's autobiographical heroine Martha Quest meandering around as the Second World War enters its last stages and then the early post war years bring the rift between the wartime allies in the West and East. Set between the years 1944 and 1949, when Martha is in her mid and late 20s, it portrays a young woman with no real goal or purpose in life as the great cause of her earlier years, her dedication to radical leftist politics, sputters in the face of cold war hostilities, defections and emigrations, ideological splintering and the alienation of black activists from the white-dominated Communist movement. At this point, Martha's political activities consist mainly of running errands and performing fairly minor tasks like transcribing speeches.She is still married to Anton Hesse, modeled on Lessing's real-life second husband Gottfried Lessing, but both both are seeing other people, with each other's knowledge and consent. Martha has an affair with Polish ex-patriate Thomas Stern but it seems more self-indulgence than grand passion, while her relationship with her mother remains prickly as her father suffers through a prolonged terminal illness. She is also alienated from Caroline, her daughter from her first marriage, to whom she can't even acknowledge her maternity.Reflecting Martha's rather scattered life, Lessing's narrative also farts around. There is no real unifying theme or central plot line. It's almost as if Lessing novelized her journal entries from this period, which ends with Martha's emigration to Britain in 1949.At least the book does explain the novel cycle's title, Children of Violence, by noting that Martha was born just after the first Great War and spent most of her formative years at a time when the world was getting ready for the next one. It was a girlhood and young adulthood marinated in violence and the expectation of violence. The book repeatedly notes that 44 million people were estimated to have died in WWII just to try to put some number to the enormity, though I don't know if Lessing grasped the irony that her autobiographical character spends this time supporting the activities of one of history's biggest mass murderers, Josef Stalin. In any case Martha does toward the end of the book begin to start having doubts about whether the Soviet Union really is all that in terms of promoting human happiness. Call it a sign of growing maturity.One note for anyone wishing to read Landlocked: read the first three Children of Violence books first, not only for context on Martha's activities, but because Lessing's narrative pretty much assumes you have, as she reintroduced a number of characters from the previous books with no real review of who they are or where they fit into Martha's previous life. While I did read the first three books, it was over an extended period and so I had some trouble remembering who each one was again.
