From 1845 until 1875  inventions, discoveries, and wars were changing Europe and the adolescent United States, but Ms. Cather's Saga focuses  tightly upon the newly annexed territory of New Mexico ("a territory larger than Central and Western Europe, barring Russia")  and the efforts of a few individuals to influence the course of history.  Jean  Marie Latour, French missionary serving in Ontario, accepted the challenge that accompanied the bishop's mitre  for this new diocese, a diocese which boasted thirty indian nations, "each with its own customs and language, many of them openly hostile to each other."  The vicarate was also home to  "the Mexicans, a naturally devout people" who were "untaught and unshepherded," "disolute priests," and the new and often unwelcome Americans who sought to  reshape the countryside into another American state.The French cleric must of necessity "eat dried buffalo meat and fijoles with chili, and he will be glad to drink water when he can get it. . ..(the) country will drink up his youth and strength as it does the rain.  He will be called upon for eery sacrifice, quite possibly for martyrdom."  Death comes to the Archbishop is paradoxically the story of the life of the remarkable Bishop Latour, his great friend and comissionary Father Vaillant, who ultimately became the bishop of Colorado, and the story of the diverse men and women who lived, loved, fought, and died together in the amazing time of change.  This bishop walks, rides on mules and horses, and travels immense distances to meet, to baptize, to marry and to serve the many communities and cultures that are now called New Mexico.Willar Cather is a master storyteller, and the story she tells is as huge as the territory of New Mexico.  She weaves the perspective of a Frenchman who loves his food and wine and dislikes the American "flimsy wooden buildings with double porches, scrollwork, and jackstraw bannisters and posts painted white," together with the commentaries and observations of the local individuals- each one unique, each one startling, and each one intensely human.  This is a story of a land.  This is a story of people.  This is the story of thousands of individuals and how each one influences his friend, his enemy, his progeny and his future.  This is the story of how one man who is human is capable of a love as diverse and as immense as the territory in which he serves, lives and ultimately dies.Ms. Cather's style is amazingly simple yet intensely descriptive.  She chooses her words carefully, and each one draws the reader into the story, into the territory of New Mexico.  She does not editorialize; she shows. And what Ms. Cather reveals includes the perspective of not one man or one culture but a prism of cultures and individuals brought into focus through the lens of an outsider, a French Bishop who cares.
