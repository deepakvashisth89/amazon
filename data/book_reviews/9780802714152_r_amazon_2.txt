I bought this book when Gingerich's recent death reminded me that I knew a bit about his work, but had never read any of his books. This one is a fairly light read on the subject of his investigations into the work of Copernicus. It is the story of his search for original early editions of Copernicus' work, and in particular the early impact it had on European science as shown by the marginal notes made by early readers. Gingerich is thus illustrating the practice of historical research by studying who had the books, what they wrote in or about the book, and how it related to the evolution of scientific thought. It also provides the story of tracking down copies to study, where they are now housed around the world. Much of this work was done ~50 years ago around the time of the 500th anniversary of Copernicus' birth, and involved travel to libraries behind the Iron Curtain where Copernicus lived and worked. The book makes me appreciate the efforts of scientific historians to clarify the process of science and the process of diffusion of knowledge, which is rarely as simple as we might assume. It is definitely not a story of better ideas being recognized and immediately adopted by all.
