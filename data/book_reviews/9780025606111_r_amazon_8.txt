Although great things were expected when Roger Kahn teamed up with Pete Rose to write the autobiography of baseball's Hit King, the product was a body of work that left everyone unsatisfied.  A spokesman for Pete complained that Kahn barely mentioned the five seasons that Rose played in Philadelphia in `Pete Rose: My Story.'  The author complained that the subject was uncooperative and evasive.  The public waited three years for the book, and may have waited longer if Pete Rose was not banned from baseball.  Sales were sluggish and never picked up after Pete used a promotional visit to the Donahue Show to announce that he was seeking professional help for gambling, despite the fact that he insisted in this book that he had no problem with betting.Pete Rose had three noteworthy mannerisms on the diamond that made him a conversation piece among baseball fans.  The book mentions his head first slides and his habit of running out walks to first base.  His third mannerism occurred when Pete played defense.  Pete would SLAM the baseball into the turf after a putout if it was the third out of an inning.  It was something that I noticed during his years in Philadelphia, when Pete moved to first base.Kahn did not mention this mannerism or the amazing 23-22 game at Wrigley Field in 1979.  Pete most assuredly recalled that wild game because he had four RBI and scored four runs in the contest.  Rose also made a great catch of a foul ball that Bob Boone dropped in the 1980 World Series, the first one played entirely on Astroturf.  In the 1980 Fall Classic, Willie Mays Aikens was the second coming of Gene Tenace.Tenace was not as much of an average ballplayer as Kahn suggested.  After the 1972 series, Tenace became a starter with 600 plate appearances a season and produced solid OBP numbers with some pop.  It was considered a notable acquisition when Tenace joined the San Diego Padres.The 1972 Fall Classic had the fake intentional walk, which Pete also must have recalled even though it happened to Johnny Bench.  As a joke, the Athletics pretended to call for an intentional walk when Pete came up to bat later in the series.  There is a picture of Pete laughing about it as he stepped into the batter's box.  Yet that incident was not discussed, nor the famous knock-down pitch by Dickie Noles at George Brett in 1980, or even the controversial hit by pitch in Game Six of the 1975 World Series.  Rose was hit by a pitch in extra innings to lead off the 11th inning of Game Six, but nobody could figure out where, or if, the pitch hit Rose.  While Rose was forced out at second on the next at-bat, Griffey remained on first and might very well have scored the winning run in the 11th inning if Dwight Evans had not made a superior defensive play in RF to rob Joe Morgan of an extra-base hit.If Roger Kahn wanted to throw a tough baseball question at Pete Rose, he could have asked why he believed the Reds lost so much when they traded Tony Perez after the 1976 season.  The 1977 Reds were still 2nd in the NL in scoring runs, and Dan Driessen had similar offensive numbers to Tony Perez not only in 1977, but for the next four seasons.  The Reds pitching staff gave up nearly ¾ of a run more a game in 1977.  They lost Don Gullett to free agency, and Gary Nolan to a recurrence of problems with his sore shoulder.  The two pitchers from Montreal acquired for Perez were totally inadequate.  The Reds were overtaken by the Dodgers in 1977 because the Reds had a mediocre pitching staff.Cincinnati made a blockbuster trade for Tom Seaver during the 1977 season, but Pete Rose had nothing to say about it.  Or, rather, Roger Kahn had nothing to write about the trade.  Pete, most assuredly, would have had something to say if Roger Kahn had asked him about Seaver.  These two giants of the National League, Seaver and Rose, played together for a season and a half and there is not one peep about their time as teammates in this book.  Yet, pointing a finger at the ghost-writer may be unfair because Kahn insisted that Rose was uncooperative with his time and his contributions.Some of Pete's career highlights are mentioned in the book, so it was not a total bust.  His collision at home plate with Fosse in 1970 and his 44-game hit streak in 1978 are discussed in detail.  Rose did say that he would walk through hell in a gasoline suit to play baseball, but he also wanted to be paid fairly for his accomplishments.  His contract squabbles are an interesting part of this book.  There is an early chapter about his record-breaking 4192nd base hit, as well as some details about his return to Cincinnati as a player-manager that made his chase of Ty Cobb possible.  When Rose collected his 4000th hit as an Expo, there was some debate as to whether or not he had enough hits left to break Cobb's record.The book also included a flimsy defense against Pete's paternity suit and a tawdry implication over the writer for Playboy who interviewed Rose.  The implication, by the way, could be true - Samantha Stevenson's daughter bears some resemblance to Doctor J.  Erving denied the allegations, and the issue of paternity probably did not matter in the life of the child.A reader cannot construct an adequate opinion on whether Pete Rose deserves his lifetime ban on baseball, or whether he belongs in the Hall of Fame, after reading only this book.  Pete admitted that he did bet on baseball, and he did bet on the Reds, in alater autobiography with Rick Hill. Pete also said that he did not make bets from the clubhouse in that book despite the contention of the Dowd report that he did.  Mike Sokolove described the case that Major League Baseball had on Pete in his unauthorized biography of Rose, released one year after this book.Kahn admitted that the advance of $750,000 - split two ways, less the fee to the agent - meant a tempting $300,000 payday.  He also said that the opportunity to collaborate on the project with Pete Rose made the project hard to resist.  Little did Kahn realize that collaboration with Pete meant, `You're the writer... figure it out.'Other writers - such as Mike Sokolove - claimed that the book hurt Kahn's reputation, but Kahn will only go as far as to characterize the project as a disappointment.
