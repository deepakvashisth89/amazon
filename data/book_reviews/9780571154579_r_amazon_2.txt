It is very difficult to say exactly what this novel is "about". Is it a "gay" novel? Hard to say. Knowing, as I do, the back story of the English writers Isherwood, Auden and Spender who went to Germany for sex (this is openly admitted, by the way, in the early passages of the book) there is surprisingly little or no sex in the novel at all. Men become intense friends with German boys, some of whom are in it for cash, men share bedrooms and even beds with boys but nothing takes place at all. The most erotic passage of the book deals with the moment when the principal character orders the (presumably beautiful) young German to undress so that he can photograph him in the nude. All for art, as they say.Since the novel takes place first in the late 'twenties, and then on the cusp of the 1930s, we have the Nazis appearing and becoming ever more powerful as a political force in Germany. Some of the boys are attracted to this new political force. The Englishmen are horrified. The novel ends before the Nazis take power. Thus it cannot be classified as a "political" novel at all.The prose is luxuriant and baroque, as one would expect from a writer who is principally known as a poet. No doubt Sir Stephen has a firm, even luxurious command of our language (his poetry alone proves as much) but to waste his talent on a semi-pointless narrative seems unfortunate. The late Evelyn Waugh was not an admirer of Sir Stephen's prose. He said that to read a passage by him was "to experience all the horror of seeing a priceless Sevres vase in the hands of a chimpanzee". Readers will have to make up their own mind.Read moreRead less
