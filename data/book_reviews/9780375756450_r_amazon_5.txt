As has been widely commented, historian Edmund Morris uses an extremely unconventional device in this Reagan bio by framing the narrative as his (sometimes dramatized and fictive) recollections as a character who weaves in and out of Reagan's life. Yet Morris is also meticulous about annotating all of his references to Reagan so that his observations of RR and the events surrounding him are verifiable; indeed Morris often uses multiple sources to support a single event. Ultimately, Morris attempts (and often succeeds at) insights that most historians and journalists would fumble over. The quality of his writing and his penchant for irony, intimate description, humor, and elegies to people's lives and hopes are just astounding at times. And those sections of the book, particularly the last third, where Morris is recounting his own true experiences as a hand-picked biographer are of tremendous historical value. Morris had a front-row seat at some key events, such as the first Reagan-Gorbachev summit. The book is especially insightful in plumbing the draw-and-pull that Reagan had over people. Morris's achievement -- and part of the reason for his unconventional approach -- is to show that Reagan possessed the strange capacity to imbue meaning in the lives of people around him, so that others often rotated like satellites around his gravitational sun. This book is psychological history on a grand scale, and is often quite convincing at its most personal moments. Whatever one ultimately makes of Morris's literary choices, this book attains remarkable heights in capturing both the personal dimensions and outer significance of a presidential era.
