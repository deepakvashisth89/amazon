Several years ago an employee at my daughters' daycare heard I liked to read and gave me a huge bag of Stephen King books. I think she was moving and was hoping to downsize. It was a very nice surprise: There were some mass market paperbacks, a few trade paperbacks, and even a few first edition hardcovers. Because all of King's books are still in publication (with one exception) I'm usually reading an anniversary edition with review quotes and a foreword about the book's lasting impact. Even though I'm not really a book collector kind of guy, it's kind of neat sometimes to get my hands on a 35-year-old first edition, with its flashy cover and text that has no idea if it'll be a hit. It's a cool feeling.That said, after carrying the massive Needful Things around with me on the train for two days, its 690 pages felt like 690 pounds in my hands, so I said nuts to that, and bought the Kindle edition. It felt a little wasteful, sure, but the shame was fleeting because it was, like, really heavy.Needful Things is about a mysterious man who opens up a new store in a small town. He has a knack for knowing just what a person wants, a talent so well-honed that he'll surprise people with things they hadn't even realized they desired. The items are so bespoke that the buyers realize soon after they can't live without whatever item Gaunt sold them at his little shop. They become protective of it and suspicious of others. Within a week the town is crawling with Gollums, curled up in their homes, doors locked, curtains drawn, each cradling their own personal "precious." The items are so specific to each customer that most other people would look at them and see only junk — a lampshade, a foxtail, a fishing pole, a necklace, sunglasses, teapot, socket wrench set, board game, a beat up copy of a ubiquitous novel.The prices are so low that Gaunt is able to persuade customers to little favors for him in return. Pranks, he calls them — this is the real payment. None of these pranks are particularly friendly, and many of them are cruel, but for an autographed Sandy Kofax baseball card? Or the same fishing pole my dad used when I was a kid? Sure, I'll fling mud at those sheets. I'll slip a letter through that mail slot. Each prank is convincingly "signed" by another person who had nothing to do with it, but the town is whipped up into such a paranoid frenzy that no one even attempts to talk things out. Gaunt successfully brings out the worst in everyone he interacts with, pushing them fists first into their biggest adversaries."It was him, playing us off against each other, like he has just about everyone else in Castle Rock. Because that's what he does. ... It's not just your LIFE, can't you see? He makes you buy back your own sickness, and he makes you pay double! Don't you understand that yet? Don't you get it?"The writing is stellar as always. King's got a way with words and a talent for creating distinct, interesting characters. They all have their own histories and traumas, their own desires, their own reputations with everyone else in town. Kings takes his time introducing everyone and delicately weaves an incredibly long fuse between everyone's legs. I swore I felt Polly's arthritis (and not just because of the heavy book, lol).Right before the first deaths occurred I was sure something would intervene; sure they would come to their senses, or that good old Alan Pangborn would drive by and break up the fight. And when the fight was over I said, Goodness, is King doing a 'Salem's Lot here? A hundred pages later I said, oh wow, King's doing a 'Salem's Lot. I guess the answer was there all along, but I didn't realize the tagline on the cover, "The Last Castle Rock Story," was a double entendre. Sometimes the book is so small-town charming that you forget it's a King story until someone is literally leaving footprints in guts. At the very end I said, Holy cow is King doing an It?! He's doing an It! The way the antagonist is defeated, for better or worse, is trademark King.Two disorganized thoughts:1. King occasionally uses neat little narrative touches in his writing to set this-or-that book apart from the rest. Running Man's chapters numbers count in reverse. Half of Carrie's chapters are newspaper articles. The first several chapters of 'Salem's Lot are not numbered but, rather, titled as the time of day when that chapter occurs, giving you a tour of the whole town and introducing you to everyone just as they wake up and step out their front doors. Needful Things is bookended by chapters written from a second-person perspective, where the town gossip spots you, the reader, invites you to sit down, and strikes up a conversation. In a couple of pages, he'll tell you everything there is to know about the people of Castle Rock in conspiratorial whisper. Super neat idea, executed perfectly.2. The book was long. Adam Sandler has this joke about waking up too early where, as he tells it, he occasionally wakes up at 4:30 a.m., against his will, but decides to get out of bed and just start the day. Things start out great—he's making himself breakfast, watching the early news before the regular news, seeing the sun come up, meeting the fabled early bird, seeing the bus his kids take to school. It's a whole new world. But then by 11:00 a.m. he's screaming WHEN IS THIS FREAKING DAY GOING TO END?!I really enjoyed Needful Things but books this long I typically listen to on Audible. It was great to travel all around Castle Rock and get the feel for its layout and architecture. I could see the map in my head, could see the high school parking lot and imagine it relative to Brian Rusk's house, could see the blocks that connected that to Nettie Cobb's house and on and on to Wilma Jerzyck's backyard. The dozens of characters were perfectly rendered in my head. This isn't even his longest book (there are a dozen with higher page counts, most of which I've read), but right around the time Leland Gaunt turned his sign around to say Closed Until Further Notice I found myself internally screaming something similar to Sandler's punchline.Read moreRead less
