I had never even heard of Conrad Richter until I read David McCullough's account of the Sayward Luckett trilogy, yet this is better than any of the American novels that were considered classics in my younger days.  I've virtually forgotten everything written by Faulkner, Steinbeck and the rest of the literary lions of post-war America, but I can't imagine forgetting this amazing trilogy (don't read "The Town" until you've read "The Trees" and "The Fields").  Richter's style is simple, direct and powerful, and none of it feels the slightest bit contrived.  Richter exists completely outside the literary tradition-- no doubt this is why he has been so neglected by literary types, especially the post-modernists who sound so pleased with their stylistic tricks, yet really really have nothing interesting to say.  This book did win a Pulitzer prize and it enjoyed a modest success in its day, but I seriously doubt that you'll find it in many Lit Crit classes, even in American Universities.Before he wrote this trilogy, Richter spent a lot of time reading original papers left behind by the first settlers of Ohio, many of whom were poorly educated and wrote as ordinary people spoke.  Some of the usages and phrases obviously date back to pre-colonial days; they survive in Britain, but not in modern America.  When I first started reading "The Trees", it was a little hard to follow, but very quickly I understood that Richter was putting us right in his characters' shoes (if they had any).  There are no graphic passages containing sex or violence, although of course it is ever-present as a backdrop to the action.  Life was brutal enough without dwelling on such things.  Nonetheless, Richter clearly had a deep attachment to the simple ways of the settlers, and "The Town" is actually an elegy for a way of life that was passing.  When Sayward leaves the humble log cabin her father built, and the land she cleared with her own hands, something goes out of her life, even though by this time she is very wealthy and her husband has built a small mansion in the Town that grew up around her original clearing.  Although she insists on doing her own housework without hired help, she no longer grows her own crops and makes all her family's clothes from raw wool.  Even though her eldest son becomes Governor of Ohio and another son becomes a naval officer, you know she really feels more for the one who married an unfaithful wife and became a fugitive after killing his wife's lover.
