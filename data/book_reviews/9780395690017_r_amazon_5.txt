First, I am not a primatologist nor an evolutionary biologist, and I have not done a great deal of reading in those fields. So I can't presume to evaluate some of the claims that this book sets forth. That claim is that violence is programmed into the genes of male humans, just as it is programmed into the genes of SOME of the other great apes.The authors say that there are five families of great apes, and Homo sapiens is one of those families. The other four are the orang-utans, the gorillas, the chimpanzees, and the bonobos.The orang-utans do exhibit some violent behaviors such as rape, but these rapes are only committed by the smaller males, not the dominant large males. Gorillas apparently almost never rape anybody, but they do kill infant gorillas sometimes. The chimpanzees are quite violent, according to the authors, and they not only abuse female chimpanzees, but they also go on raids into the territories of other chimps, apparently with the intent of waging war and killing some of their "enemies."But what about the bonobos?  These great apes apparently do none of these things.  They have a peaceful, pan-sexual existence on the south side of the Zaire river, secluded from their meaner cousins the chimps and gorillas.For some reason, the authors seem to think that humans must be genetically more like chimps than like bonobos, because human males are often violent and war is endemic to most human society at present.  But to me it seems just as likely that humans could be genetically more similar to bonobos, and that the human male penchant for war and mayhem could be a cultural construct. After all, not all human societies are very violent. Some societies have glorified war and male dominance to the extent that most men in those societies identify maleness with violence, but in other societies, murder, rape and war are rare or absent. Not all human societies are patriarchal.  IT seems unlikely to me that violence is programmed into the genetic makeup of boys and men.We may never know what early humans were like in terms of their level of aggression, compared to other primates.  In a way it doesn't matter. Any rational person living today would say that less violence is better; less rape is better; and fewer wars would be better.  The question is how to limit aggression in humans.  The authors of this book posit that societies with strong central, state power as opposed to warlordism have less interpersonal violence; that a strong central authority such as a state can police and limit interpersonal violence. That is probably true, but history is replete with examples of strong states that go to war against each other.I am more persuaded by the authors' contention that powerful female alliances limit male violence in some primate and human societies.  Certainly a woman surrounded by her family allies is safer from interpersonal violence at the hands of men.
