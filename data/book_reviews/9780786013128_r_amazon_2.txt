If there is one relatively consistent failing in horror novels - particularly supernatural horror novels - it is that they often have poor endings.  I suppose it's part of the nature of the genre.  When you're dealing with vast supernatural forces challenging mere mortals, often times it's difficult to come up with a satisfying resolution.  The two sides are so unequal, you wind up with typically two alternatives:  either the evil forces win, which is no real twist since they had all the advantages anyway; or the "good" guys win, which often involves some poor twist that could level the playing field.  Not every horror novel has these problems, but almost all authors do suffer from it at least occasionally, even the big names like Stephen King and Dean Koontz.  Unfortunately, so does Bentley Little, and The Summoning, good as it often is, does fall apart a bit in the end.In The Summoning, Bentley Little has written a novel that is in some ways his most "traditional" horror story while at the same time retaining the elements that are distinctly his own.  It is a vampire novel that owes a lot to Stephen King's Salem's Lot in its depiction of the destruction of a small town by a malevolent bloodsucker.  Since it is Bentley Little, however, this vampire dwells in the hot arid environment of Arizona rather than King's more temperate New England.In the southwestern town of Rio Verde, a dead body is discovered with a bite in the neck and all the fluids drained out of it.  Near the corpse are the similar remains of a number of animals.  Meanwhile, Pastor Dan Wheeler starts getting visions of Jesus telling to build a special church; Wheeler's view of Christianity is a rather extreme version which will get worse as time goes by, getting very judgmental, sexist and racist.  For the reader, it is soon obvious that "Jesus" is the monster that is preying on the townspeople and is using Wheeler to promote its agenda; what's even more sinister is that it has imbued Wheeler with a dark charisma that is luring people onto his side.Opposing this evil is Robert Carter, the local police chief who is willing to resist the rumors of a vampire until it's obvious that there is no alternative.  There is also Rich, his brother who runs the local paper; Rich's wife, Corrie, has gotten a job with Wheeler and is falling under his influence.  Most important of the heroes is Sue Wing, a young woman of Chinese descent; she acts as the middlewoman between the Carters and her grandmother, who knows of the legends of the "cup hu girngsi" and remembers one from her own childhood.  Though the term may be of Chinese origin, the creature is similar to a vampire, but instead of being repelled by garlic and crosses, it fears jade and willow branches.The grandmother is sadly a rather cliched character, the wise old woman who seems to have all the necessary knowledge about the creature.  This is what leads to the weak ending when there is the final confrontation.  But until the conclusion, this is a really good novel.  Little creates a nice sense of menace with the vampire (or whatever it is) lurking in the background and rarely appearing before the end.
