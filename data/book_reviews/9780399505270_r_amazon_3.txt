While I much prefer Kawabata's Snow Country,  I found The Sound of the Mountain a very worthwhile read as well.  Kawabata is a master of the delicate detail,  the telling nuance.  This novel works on a number of levels.Most basically,  it is the tale of Ogata Shingo,  an aging businessman,  slowly losing his faculties and descending into old age and decrepitude.  He is not there yet,  as he struggles to deal with his scrappy, common sensical wife,  his depressed and quietly rebellious son Shuichi,  his divorced and unhappy daughter Fasuko and her bratty daughter Satoko,  and on the other end of the spectrum, his beautiful, dutiful,  half in love with him daughter in law,  Kikuko.  Even the dog Teru has a role to play as she efficiently brings five pups into the world who are eventually given away.  The action is quiet,  almost non-existent,  as the family eats meals,  worries about the cherry trees and other flora,  as well as fauna in the yard,  and learns to adjust to Fasuko's divorce and Kikuko and Shuichi's rocky start to their marriage.Shingo tries to deal with this with all the clarity that he can muster,  but he is far from perfect.  His own marriage is dogged by his constant harking back to the fact that he was originally attracted to his wife's much more attractive sister,  who unfortunately died.  Shingo in that and other things lacks sufficient clarity of thought and feeling.  He tries to apply the Confucian and other endemic principles imbued in him in a world that is changing before his eyes.  His own emotional make up causes difficulties,  as he is drawn to Kikuko,  at the same time that she is drawn to him emotionally,  but he is much too fine a man to become physically involved with his daughter in law  (as is she).  But the pull is always there and most delicately rendered by Kawabata.All of this is rendered against a backdrop of a nation, ethnic group and culture putting the shards of life back together after the crushing, humiliating defeat of World War II.  I have never seen any writing that gives one more insight into the visceral disappointment,  humiliation and dislocation that the outcome of the war visited upon the Japanese.As painful as it is,  we see the family struggling to adjust to the new ways and the new life they face in the 1950s.  As Shingo feels his own death and the death of old Japan approaching,  we see the following generation,  scarred by the war,  yet moving ahead into a less formal,  more down to earth future.All of this is rendered by a master of the telling detail.  The reader can sense the depth of Kikuko and Shingo's feelings for each other--yet in the 270 pages of this edition they never touch each other passionately or express other than the most respectful of father-daughter sentiment.  Yet the cauldron boils underneath.  For Kawabata a few strokes of the pen do what most writers cannot achieve in a lengthy chapter.The insights of a great artist,  showing us the inner life of a few individuals, yet exemplifying the forces driving an entire nation in a trying time.Read moreRead less
