In an effort to learn more about what really happened, I've been reading books about the crimes of Karla Homolka and Paul Bernardo.  I'd previously read Williams' earlier book, and this--his second book--gives significant new information about the case that wasn't in the earlier book or others that I've seen.  This book focuses more on the investigative and legal maneuverings surrounding the case.  As such, there is a bit less graphic detail on the crimes than in the earlier book--but still, I suspect, it will be highly triggering for many readers.  Much of the book explains the particulars of the controversial plea bargain that Karla was offered.  It does a very credible job of explaining how the plea bargain happened.  In Williams' view, timing was really key--and this book explains both why the plea deal happened and why it happened when it did.It also describes the evolution of Karla's case after she went to prison.  It was originally published in 2004, not long before her release in 2005, so by the time this book was published it was clear Karla would not be paroled as she'd hoped but would have to serve out her full sentence.  This book helps to explain the process by which Karla was denied parole--a denial that is apparently very rare in the Canadian justice system.There are two small weaknesses:  Williams explains why the deal was originally offered, but in my opinion he glosses over why the deal was later allowed to stand in the face of devastating new evidence that seemed to suggest that Karla lied and, hence, had not honored her side of the agreement.  And the last section of the book, where he seems to be trying to see how Karla measures up against truly despicable characters from earlier generations, adds little of value and also contains some serious factual errors--although those errors aren't relevant to Karla.Overall, though, people may not agree with Karla's plea bargain but, if so, they should try to understand why it happened.
