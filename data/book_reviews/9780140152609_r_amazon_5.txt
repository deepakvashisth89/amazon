I wanted to learn something about the life of Benjamin Franklin, so I looked on the internet for reviews of the many biographies of Franklin. Among the reviews I found no clear winner, but this book stood out as being a Pulitzer Prize winner. It is a long book, nearly 800 pages of text, so reading it looked daunting to me at first, but after having finished reading it, I can say it was well worth the time spent.Van Doren makes extensive use of quotations from Franklin's autobiography. At times the quotes are so seamlessly integrated with the author's own text that one has to look for the quotation marks to tell the difference. This style is used the most for the account of Franklin's early life, probably because there is so little material, apart from the autobiography, available on Franklin during this period.Clearly the author was impressed with Franklin's talents, and he let the reader know this in a reasonable way. Thankfully, he refrained from making direct value judgments for specific actions using today's standards. For example, while holding political office, Franklin intermingled his personal business dealings with those of the government in ways that would probably be considered criminal today: receiving contracts for government work, getting political appointments for his relatives, accepting emoluments from foreign governments, etc. Mores, particularly for politicians, change over time, and what was acceptable in the 18th century would not be in the 20th century.Franklin was probably unique in that he had significant involvement with government before, during, and after the American Revolution, so the details of his life, captured nicely by Van Doren, give a very good record of American history during this important period. Franklin signed both the Declaration of Independence (at 71 years of age) and the U. S. Constitution (at 81 years of age).If you decide to purchase this book, I recommend the American Past, Book-of-the-Month Club edition from 1980, which is often available used.
