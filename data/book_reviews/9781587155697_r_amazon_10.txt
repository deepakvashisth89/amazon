(To avoid the usual Amazon mix-and-match confusion when it comes to comments and the items to which they refer: this small review is of the "Everyman's Library" edition of Erich Raspe's "The Surprising Adventures of Baron Munchausen [Hardcover]," seen on the following page at the time of the comment's posting: http://www.amazon.ca/Surprising-Adventures-Baron-Munchausen/dp/0307961478/ref=sr_1_1?ie=UTF8&qid=1389140321&sr=8-1&keywords=The+Surprising+Adventures+of+Baron+Munchausen+%5BHardcover%5D)The work itself is a classic, funny as can be in the best tradition of tall tales as only Hieronymus Carl Friedrich von Münchhausen can tell. Or, well, Raspe, the writer himself, by proxy, for deciding his (anti-) hero ought to be named after that real-world counterpart. Arguably, the name alone might qualify as a 'tall tale.'. And, as the real-world story goes, Raspe himself was no stranger to tall tales (of a 'grifter' bent) -- he fled for his safety after attempting to earn some capital by 'inventive prospecting,' capitalising on a very literal interpretation of 'rich land,' on which he himself planted some gems (which may or may not have been fake themselves, one would assume in concordance with one's own level of gullibility or scepticism). In the end, though, all that has faded (if not, it would only add to the intrigue), and this book is Raspe's true and lasting legacy.As to the edition: beautifully hardbound nice cover and spine, sturdy and well-made as well as aesthetically pleasing. You won't have to fight the spine to keep the book open, and it won't lay half open like a dishevelled creature of the night when laying it aside.Pages sawn, the best quality paper (in terms of weight/thickness, reflectance/glare, tint/brightness) and typeface one can hope for, which makes for a pleasurable read and adds to that experience, rather than distracting from it.And the cherry on top of this tall cake: plentiful Gustave Doré illustrations, with Doré at his humorous best adding a sprinkling of grotesque which his sense of humour always keeps on the funny side. He himself must have greatly enjoyed the book, because his delight in this particular commission, in being tasked to illustrate it, is very obvious. He had more than a laugh and a mirthful great time reading the book, same as millions of readers have since the book was first published, then translated into many languages across the globe. And if you haven't yet, now so can you, by reading it in the indispensable accompaniment of Doré's illustrations -- a fortuitous meeting of minds with humorous exaggerations from both inventive, imaginative creators complimenting each other in perfect harmony. As illustrator, Doré had to do the synchronising of spirit and the self-adapting, while Raspe did the inspiring through his writing (since Raspe's text preceded Doré's images), but Doré did such a good job of it, writer and illustrator both seem to feed off each other's ideas to make this book of often fantastical and surrealist humour that much more funny, enjoyable.A little gem of literature that inspired more than one film-maker (none able to capture all the Baron's tall tales in all their exaggerated glory and extravagant majesty, obviously -- for technical reasons as well as the usual time and budget constraints associated with movie-making in general, and with expressing wild and improbable ideas and impossible scenes and deeds on screen in particular: parts of the story are changed to make them less expensive, others to save time, and many are dropped altogether). It also inspired many writers. And by now, it is probably ignored by many readers, for various reasons. In which case, this review was written for a sole purpose, and I took time and put some thought and care into it for one reason alone: to do it some small measure of justice by confirming the fact and letting readers know -- or reminding them, as the case may be -- that it is well worth their time (and the surprisingly small price) of the (re-) discovery.Read moreRead less
