PKD’s Deus Irae, God of Wrath – a play on Dies Irae [Day of Wrath or Judgement Day], was first published in 1976 and still reads well today. The post-apocalyptic and dystopian vision of the future has changed little since then. As with most of these genres Deus Irae speaks more to its own time and the fears of this time than it does of a plausible future.The tedious thing about these end times books is that you have three choices:1. The world is in the process of ending [such as Cormac McCarthy’s bleak reading in ‘The Road’]2. The world has been through a tough time but will recover [Deus Irae].3. The world faces an uncertain future [perhaps Walter M. Miller’s A Canticle for Leibowitz]They are tedious, while at the same time being compelling, because the choices available appear to limit character and the possibilities of interactions between characters. The possibilities are limited because there is a lack of faith in the future. To a greater or lesser extent, all social relationships occurring over time are about the future. These needn’t, necessarily, be about the future of the two characters involved but they have to be helping, or using, one another to achieve a future goal.Where there is no future, or where this future is seen as stunted or limited, the interactions of the characters are limited—almost atrophied. With the reification of character comes a necessarily archetypal construction, which, unless handled carefully, may tumble into cliché. This is what has happened in Deus Irae.Everyone here is a stock character fulfilling set goals that can be found in many other books of this kind. Although PKD handles these clichés well, he still must work the plot out through them. This leaves the end of the book in little doubt; therefore there is little by way of dramatic tension in the read.Having said this, the book, though a minor work, is still interesting and an enjoyable read. Even if not central PKD, Deus Irae will be an enjoyable read for those who have made it through the major works and still want more PKD.The fantastic creatures created by world war 3 and its fallout are fun—the worm especially. Also, the remnant tech from the pre-war period is amusing as it is slowly failing and in failing manages to kill even more people in spectacularly bizarre ways that will allow foes of capitalism to nod sagely at.If there is an ultimate goal in Deus Irae it is to satirize, or lampoon—lampoon may be closer to the mark, the consumerism and, supposedly, the late-capitalism of the ‘70s. This reads a bit dated here, but it is an interesting reading of the time in which it was written and useful, now, as a window on a world that is already beginning to vanish from living memory. Therefore, as a cultural artefact Deus Irae may yet have many decades or centuries of value in it.In the end, it was an enjoyable, if predictable, read.Rating 4 out of 5 stars.Read moreRead less
