Seize the Day takes place over several hours in the life of its protagonist, Tommy Wilhelm.  It’s a significant day in Wilhelm’s life, as he’s a failed actor who recently lost his traveling sales job, he’s separated from his wife and kids, he’s on the outs with his girlfriend, and he’s just invested his last remaining savings in the commodities market under the guidance of his friend, a Dr. Tamkin.When Robert Louis Stevenson wrote Treasure Island, his nephew asked him to write a book “with no girls in it,” and other than a brief appearance of the narrator’s mother, Stevenson obliged.  Saul Bellow must have had a similar request; in Seize the Day, no women appear directly other than a phone conversation Wilhelm has with his estranged wife.  The milieu, New York City’s Broadway in the decade after the Second World War, should feel familiar to any modern reader from Gen Alpha to older Millennials, who live in a world dominated by powerful old men.  Wilhelm lives in this world as well; as the youngest denizen of a residential hotel for the elderly, he’s surrounded by wealthy geezers with no concerns other than their failing health.  None of them appear to have families, either—they’re completely carefree.  Dr. Tamkin, despite being close to Wilhelm’s age, is the same type; as far as we can tell, he’s independently wealthy, and without a family.Tamkin is also a grifter, who plays on Wilhelm’s need for guidance and approval, none of which he gets from his father, also a physician and successfully retired, and unwilling to provide Wilhelm with the financial assistance and emotional connection he desperately needs.  In a flashback, Wilhelm meets with a movie industry talent scout who claims he’s “not an agent,” although it’s unclear what the guy does as his job involves connecting actors with acting jobs and presumably representing them.  Everything about this man exudes desperation and phoniness; even his name, Maurice Venice, is questionable as it’s unclear if it’s supposed to rhyme or not—is Maurice pronounced like Morris, which would match the conventional pronunciation of Venice, or is Venice pronounced like the conventional version of Maurice with the stress on the second syllable?  We quickly learn that this man is a fraud, the unsuccessful scion of a powerful movie clan, whose recommendation is “the kiss of death.”Just as there are books that serve as object lessons in sin and redemption from a Christian perspective, Seize the Day could be an object lesson in a Buddhist one.  Wilhelm’s problem is that he is in a perpetual state of desire, but is unable to achieve fulfillment.  He needs a job, but is unwilling to “crawl” back to his former employer.  He wants to be a husband and father to his sons, but his wife won’t let him come home.  He wants to be a good son, but nothing he does is good enough for his father.  And above all, he wants money, while observing that the old men around him who have more money than they need, don’t seem to care about it.  Wilhelm cares, so he doesn’t have it.  At the same time, he recognizes the rat race and is contemptuous of it.On a stylistic level, Bellow delights in describing peoples’ physical features, mostly unattractive.  Venice is “huge and oxlike, so stout that his arms seemed caught from beneath in a grip of flesh and fat.” Of a Mr. Perls, Wilhelm wonders  “how can a human face get into this condition” and later notices that Perls “dragged a large, built-up shoe” because he has “a bone condition that is gradually breaking him up.”  A Mr. Rappaport is “half-blind” and has a “big but light elbow.”  Wilhelm himself is described in great detail.  None of the women who appear in the outer margins of the book are described at all except Venice’s fiance, “a bathing beauty—short, the usual breasts, hips, and smooth thighs.”I first heard of Bellow in college, when someone on the staff of a magazine I edited wrote a review of Eusebio Rodrigues’ “Quest for the Human: An Exploration of Saul Bellow’s Fiction.”  Filtered through Rodrigues and the staff writer, who may or may not have actually read anything by Bellow himself—Bellow’s writing sounded turgid, overblown, and self-important.  So I was pleasantly surprised when I was assigned Seize the Day as part of the curriculum for a class in 20th century American literature and thoroughly enjoyed it, so much so that I read several of Bellow’s other novels on my own.  The reason I returned to Seize the Day after 40 years was because I read an article on the recent revelations of Bellow’s repeated beating of his wife Sondra and his refusal to believe her accounts of sexual abuse by her own father, the painter Nahum Tschacbasov.  When I first read Bellow, I didn’t notice the lack of female characters or his utter failure of the Bechdel Test, but now, I can’t help but wonder if this was a reflection of a fundamental contempt for women on his part.  Bellow himself had been beaten by his own father; it’s not out of line to venture into psychology and propose that he may have blamed his mother, and by extension all women, for failing to protect him.  Or, maybe he was just a jerk, like some gifted artists are.We don’t read Bellow for memorable female characters, however; we read him for his treatment of conflicted men—men like Tommy Wilhelm, whose tragedy is that the more he desires, the less he has.  The book’s ending, where Wilhelm finally achieves fulfillment after having lost everything, reminds me of the ending of Hermann Hesse’s “Siddhartha.”“Govinda bowed low. Uncontrollable tears trickled down his old face. He was overwhelmed by a feeling of great love, of the most humble veneration. He bowed low, right down to the ground, in front of the man sitting there motionless, whose smile reminded him of everything that he had ever loved in his life, of everything that had ever been of value and holy in his life.”Siddhartha is an appealing character because we see him as something to which we should aspire, while Tommy Wilhelm is an object lesson in what to avoid, even as we recognize something of ourselves in him.Read moreRead less
