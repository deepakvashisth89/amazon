On the plus side, this is a great story: it starts with a really interesting premise, explores its implications, and follows a fast-paced sequence of events to a satisfying conclusion. The author is at his best when describing the physics of space flight: although some of the space travel in this book is hypothetical (involving worm holes or "foldpoints" as they are called), there is also a great deal of conventional flight, with parking orbits, acceleration, deceleration, good old fashioned "delta V" (change in velocity). If you find the details of this more satisfying than watching Star Wars or Star Trek spaceships swooping around and coming to instant stops, then you will love this book. There is also a good deal about supernovas and stellar evolution. For all these reasons, the book can rightly be put in the sub-genre of hard science fiction.But oh my god, the dialogues are awful! Most of the characters come across as unbelievably stupid at some point or other, as someone is explaining something to them, and they fail to grasp its significance long after it's obvious to the reader. I lost track of how many times someone says, "I don't understand" or "What is your point?" When characters are trying to engage in subtle political maneuvering or negotiating, it always comes across as clumsy and childish. And related to this, they sound like they all came straight out of a old Western movie: the men are strong, gentlemanly, wholesome cowboy-types; when women try to assert themselves, playful chauvinistic banter usually ensues. I kept checking the publication date to see if this was really written in 1986, because it sounded a lot more like the 1940s or 50s. I almost wish the author had sketched out the plot and then given it to someone else to write: instead of all the cheesy dialogue, this story deserved a tone with more grandeur and mystery.Anyway, it's very worth reading; I will probably read the two sequels sometime soon.Read moreRead less
