This is my first Saul Bellow novel and it will not be my last.  He is truly in the top class of literary authors.  This is a literary book about, as much as anything else, literature and is packed with erudite references and allusions to other works and writers throughout.  However, this is managed without being ponderous or pretentious.  The prose throughout is beautiful whilst managing to remain entertaining and rugged.  I had the curious experience of picking it up after just having heard an Audio book of Keats poetry and somehow my brain continued superposing the Keatsian metre onto Bellow's prose, and it worked with a remarkable naturalness.So it's beautiful prose as one would expect from a Nobel winner.  It is also very humorous.  There were moments early on, when I was getting used to the style, that I thought I was verging onto heart attack territory, unable as I was to control my laughter.  But the book is also deeply humane.  The plot involves a cast of extremely colourful characters, all marvellously realised.  Even the less savoury characters, seen through the the eyes of Charlie Citrine, the novel's central protagonist, are portrayed with love and compassion, even as their foibles are mercilessly deconstructed.So the book is beautifully written, very funny but humane.  So what is it about?  At the highest level it's about anything and everything pertaining to the human condition, bringing to bear multiple perspectives from the history of thought.  Zooming in closer, it is an examination of the role of an artist, who happens to be a humanitarian, in mid-twentieth century America, at a time when capitalism is stepping up a gear, and when post-war optimism is just about to turn sour.  At another level still the novel examines an artist for whom inadvertant material success has bought about a set of relationships which are essentially exploitative of him. These have arisen from over-generosity and naivity.  We are examining a point in this man's life where he is obliged to dismantle these relationships.  In one sense his life is in meltdown, in another he is arriving at a point of liberation and a deepening spiritual perspective on himself and his fellows. One arrives at the end of the book with a curious admixture of feelings that I will not attempt to analyse to avoid ruining the experience for others.  Just to say, this end state is sufficiently emotionally complex that it has taken me some days since completion to really figure out what the book has meant for me as a reader.As a word of warning, Citrine's attempts to interpret the manic events around him in terms of the mystical tradition of Rudolf Steiner's anthroposophy, whilst being handled with humour and irony, might make the book seem 'dated' or uncomfortable to those of the more militantly atheistic mindset, that has become fashionable in the decades since the book was written.  However, this aspect does provide a dimension for the examination of the place of mysticism and religion within the human condition melting pot, and is handled with a wisdom and charm that manages to be both reverent and irreverent.So, this is a great American novel from a great American novelist, containing, as a true work of art should, many strands that cannot be easily summarised, except to say that an immense heart of goodness, humanity and humour beats beneath the whole enterprise.Read moreRead less
