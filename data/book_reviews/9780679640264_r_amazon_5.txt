Native Californian Joan Didion left her home state after graduating from the University of California at Berkley in the mid-sixties to become a writer in New York. While working first as a copy editor and later as an associate features editor at Vogue, Didion wrote many insightful freelance stories about several arcane subjects in which she often interjected herself to unearth the story's essence. I still recall reading several of her pieces during the nineteen sixties in the Saturday Evening Post that made me, a hapless small town teenager, want to flee to New York City and California ex post haste. Certainly, her most compelling essay in this essay collection is the book's title piece, "Slouching Towards Bethlehem," taken from the William Butler Yeats classic poem about anarchy being loosed upon the world as a hideous beat slouches toward the Christ child's cradle, presumably to devour the innocent babe and extinguish mankind's hope for salvation. As with other of Didion's early work, this story, too, appeared in the Post. Published in 1967, the writing provides as good as any expose I've ever read about San Francisco's "Summer of Love" with its ongoing indulging by run-away teens in a potpourri of psychedelic drugs; "free Love," including gang rape; and hippies hanging out in Haight Ashbury Park listening to free concerts by such groups as the Grateful Dead. In short, this long ago summer, despite the much ballyhooed braggadocio by duplicitous culture critics, destroyed many young lives and would have dragged into this morass a less-savvy writers than Didion was and has been throughout her long, successful career.
