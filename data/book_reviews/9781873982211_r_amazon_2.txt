This book was published in 1994 and contained 11 works by as many authors: six from Germany and five from Austria-Hungary. There were six excerpts from novels and five short stories. All but two of the pieces ranged from 1890 to 1913; the exceptions were extracts from Venus in Furs, published by Leopold von Sacher-Masoch in 1869, and Blaugast: A Novel from Old Prague, written by Paul Leppin in the early 1930s and published posthumously in 1948.The oldest author was Sacher-Masoch (1836-1895), who was described as obliquely related to decadence, since his work predated the others by several decades. The youngest writers were Georg Heym (1887-1912) and Georg Trakl (1887-1914), others included Hanns Ewers and early Thomas Mann.An introduction by the editor, Ray Furness, defined literary decadents as focused on inner visions, reacting against naturalism. They drew morbid sensations from contemplating dissolution, and preferred the artificial and unnatural, with a tendency toward sterile estheticism. They flirted with cruelty for the sake of stimulation, and dabbled in feverish mysticism or immorality with satanic overtones. “But a drop of poison," said the editor, "can improve the health of an organism, just as an exotic spicing can improve the taste of the blandest offering.” The intro passed over the question of why so many of the authors’ portrayed woman as an intoxicating destroyer of man.The editor briefly surveyed German-language literary decadence from the late 1800s into the early 1900s. Quickly described were some forerunners (Poe, Baudelaire) as well as a critic who helped transmit styles and ideas from Paris to Vienna (Hermann Bahr); for the German-language writers, other important cities included Munich, Prague and Berlin. A link was made to other arts (the operas of Richard Wagner) and critics of decadence were noted (Max Nordau, Friedrich Nietzsche). There was no space for a discussion of the relation of decadence to expressionism -- where does one end and the other begin?For this reader, the standouts in the collection were “The Blood of the Wälsungs” by Mann, written in 1906 but not published until 1921; the extract from Blaugast by Leppin; the extract from Alraune (1911) by Hanns Ewers; and “The Post-Mortem” by Georg Heym, published in 1913. Mann’s story was astonishing, managing to combine dark humor with irony, ominous parallels between the twin characters and the Wagner opera they attended, and racist caricatures. (The anthology contained his toned-down ending from 1921, rather than the 1906 original.)Blaugast by Leppin described convincingly a weak man’s spiral into sexual obsession and degradation. Alraune by Ewers followed a mysterious girl’s path into adulthood, in the course of which those around her suffered and died. These two works were compelling and readable even though they were extracts. The story by Heym followed the thoughts of a corpse on a table, ending with a vestige of love for another, as if this emotion was strong enough to overcome death and decay. Sacher-Masoch’s writing was surprisingly clear, with some narrative drive and a clear-headed discussion of hedonism and the search for control over another person -- though it tended to repetition when the main character relived his fixations.For this reader, the rest of the works contained much less of interest (Bahr, Arthur Holitscher, Peter Hille, Stanislaus Przybyszewski, Kurt Martens). Amounting to nearly half the collection, they tended toward florid description, emotional outbursts and a pileup of cluttered detail that lacked narrative force. The piece by Przybyszewski, included in full, was virtually unreadable. These authors seemed the weakest of those included, and for me their selections showed the drawbacks of decadent writing. Still, the book was worth reading for the other works it contained.A few tales resembling some of the better pieces can be found in anthologies of wider scope like the 2003 Dedalus Book of Austrian Fantasy 1890-2000 (“The Master” by Meyrink, “The Kiss of the Stone Woman” by Csokor) and the 2012 Tales of the German Imagination (“The Seamstress,” an early tale by Rilke, and “The Madman” by Heym).Read moreRead less
