In Virginia Woolf’s short story, “Mrs. Dalloway in Bond Street,” we are introduced to Mrs. Clarissa Dalloway, a member of the English upper class, as she wanders through London to purchase a pair of French gloves (Woolf later developed the story into a novel, Mrs. Dalloway, which was written in 1925, the same year Fitzgerald wrote The Great Gatsby). The short story gives the appearance of a portrait, as it disdains any semblance of a plot structure. The character of Mrs. Dalloway is revealed through her actions at a glove shop. Mrs. Dalloway momentarily considers offering her country home to the shop-girl for her vacation, but quickly changes her mind, remembering the admonition of her husband had “shown her the folly and giving impulsively,” and rationalizing that “the girl wouldn’t like to be given things” (Woolf 9). A moment later, a mysterious explosion rocks the shop. The shop-girls instinctively “cowered behind the counters,” but Mrs. Dalloway and another patron remain “upright,” unflummoxed, and apparently displaying the resolute behavior refined through the grace of nobility (Woolf 10).While many stories and novels can be interpreted through many different literary approaches, in my opinion, this story seems difficult to interpret without the lens of a Marxist literary approach. One of the central ideas in Marxist criticism surrounds the way in which an author either applauds or subverts the dominant political system. According to Rivkin and Ryan, a Marxist approach sees a literary work “functions as a mechanism to reproduce the class structure of society,” “or as a “weapon in class struggle” (644). Mrs. Galloway, as a member of the British upper class, reveals herself to be conscious of her role in high society. Not only is it important for her to obtain the material trappings of her society position (the gloves), but more importantly, her role in society is revealed in her choices. She chooses to deny her human impulse to help the shop-girl enjoy a vacation, and resists the human temptation to seek a protective posture when she hears the explosion. Further, instead of the natural reaction to express concern for those that might have been harmed by the “disturbance” in the last moment of the story, Mrs. Galloway recognizes the identity of the patroness in the shop. Mrs. Galloway isn’t a destructive person like Tom Buchanan. However, Woolf nevertheless portrays her as a woman that has suppressed her humanity:  suppressed the natural instinct to be kind to others, and even the natural instinct to protect oneself.Marx noted that “the individuals composing the ruling class possess among other things consciousness” and that they “regulate the production and distribution of the ideas of their age” (656). In Woolf’s story, the idea of class consciousness is evident in the thoughts and actions of Mrs. Dalloway. However, the shop-girl also reveals her knowledge of her status as a member of the working class. When Mrs. Dalloway asks her if she gets tired from standing all day and when she gets her vacation, she replies that she takes her holiday “in September, when we’re not so busy” (Woolf 9). She does not even answer the question about being tired. In the response and the silence, the shop-girl, reveals her awareness that her needs and desires must be subverted to the needs and desires of the class of which she serves. As a member of the working class, she must postpone her work break until the needs of the shop owner and those with the money that can afford the luxurious items found within, such as French gloves lined with pearl buttons, have been fulfilled.Although the portrait of the upper class rendered by Woolf is much more subtle than the one painted by Fitzgerald, in the end it is unflattering and shows the need for social change.
