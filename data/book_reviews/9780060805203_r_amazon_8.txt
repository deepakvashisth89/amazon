Nicholas Blake’s first crime novel appeared in 1935.  His style is more character-oriented than plot-oriented, which makes his novels more modern than Christie’s or the ‘locked room” mysteries of the 1930s.  This novel features the amateur sleuth Nigel Strangeways, as do most of Blake’s crime novels.  Strangeways makes a decent hero.  He doesn’t have a lot of personality but he’s not annoying.  Like many British crime heroes, Strangeways quotes poetry a bit too often.  This is to be expected though since Nicholas Blake was the pseudonym for British Poet Laureate Cecil Day Lewis.  This novel came out in 1966.  Our hero is visiting an American Ivy League school, modeled on Harvard, where Lewis taught in 1962 and 1963.  The focus is on the characters at the beginning and they are well drawn.  This was the best part of the book and I didn’t mind that the murder occurred later than usual for mystery novels.  The strength of the murder investigation part is that Strangeways is a friend of all the suspects so he is able to gather information in casual conversations.  We don’t get the formal police interview routine in which every suspect holds something back so they can reveal it in a second interview.  I became annoyed by the frequent generalizations about Americans by Strangeways and an Irish character.  These kind of observations about the USA had become British clichés long before 1966.  The plot is a bit thin but Lewis pads it out well with his ability at characterization.
