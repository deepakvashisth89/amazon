Of course, I had heard of Anais Nin and her erotica, but I had never read any of her books. I bought the smear campaign against her writing being mere pornography. Now that I am reading her essays, I am learning how intelligent and well read she was. Anais Nin never called herself a feminist. While she believed in women getting the right to vote, having equal civil rights like their male counterparts, and the right to bodily autonomy, she was put off by the politicization of women's rights. As a longtime advocate of free speech, she also found some of the feminism's criticism of male sexuality and pornography to be the dregs of Puritanism that would restrict women's sexual freedom.  Her decades-long psychoanalyses convinced her that true liberation came from deep self-exploration that would heal the emotional wounds of one's life "...that have prevented woman from her fullest evolution and flowering.  I have studied the negative influence of religion, of racial and cultural patterns, which action alone and no political slogans can dissolve." (p. 27) She wrote that her famous (or to her critics, infamous) diaries were her judgement-free outlets and that it was a good exercise for women who wanted more self-fulfillment.  However, she is adamant that this is just the first step and women are free to be politically active if they so choose.While she complained about feminists trying to emulate men at the expense of their natural femininity, Anais was far from a shrinking violet.  She firmly believed in Jungian androgyny, that men had a feminine side ("Anima") and women had a masculine side ("Animus"). When in the 1940's American publishers refused to publish her books, "Winter of Artifice" and "Under a Glass Bell",  because they were "uncommercial", she took to self-publishing. She received loans from friends to buy a second-hand printing press, type, and scrapped paper and she learned how to set type. She possessed amazing vision and fortitude for a woman of that era.
