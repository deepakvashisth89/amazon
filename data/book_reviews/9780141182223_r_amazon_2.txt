Taking place exclusively in the eponymous dwelling, CG is a long-winded and by turns creepy and satirical yarn told primarily through the eyes and thoughts of a psychologically, and literally, trapped woman. The book's arc, such as it is (Gaddis favors page-long soliloquy over driving narrative action) concerns said woman dealing with the rigors of living with an abusive, alcoholic husband whose ties to a wayward reverend have gotten him into a mess of trouble. Also worth noting are the woman's interactions with her new home's original owner, a mysterious figure who pops in and out to deliver cryptic messages, smoke incessantly, and rifle through an unsettling amount of ancient documents in an upstairs study. The woman, our hero, is quickly at her wits-end and is pushed even further into the all-consuming darkness of America past and present, in which themes of bigotry, sexual tension, addiction and other general nastiness come to the fore. Imagine, if you will, a very literary (and talky) version of the recent American Horror Story (season one, natch) and you'll have a pretty good idea of the goings-on in Gaddis' house of horrors. Linguistically, the book is a raw and circuitous machine, wherein phrases like 'paper tore' are repeated ad naseum, creating a patchwork effect that adds to the isolation and claustrophobia of the titular house and its effect on its captives. It is not only physically ensnaring, this locale, but verbally so as well, creating a sense of narrative dread within a narrative absent of physical push toward a satisfying climax. That is not to say things end without a certain level of change on display, which is all I'll reveal.
