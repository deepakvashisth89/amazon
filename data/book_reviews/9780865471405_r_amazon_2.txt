The book discussion group met at The LGBT Center in NYC in February 2016 to discuss "Totempole," the "re-discovered" pre-Stonewall novel from 1965. It was a small group and everyone liked the book but the two Jewish members of the group loved it. Maybe it has some special appeal to New Yorkers who have a connection to an older New York?The novel "Totempole" and the narrator's life are both divided into two parts: youth, followed by the narrative in the Korean prisoner of war camp. The  "young" chapters cover Stephen's life as a child at the beach with his bickering parents, a grade-schooler at camp where he gets a crush on a counselor, a high-schooler who becomes neurotic about masturbating, and then a college student in the drama department, when he experiments with heterosexuality and freaks out about his homosexuality. The last "Korean prisoner of war camp" chapter is the longest section and the most extended narrative that revolves around his affair with a Korean prison doctor being kept in the camp, but seemingly leads him to a healthy gay life. Overall, the tone novel is chilly or distant. I thought it was cerebral and stilted at times, but the story itself is fascinating.Each of the chapters is titled after a major animal in the section: horsie, ocean (OK not an animal, but alive), salamander, loon, moose, monkeys, lice, and rats (in Korea). Friedman builds the novel like the totem pole that Stephen helps to build at camp. Each section clearly stands apart from the other sections but builds to a single structure with a common purpose, both the physical totem pole and the novel have a life of its own. It's interesting to note that Friedman entitles one of the camp chapters "Loon," which is a popular gay theme. The famous mystic-pastoral-erotic novel (and later popular porn movie) "The Song of the Loon" by Richard Amory appeared in 1966. Loons have a mysterious, nocturnal appeal in folk lore. Lice (aka "crabs"), unfortunately, are also a common but undesirable theme of gay life.Some of the psychology of the book is a bit old fashioned and poorly explained. It was written during a period when Freudianism was on the rise and Stephen's move from oral to anal is clear. The novel handles childhood sexuality as a Freudian commonplace. As a teenager, Stephen has a completely unnecessary appendectomy, which serves as a kind of castration complex, leaving him with a scar he finds disfiguring and imagining himself to have an embarrassingly small member. All pretty standard stuff. But the S&M sequence at camp is poorly explained in terms of Stephen's growth. And at several points in the novel, Stephen seems awfully slow to catch on to things, especially since he's supposed to be so smart.The politics in the final Korean section is also rather heavy handed at times, both in describing the non-election of Adlai Stevenson back in the United States and the current "conflict" between the communist North and anti-communist South Korea.The novel isn't especially literary, but uses a number of techniques that "literary authors" use, such as first-person point of view and vocabulary (especially in the first "very young" chapters), references to classic mythology, ideas from other books (including Freud), and related movie plots. Friedman also uses smells to set the mood and involve all the senses, such as horses and manure, as well as dad's cigar and mom's gardenia in the early chapters; and then exotic smells in Korea: kimchee, incense, stagnant water, smoke in the tents, spices in the markets, and then clear mountain air.In high school, Stephen adopts "Oscar II," a small toy monkey that he carries around in his front pants pocket. I think that this totem serves as a symbol of his repressed homosexuality. He hides it away near his dick and fondles it as a substitute for real connection and sex. But once he gives himself to the Sun Bo, the Korean doctor in the prison camp, he also gives the monkey away; he doesn't need it any more.And I should say something about the sex between Stephen and Sun Bo. This is a pre-liberation novel but the sex is pretty hot. It's accurate and explicit (neither of which was often the case in novels before "The Joy of Sex"). It's the first written example that I know to clearly describe rimming (and it's a pleasure!) and to uncover the joys of anal sex. While Friedman may be accused by modern readers of exoticizing Korean men (so many hot Korean men in prison!) and especially his Korean lover, it's not a serious problem and allows Stephen to grow and become happy.That's the real revelation of this book. At the end Stephen is on his way to becoming a healthy, happy homosexual. He's gotten rid of his repression (by getting screwed once! how convenient!!) and doesn't consider settling down with a wife, becoming an alcoholic, turning into a bitter queen, or committing suicide, all of which were the common recourses to gayness in the 1960's and earlier. So it's an interesting book, a bit dated, but worth reading for its ending that points toward healthy modern life for both Stephen and men in general.Note: Peter Cameron's "Afterward" is everything that an "afterward" should be.
