I know that this is going to be a very self-indulgent “product review”, but I’m going to start with my personal point of view in reading this book.I don’t consider myself to be a disbeliever, per say, but at this stage in my life I’m also not a religious person or a churchgoer.  However, I find the topic of biblical history to be incredibly fascinating.  It seems to me that, even for those that don’t believe in God, there is a wealth to be learned from studying what Christians regard as the “Old Testament”.I find that disbelievers or anti-religious persons (I know many personally) are too quickly dismissive of the contents of the Bible.  If you strip away the mysticism of it, the books of the Torah should still be regarded as the oldest historical document of its kind.  We know with certainty that historical figures like King David, or Solomon, were definitely real people whom existed.  So to instantly cast the Bible as fiction without learning about it is a mistake.  And, while the story may have evolved into a myth as it was passed down from antiquity through oral tradition, there probably was a great flood in the Middle East at some point which inspired the flood myth in Genesis (and flood myths of other civilizations).What I’m getting at is that there is great value in studying our shared history (much less can be gained from grousing it away dismissively).  For many of us that shared history begins in the Middle East and in The Holy Bible.I’ve been aware of the Richard Friedman book “Who Wrote the Bible?” for some time, and it finally made it to the top of my reading list.  It first came to my attention when reading the excellent Straight Dope newspaper article of the same subject (“Who Wrote the Bible?”).  As an aside, if you just want the short answer, simply Googling that newspaper column will be a good start.  I read the column when I was younger, returned to it recently, and was inspired to add the book to my reading list.  As I often do, I purchased both the Kindle version and a physical copy of the book (I prefer to make notes and highlights in my Kindle but to read paper).I found the book to be extremely well-written.  Often, it can become frustrating when someone writes too much about themselves (this product review is probably an example).  In the case of Friedman’s writing in “Who Wrote the Bible?” I instead found the occasional reference to his life and his connection to theology to be very interesting.After starting with a bit of history in the exploration of its base question, “Who Wrote the Bible?” explores what Friedman delicately discusses as the present “scholarly” view.  This is, in short, that the early chapters of the Bible were written, primarily, by 4-5 individuals (or groups) and then compiled into one volume by at least one other individual.  This is oversimplifying the scholarly view, but that is the basic concept in one sentence; read the book for details!  Friedman gets into explaining what we know about each author, along with some exploration of whom they may have been.I did feel like the book seemed to delve sometimes a little too much into conjecture or simple speculation in trying to identify the authors of the Bible.  That’s not necessarily a complaint, or a thought that something else should have been done (after all, we don’t know who the authors were).  My feedback is simply to state that some portions which were written as “big reveals” were left to feel a little anti-climactic.In some cases, I found the writing itself to be overwhelming.  This isn’t necessarily Richard Friedman’s fault.  I felt that some portions of the book moved too quickly, with too many referenced names, events, etc. for someone that hasn’t read the Bible (confessedly, me).  Obviously, my personal starting point of view isn’t Friedman’s fault.  But, while I read most of the book very attentively, I also found myself skimming in a couple of portions.“Who Wrote the Bible?” was well worth the read.  It has inspired me to add several other books on early religion to my reading list.  I’ve also started reading the first two books of The Bible itself (Genesis and Exodus), with the intention of then moving into one of Richard Friedman’s other works, “The Exodus”.
