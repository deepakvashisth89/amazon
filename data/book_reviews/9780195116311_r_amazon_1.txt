I picked up this book as foil to Allan Pinkerton's *The Molly Maguires and the Detectives,* having found my grandmom's notorious great-granddad a recurrent character in Pinkerton's derisive narrative.  He'd fled arrest in the 1870s, leaving behind a wife and five children; he became a silver miner in Colorado and was murdered for his discovery of a rich deposit of silver by an antagonistic, nativist bully who'd made his life hard for some while.  In Colorado, it does seem that this ancestor of mine was either followed by--or carried his own--reputation for having murdered three men, so I am enjoying the detective work of trying to figure which were more likely his doing.  For anyone who is curious, his destitute wife, my grandmom's great-grandmom, became an undertaker to support her children, although the story of, murder in the coal fields, abandonment and finally murder in the silver mine was impactful enough to travel across seven generations and 150 years.The book must appeal chiefly to those interested in its scholarship, as the details richly supported and often granular.  But as a layperson, I am enjoying the book greatly and recommend it.  It seems a balanced and reasonable examination of a much-maligned but certainly not angelic movement.
