The author, a professor of religion at Smith College, discusses the Jodo Shinshu school of Pure Land Buddhism. This is the most popular variant of Buddhism in Japan, and Pure Land Buddhism generally is the most popular variant of Buddhism in East Asia. Pure Land is quite different from the more well-known Zen school. Whereas Zen is a meditation-focused philosophical system, Pure Land has many similarities to Christianity.Pure Land Buddhism believes that Amida Buddha made a vow to help all beings achieve enlightenment and liberation from the cycle of rebirth. By reciting the mantra "namu-amida-butsu" ("I take refuge in Amida Buddha"), an individual will recognize they are embraced in Amida Buddha's boundless, universal compassion. Through simple faith in the refuge in the Amida Buddha by reciting the mantra, such individuals will be reborn in the Pure Land, where it is extremely easy to achieve enlightenment. Once they achieve enlightenment, those same individuals, motivated by and one with the boundless compassion, will return to the lower realms (including hell) to help all beings achieve enlightenment.The recitation of the mantra is intended to have the same ultimate effect as meditation or koan instruction, in that it should enable the reciter to recognize non-duality and no-self. However, Pure Land Buddhism's emphasis on mantra (rather than meditation) makes it more accessible to common people. And in fact, Pure Land Buddhism has found great popularity among the lower classes of East Asia. This is especially true because of Pure Land Buddhism's emphasis that people are "karmic-bound beings" and are not expected to be perfect. Rather, like the Christian concept that everyone is a sinner, the Pure Land Buddhists insist on the inner depravity that is in all people's hearts, even the Pure Land priests. Also similar to Christianity, the Pure Land Buddhists argue that it may be the depraved and evil who are the most beloved and the most enveloped in compassion.The author writes in a very conversational stuff, with a number of personal anecdotes. I deduct one star for two reasons. First, it is written from the perspective of a practitioner so in many ways is a Pure Land apology -- I would have preferred more historical / sociological analysis. Second, there are suggestions of Japanese ethno-nationalism, which is odd for a work that is ostensibly focused on boundless compassion. The author condemns American treatment of Indians and the atomic bombings and praises the heroism of Japanese-American veterans of World War 2.
