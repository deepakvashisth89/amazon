I have enjoyed many D.H. Lawrence short stories and felt he was a master of allusion; saying things without ever saying them at all. The mistake I made was reading Lady Chatterly's Lover before reading The Rainbow. Had I read The Rainbow first, I would have been expecting characters who were inhibited by Lawrence's writing and social mores of the times. I believe he was testing the waters of how explicit he could be, which is not explicit at all in this book. Not that I'm looking for Fifty Shades of Grey explicity, but for the love of Mike! Did they have sex or did the moon just break out of the clouds?The Rainbow doesn't have romance or excitement. Instead, it feels more like a study of three generations of women who never learned to feel or express their emotions appropriately. As a result, the only way they deeply connect is sexually. By the third generation, the dysfunction runs so deeply that Ursula seems to require some sort of arrousal to even talk with someone.Lawrence is great at demonstrating his characters' self-involved thought process, but in this book his own inhibitions buried their actions in analogies and inference.
