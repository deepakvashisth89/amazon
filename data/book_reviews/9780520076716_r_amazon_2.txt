Thomas Mann’s original German title for this novel was “Der Erwählte”, meaning “The Chosen One”. For some reason, however, when Helen Lowe-Porter translated it into English she chose to call her version “The Holy Sinner”, and the name has stuck. It is based on the verse epic “Gregorius” by the mediaeval German a German poet Hartmann von Aue, which was itself based upon French sources and which ultimately derives from the Greek myth of Oedipus.Like Oedipus, Mann’s hero Gregorius unwittingly commits incest by marrying a beautiful older woman, here named Sibylla, who turns out to be his mother. Gregorius’s family relatoinships, however, are even more tangled than those of Oedipus, because he is Sibylla’s child by her own brother Wiligis, the two being the twin children of the Duke of Flanders. Unlike Sibylla’s marriage to her son, her incest with her brother was committed wittingly and willingly by both parties. In order to hide their guilt, Sibylla and Wiligis, on the advice of an elderly counsellor, set their infant son adrift on a raft on the sea, but he survives, is rescued, and as a young man makes his way back to Flanders where Sibylla now reigns as Duchess, Wiligis having died on a Crusade.Again like Oedipus, Gregorius is horrified and overcome with guilt when he discovers what he has done, even though he had no reason to believe that Sibylla might be his mother. Unlike Oedipus he does not blind himself, but finds an equally drastic way of punishing himself to expiate his sin. God, however, still has plans for him.I have always thought that this was a strange story for Mann to retell for the mid-twentieth century- it was published in 1951- because it seems to have little to do with modern concerns. It is notable that Mann tries to distance himself ironically from his mediaeval source material by writing in the persona of a mediaeval narrator, not Hartmann but the fictitious Irish monk Clemens, who himself claims to be inspired by the "spirit of storytelling". The English version is written in a mannered, slightly archaic style; I have not read the original but understand that this was also a feature of Mann’s German.What makes the story a strange one is that is very difficult for the modern reader to enter into the mediaeval moral universe. Gregorius strikes us not as the most despicable of sinners (which is how he comes to see himself and how Clemens invites us to see him) but as a man of good moral character who finds himself in an impossible position through a chance stroke of fate. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries we have difficulty in regarding an act as evil unless it is the result of a deliberate, conscious choice to do evil. Incest may still be regarded as morally wrong, but only if it is committed deliberately. It is even more difficult for us to accept that a young child inherits at birth the sins both of its parents and of all its ancestors all the way back to Adam and Eve, or that one can atone for one’s sins by going off to kill people in the name of religion (as Wiligis does when he goes on Crusade). The mediaeval mind would have had no difficulty in accepting the truth of either of these propositions.According to Wikipedia, the book “explores a subject that fascinated Thomas Mann to the end of his life – the origins of evil and evil's connection with magic”, but this strikes me as inaccurate, partly because, as explained above, our conception of evil is very different from that of Hartmann and his contemporaries, and partly because magic plays very little part in the story. Supernatural events are ascribed to the miraculous goodness of God, not to evil magic. “The Holy Sinner” is something of a curiosity, certainly not a powerful exploration of the roots of evil, unlike Mann’s previous novel “Doctor Faustus”.
