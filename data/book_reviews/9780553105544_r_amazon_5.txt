Using his dark vision, Thomas Cook has done it again, created a novel that captures the reader in its shrouded net of gloom, guilt, sorrow, and imagination.  The protagonist is a novelist named Graves (fitting!) who writes thrillers featuring a sadist, Kessler, who with his despicable assistant, Sykes, kill for thrills in Victorian New York.  An aging and determined detective, Slovak, endlessly pursues the two through the streets and across the rooftops of the city.Graves lives a lonely and isolated life, his imagination the only thing he allows himself in penance for witnessing the horrific murder (the account of her suffering is difficult to read) of his teenage sister when he, himself, was a boy.  Invited to a summer resort to "solve" a 50 year old mystery of another 16 year old girl, Graves approaches the task as his fictional detective would, and unexpectedly finds himself aided by a clever, insightful fellow writer, a woman playwright also in residence at the resort.  As Graves seeks information about the old murder, his own history is revealed, so that, bit by bit, we come to believe in and accept his sense of doom.  And yet Cook holds out hope, like a carrot, both to Graves and to us, in the form of discovery, and a woman who offers friendship, admiration, understanding, and, finally, forgiveness.Cook has a remarkable ability to maintain dramatic tension as he leads us through his plot and characters, and his writing, as always, is excellent.  Deftly twisting and turning, he offers believable scenarios which we follow with conviction until he suddenly takes us in another direction entirely.  The resolution of the mystery can be anticipated, if one pays close attention.  That is true, perhaps, of all the threads Cook so cleverly weaves in this novel, as shocking and appalling and credulity straining as I found the main one to be.I do have a few quibbles.  There are too many coincidences of people living in the same place, without a real and satisfactory explanation for why they are all there; and, there are far too many "convenient" deaths.  Also, one character's true motive is never clearly revealed, so that, finally, her purpose for beginning the entire investigation seems like nothing more than a writer's contrivance.  A deeper motive is hinted at, and her behavior is portrayed as peculiar enough that it keeps us guessing, but there is no final explanation.  In the end, she is simply a red herring.  Then there are conversations and events remembered too clearly from fifty years ago.  And finally, there are two descriptions of photographs which troubled me:  One is a 1946 snapshot (with no explanation of why it would have been made in the first place) of a young girl inside a house, standing by a desk.  It seemed unlikely to me that a snapshot would be made inside a house, when home owned cameras with flash attachments were uncommon, even to the wealthy.  The other is a photograph of a body in which the victim's hands are described as looking "reddened."  A 1946 crime photograph would have been black and white and while hands may have looked "darkened" or "bruised" they would hardly be seen as "reddened".  Cook also describes the trousers worn by three old men whom his characters encounter in separate incidents and locations as "baggy".  Not all old men wear baggy pants and even if they do, find another word or omit the adjective, especially when the passages are close enough together that the reader notices, as I did.But these are minor complaints.
