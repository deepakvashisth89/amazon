This is an excellent book of military history for the casual layperson (non-specialist in military affairs). Fourteen chapters each chronicle a battle that produced results other than what would have been expected as outcomes (one chapter is about the assassination of Archduke Ferdinand in Sarajevo, and another about the Irish uprising in 1916, not actually battles. More on this below.). They range from Chapter 1 Cannae (Second Punic War) to Chapter 14 Tet Offensive (Vietnam).This is quite alot of ground to cover in only 288 pages. Weir does it with concise to-the-point writing.Yet, he still makes room to cover the background material surrounding the context of each military contest. For instance, Chapter 3 Hattin concerns the Second Crusade, and he starts this chapter with a capsule history of the First Crusade. Other background given include the prevailing international situation, geography and terrain, and overall capabilities of the belligerents (things such as supply, training, past battle experiences of the troops).Weir also makes room to include pertinent details such as the personalities, motivations and past experiences of the commanders. Of course, and most importantly, he also sketches the strategic and tactical perspectives of the commanders.Certainly not least, details pertaining to the technologies of arms are not neglected.Lastly, each chapter ends with an explanation of the battle's unexpected outcome, and how it profoundly influenced history. Weir chose the battles to be included presciently: each had profound consequences for later conflicts. For instance, Saladin's victory at Hattin led to Moslem overconfidence that ultimately resulted in the rise of Europe. It also led to efforts by the European navies to circumnavigate around the Moslem nations of the mideast to link with the Orient (producing the voyages of Vasco de Gama and Columbus).As another for-instance, Weir points out that the Confederacy actually lost its war for independence at Chickamauga Creek. He quotes Lt. General Hill: "It seems to me that the elan of the Southern soldier was never seen after Chickamauga. He fought stoutly to the last, but, after Chickamauga, with the sullenness of despair and without the enthusiasm of hope. That 'barren victory' sealed the fate of the Confederacy." (note: Weir actually quotes only the first sentence; I have taken liberty to include Hill's entire quote copied from Wikipedia.) Here the true meaning of the chapter's title is made clear, "You Got Into Our Inwards". Going into this chapter, I assumed that someone had made a clever cutting stroke into an opponent's interior lines of communication. What a delightful surprise to find on the chapter's penultimate page!This reminds me of my history lesson in high school in which I remember my teacher asserting that the South lost the war at Gettysburg (or was it Vicksburg?) At any rate, as Weir explains on page 140: "By 1863, the future of the Confederate States of America had begun to look dim. Union forces had taken Vicksburg and now controlled the whole length of the Mississippi. They had also defeated Lee's invasion of the North at Gettysburg. The future, however, was far from hopeless. Although Vicksburg's loss had, in a sense cut the Confederacy in two, there had been little important commerce between the two portions since the war began. The great bulk of Confederate resources and manpower was in the eastern section. As for Gettysburg, only hindsight could make it a decisive battle. At the time, it was no more decisive than the Confederacy's 1862 defeat at Antietam. In both cases, Lee retired with his army intact, after having inflicted approximately equal, though heavy, losses on his opponent. About all that either battle had proved was that Lee couldn't conquer the North."(I quote at length to emphasize that the book contains many arguments and conclusions contrary to the conventional historical wisdom, and as a sample of the quality of Weir's work.)Also germane to the subject of historical consequences is Weir's inclusion of the two non-battle chapters involving the incidents in Sarajevo and Dublin. In some of the other reviews I see some criticism for their inclusion in the book. I think Weir should be granted some license here, because he does an admirable job of summing up their far-reaching effects on world history even if they were not strictly "battles". Of course, we all know that the assassination of Archduke Ferdinand set off WWI (Bismarck's famous "some damn fool thing in the Balkans" he uttered some decades prior to the fact) and thus set the course for the entire 20th Century (Bolshevik Revolution, Hitler, Stalin, Cold War). As for the Easter Rebellion in Ireland, Weir writes, "Using a combination of guerrilla war and propaganda, Ireland showed the world that a weak and tiny nation can free itself from a great power...It was not Ireland's military power, but its appeal to world opinion that made independence possible." As for myself, I think this makes a fine reason to include it in the book. At any rate, it's a sure bet that it at least SEEMED like a battle to the participants.This book contains a fantastic amount of material in one slim highly readable volume. As a result, and necessarily, it probably will not satisfy the serious history buff. This must be -- at once -- the book's strength and weakness. For those interested in an exhaustive treatment of the material covered, Weir includes an excellent bibliography in which he notes particularly good supplementary books.Weir has a unique talent for the big-picture overview and also the zoom-lens focus on the important details. For the casual reader of military history, I've never before seen a more enjoyable and accessible book.Read moreRead less
