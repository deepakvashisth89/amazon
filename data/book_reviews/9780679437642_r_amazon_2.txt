I was interested in Gell-Mann since I read about his contribution to complexity and the Santa Fe institute. This part of his life, unfortunately, is only described in one of the later chapters. But it was interesting to learn about the rest of his life. I particularly enjoyed the most personal chapters, specially his childhood as a prodigy. Much of the history of the development of the  Standard Theory was not interesting to me because unlike the cosmological implications of Quantum Physics, the sub-particle realm is arid an devoid of meaning unless of course, you are a physicist. Yet, I did enjoy the world of  discovery, competition, cooperation, recognition and oblivion for scientists and specifically, for physicists.  Johnson does a superb work of research, balance and synthesis. The only part of the book which was a bit dense, once again, was the theoretical part.  It must of been hard to work with Murray and his neurotic personality just long enough to get the book done,  but I wished the author had been a bit more benevolent or better yet, more dedicated to understand the psychological complexity of the character.
