It is difficult to understand the battle of Verdun because, to paraphrase Alistair Horne, it seemed to develop into a self consuming fire that promised never to end until the last two combatants had hobbled from their trenches to attack each other with pocket knives.  This is a remarkable book with remarkable levels of detail.  The narrative, with its brisk style, is compelling and highly readable.  Some reviewers on this page criticise the work for what seem now to be outdated stereotypical observations about nationalities and it's also true that it does lean towards magnifying individuals to something greater than human - not just generals commanding armies but individual pilots of the embryonic air forces.  Such an approach may reflect the `norms' of when the book was written (and our own `norms' will change over the next twenty years making current preoccupations appear dated) but I would suggest that if we are to understand the abattoir of a battle such as Verdun then we have to go beyond reason and rationality to explore the trickier matters of social attitudes, social structures, personalities and cultural differences.  If the author were writing today he may more carefully define his terms or limit his use of adjectives but by any definition this is still a remarkable work.  I am not knowledgeable enough about Verdun to comment on the accuracy of the history itself, or the weight given to actions and events, so my only criticism of the Penguin edition is that there are far too few maps and diagrams.  For instance, there is only one two-page map of the battleground, which is in the body of the text making it awkward to refer to.
