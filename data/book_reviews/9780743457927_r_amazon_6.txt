A few thoughts upon re-reading John Le Carre’s most autobiographical novel, ‘A Perfect Spy’ (first read in 1987):When I say “autobiographical”, I should clarify that it is autobiographical in that it gives its main character, Magnus Pym, the same timeline as David Cornwell (original name for John Le Carre’), with a great deal of authentic information on the workings of the British intelligence organization, MI6, based on Cornwell’s own experience working for them while conducting a legitimate career as a school teacher. Beyond that, Magnus Pym is traumatized by growing up with a con man father, much like David Cornwell’s own father. Cornwell did have an older brother and later some younger step siblings but Pym appears to be an only child whose mother disappears after being hospitalized in a mental ward.Rick Pym is a very Dickensian character, with a big personality and schemes that are literally larger than life as in they do not exist in life, only in Rick’s own fanciful brain and implanted in a few of his loyal sidekicks/followers such as Sid Lemon. Rick gets gullible investors to contribute to transit companies, insurance agencies, and various other fraudulent concerns, being jailed on more than one occasion.With a father like that, Magnus begins to learn at an early age how to lie and steal. While he is still a student in Bern, Switzerland, he is recruited into British intelligence. Almost simultaneously, he establishes a relationship with an Austrian named Axel, who becomes his lifelong contact as a double agent. Pym also refers to Axel as Poppy, due to the symbolic poppies he left for Magnus as a gift/message.The narrative framework of the novel is Pym’s departure from everyone with a “burn box”, literally a container of secret information. No one is entirely sure what secrets it contains. He’s gone to a little house in Devon where he is the occasional renter of an elderly landlady named Mrs. Dubber. He is “Mr. Canterbury”. This time he has gone there ostensibly to write his memoirs i.e. the bulk of the novel, including letters to his son Tom, reminiscences of his father Rick, and occasional third-person autobiography of “Pym”. His handler Jack Brotherhood and his estranged wife Mary, also in the “Firm” (the name given to MI6, in previous Le Carre’ novels referred to as “the Circus”) are tracking him down based on every available clue he left behind. At this point, he has now been pretty thoroughly identified as the double agent that has been playing havoc with their operations for decades.I doubt that David Cornwell was ever a double agent and he may not have been an active agent running covert operations and other agents (“Joes”), but he was definitely on the payroll. Le Carre’ claimed that the double agent Kim Filby blew his cover when his literary career was taking off with ‘The Spy Who Came In From the Cold’.John Le Carre’s spy novels are generally regarded as the more realistic, unglamorous counterpart to James Bond’s escapades. In this novel, for example, Magnus Pym has a gun but only uses it once. Le Carre’s protagonists, such as George Smiley, the mild-mannered hero of ‘Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy’ and a few others, is certainly no ladies’ man. In fact, in ‘Tinker Tailor…’ he is the cuckold, his wife having a long-term affair with one of his chief rivals in the Circus. However, in ‘A Perfect Spy’, Magnus Pym has sex with as many women as six movie’s worth of James Bond’s conquests. He’s married twice, plus almost every female contact of note eventually ends up in his bed.Magus Pym has acquired enough experience in the spy world that he has become a sort of philosopher of how the members of that secret society see their lives:‘We didn’t have anybody who wasn’t himself a citizen of the secret world, blessed with the unlined innocence of privilege. The nearest they had come to life’s experience was the war, and they saw the peace as its continuation by other means. Yet in the terms of the world outside their heads they had led lives so untested, so child-like and tender in their simplicities, so inward in their connections, that they required echelons of cut-outs to reach the society they honestly believed they were protecting.’As the seasoned professional, Magnus Pym offers a sober assessment of the espionage industry that would shock the idealistic James Bond. Here are his thoughts on America:‘No country was ever easier to spy on, Tom, no nation so openhearted with its secrets, so quick to air them, share them, confide them, or consign them too early to the junk heap of planned American obsolescence. I am too young to know whether there was a time when Americans were able to restrain their admirable passion to communicate, but I doubt it. Certainly the path has been downhill since 1945, for it was quickly apparent that information which ten years ago would have cost Axel’s service thousands of dollars in precious hard-currency could by the mid-seventies be had for a few coppers from the Washington Post. We could have resented this sometimes, if we had been smaller natures, for there are few things more vexing in the spy world than landing a great scoop for Prague and London one week, only to read the same material in Aviation Weekly the next.’‘A Perfect Spy’ is rightly regarded as Le Carre’s most literary novel. More than in any others of his that I have read, in this novel he treats the duplicity of spying as a mental illness, analyzes it, and gives it tragic proportions. Magnus Pym is the Hamlet of spy novels. As the narrator of his own story, he also writes the most labyrinthine, Proustian of spy novels as well. It is often difficult to know who he is referring to and when something is happening because he doesn’t give us narrative cues to follow another time frame. Reading it requires constant focus.If you want a more straightforward narrative in John Le Carre’s works, try ‘The Spy Who Came in From the Cold’ or ‘Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy’. For a work that stands alongside the best of Graham Greene (one of Le Carre’s literary mentors), ‘A Perfect Spy’ is the perfect choice.Read moreRead less
