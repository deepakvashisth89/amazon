Old Gods Almost Dead is one of the best of the many Rolling Stones biographies I have read over the years, right up there with books by the likes of Stanley Booth and Philip Norman. It is insightful, evocative and superbly written, going a long way to capturing the mystery of the Stones enduring success and longevity, why they stood out from the pack from their earliest days playing the tiny sweat lodge blues clubs of London and why they have stood the test of time when so many others have fallen-perhaps largely as a result of the alchemy between that strange two-headed chimera of Jagger-Richards who stand front of centre of everything and everyone. Indeed, in many ways the story of the Stones as recounted here is the story of Mick and Keith with the other members playing their support roles and then exiting stage left.I found the first part of this book more interesting than the second, covering the beginnings of the band and its Brian Jones years, that chaotic, mercurial force of nature, a devil with an angels face and his “almost desperate run for the rainbow.” As the odyssey moves in the dissolute 70s the music remains as strong as ever even as the Stones themselves seem to be losing interest in it all and by the mid-80s and middle age it all fractures apart in the worst possible way, very similar to the messy ending of their great friends and rivals the Beatles, a band who the Stones seem to eerily echo in each stage of their career.The book then ends with the Stones celebrating their 40th year as a unit following a triumphant reconciliation and reinvention as a Vegas style soul band, sans Brian Jones, Bill Wyman and Mick Taylor among many others, seemingly intent on pushing the boundaries and endlessly touring in the manner of another great 60s counterpart Bob Dylan.
