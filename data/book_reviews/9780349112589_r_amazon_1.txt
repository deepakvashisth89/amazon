Charlotte Grimshaw is a New Zealand author with a background in criminal law. This was her debut published in 1999, written during a period in England. Whilst there are many aspects of the novel that demonstrate the author’s potential - for example, her descriptions of social interactions between the ethnic and economic strands of the country’s society – overall it is a disappointing read.Described as a ‘terrifying analysis of obsessive love and a thriller of extraordinary power and intelligence’, the book is much more the former and hardly deserving of the title ‘thriller’, and suffers from its characters being rather two-dimensional, something that dissipates the reader’s empathy and interest.Stuart is a very successful and criminal lawyer working in Auckland. In his early 30s he lives with Stella a young law student who works as his assistant. Divorced from his wife and young daughter, Stuart is a control freak. It is difficult to see exactly why such a successful person would be attracted to the dreary student.Unfortunately Grimshaw provides little evidence to support Stuart’s brilliance – his legal preparation being fuelled by alcohol and sex, and one of his legal team is unable to spell.The case at the centre of the novel is that of a Carlos Lehmann, who is part Maori. Lehmann and his wife once owned a thriving fish business but then moved to a remote bush territory with the idea of establishing a successful agricultural business. Unfortunately his new neighbours resent his arrival and make life difficult for him and his family. When he plants trees and sets out his farm, he has his fences pulled out, smaller bushes dug up and trees ruined by marauding animals.Furious at this latest attack, Lehmann argues with one of the neighbours, returns home to get his rifle and returns to shoot the man. Stuart is determined to have the case but finds that the locals have their stories agreed, all being hostile to Lehmann. At the heart of the book there is a strange disconnect between Stuart’s inability to communicate with those around him, most notably Stella, and his apparent courtroom brilliance.Using the argument of provocation, Stuart manages to get Lehmann released from custody until his trial and he is confined to his parent’s home. This causes outrage in the media and supports the decision of his wife and children to move to Auckland to escape threats. The characters are rather stereotypical and the story is devoid of mystery or, indeed, eroticism. As with any book dealing with mental and physical abuse, there is a central need to explore the psychological basis of the pair’s relationship and their backgrounds. Here one searches in vain for such detail.Other storylines - a debauched lawyer [called Stain] who is determined to bed Stella [apparently a common aim of Stuart’s friends which, of course, leads to jealous confrontation], a menacing odd-job man who was one of Stuart’s legal successes and attempts to break into the couple’s home – are of only passing interest. They throw Stella into panic, as does the behavior of Stuart’s large dog [presumably an attempt to bring in some contrasting humour], but simply serve to annoy Stuart. All the characters in the book, including an undercover policeman, seem able to drive the roads whilst being unable to stand through drinking.This debut may appeal to those with an interest in New Zealand or the law [several points the legal definition of 'provocation' is expounded at length], but this reader found it very disappointing.
