Did people really talk like that in the early 1900's? I mean that unending morose philosophical probing. That devastatingly truthful in-your-face social observation. Or is this merely a literary device. As a stream-of-consciousness novel, it is as numbing as Virginia Woolf, and the lack of plot does not do it any favours.Any sexual encounters are only vaguely hinted at, which creates a sort of confusion in the storyline. Did Gerald sleep with Pussum or not? The writer only mentions him falling asleep in the same house, although Gerald later talks about "giving her money" and the author much later briefly mentions her as his "mistress".Similarly bizarre is the attempted muder of Birkin by Hermione, when she brings down a rock with full force onto his head. Strangely he brushes it off with "don't you see? it is not me who will die" and does not even bother to report it to the police.Like most modernists, Lawrence seems to reject Christianity (whilst showing a very inaccurate knowledge of it) in his search for meaning. Woolf is similar in exploring the new post-Christian emptiness, accentuated by the rapid growth of big cities and industry. To avoid becoming an automaton, Lawrence turns to the cult of nature (whilst denying its creator). By the end, it is morose: Lawrence focuses on death and futility.Still, its a vaguely interesting portrayal of how English people dressed, spoke and acted before both world wars, the beatles, and television.Read moreRead less
