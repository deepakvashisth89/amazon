Palaeoanthropology is a favorite topic of mine; I've read quite a few books on human origins and evolution, but this one is one of the best. The author does not merely dig up this skeleton of the Nariokotome boy, a 9-12 year old Homo erectus, describe it, and then move on to the next set of bones. Rather, he inquires deeply into everything this skeleton can tell him about its species and to do this the author wisely does not depend only on his own skills, which are considerable enough, but elicits the cooperation of other experts who are more narrowly focused on specific anatomic parts. Thus the pelvis is farmed out to experts and the author receives information that allow conclusions regarding brain size at birth. The author faxes measurements of the vertebrae to another expert and receives information relevant to motor control of intercostal muscles and how this may be important for speech. The teeth are studied in detail to learn what they can tell us of the boy's age and maturation pattern in comparison to humans and chimpanzees. While the author devotes much of the first part of this book to describing the history of human paleontological investigations, particularly as they relate to Homo erectus, the latter part of the book is a highly skilled description of what can be gleaned from dry bones dead 1.7 million years when experts collaborate and cooperate. The author says that his wife, his co-author, actually did most of the writing. If true, she has done a marvelous job describing her husband's work as if we are hearing it from him.
