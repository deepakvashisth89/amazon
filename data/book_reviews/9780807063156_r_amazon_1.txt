This engrossing master work about multi-generational trauma in the aftermath of slavery in America, set in the 1940s to 1960s, following the life of a Kentucky blues singer whose ancestors were enslaved. I read it straight through. I read it slowly, so I could hear the voices speaking in my ear. The novel is so vivid and filled with life it feels like a movie, or a memory from long ago, a major theme of this story. The main character was so real to me at the end she seemed to have a pulse. The story is a majestic force, reminiscent of James Joyce, Steinbeck, and Henry James, one of the author's influences. Like their novels, this one offers a wealth of insight, in this case into the trauma faced by black women and black men and how it shapes their relationships. The fact that this masterpiece did not win any major literary awards when it came out in 1975 shames a literary establishment that all but ignored Black writers in general and Black female writers in particular, though that would change with the ascension of the editor of Corregidora, Toni Morrison.
