This book features a return to the world of Videssos as introduced in the Videssos Cycle (Volume 1&Volume 2). This book takes place 600 years earlier and is the first part of a duology (withKrispos of Videssos) and later trilogy (withKrispos the Emperor). First off, the best thing that it does is get rid of the Romans as lead characters and focus instead on some native Videssans. The outsider coming in can really pay off in a fantasy setting, but I always felt that the Roman aspect of those books was the weakest part. Turtledove just doesn't get Romans and they feel so much like modern surrogates that it's a wonder he didn't just use those instead. Here he has realized that he doesn't need gimmicks like dimension jumping to draw us in as long as he does his job right. Which he does.A brief summary of the book: Krispos is a young peasant who grows up in an isolated village on the outskirts of the empire of Videssos. When the village starts having problems due to taxation and plague Krispos goes to Videssos (the City) where he quite quickly ends up advancing high into the nobility until he is serving the emperor. The current emperor, Anthimos III, is an irresponsible hedonist who gives no thought to ruling and relies on his uncle Petronas, who is actually running the emperor. There is a great deal of political intrigue and it is no spoiler to say that Krispos will end up emperor since they make that clear via prophecy fairly early on. Also, one of its sequels is called Krispos the Emperor. Kind of a giveaway.If I have a complaint it is that this novel is based rather more directly than the last ones on one specific period, namely the time of Basil I. Basil I is Krispos, pure and simple. Pretty much all the details from serving as a stableboy to having a wealthy patroness back him up for no clearly understandable reason are features that he shares with Basil. Normally that would bother me more, but Basil's was such an interesting story that it is nice to see it told in any form. Something that actually amuses me about this is that many of Basil's actual achievements can only be explained here by the use of magic and prophecy. After all, why would a wealthy Patran widow suddenly decide to back a young peasant from the boondocks of Macedonia and then continue to do so until he became emperor? It's madness, yet it really happened. I consider this a testament to Basil's achievement. Fictionalizing this in a fantasy setting also allows Turtledove to nicen him up, because Basil really wasn't a nice guy.The magic here also feels more thought out than in previous Videssan books. It blends in better and feels less like a literary inclusion for the sake of fun. I really did like this book and am quite looking forward to seeing where this goes in future.Read moreRead less
