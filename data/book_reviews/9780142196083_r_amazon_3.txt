For those of us brought up Catholic in the years before the Vatican II reforms, this book brings back a lot of reminders of what the Church actually teaches - then and now. A reminder that men matter more than women (the main reason I stopped going to Church), that no one is supposed to have a sex life since contraception is forbidden (yes, the Church bans something as sensible and well-accepted as birth control!) and sex is only for procreation, that the Church tells its members what to think, what they can read, and sometimes how to vote (against anyone who would allow legal abortion or gay marriage). It also claims to be the direct and ONLY access to God, whose mouthpiece is an infallible Pope.I quit being a Catholic sometime in the 1960s and, like the author, I had been pretty heavily steeped in Catholic thought and culture; the author, even more so, since he spent time in the seminary as a young man, thinking he would become a priest. Reading this book took me back to the 1960s and 1970s when so many priests and nuns left the Church. At the same time, the Church was supposedly reforming itself, through the Vatican II Council. Like the author, I had a fondness for the Latin Mass and never felt the same way about the newer practices. I took four years of Latin in high school (Latin is a beautiful, elegant language!), and thought the Latin Mass had a beauty about it, born of long tradition. Nor did I encounter any pedophile priests, but there were a few sadistic nuns, and the Catholic schools I attended had more enthusiasm for gym and football than they did for academic achievement. In the blue-collar city of Flint Michigan where I grew up, a Catholic girl was expected to marry a factory worker and settle down to raising lots of Catholic children. For girls, the bar was set very low.This book brought me up to date on what I've missed from all these years of non-attendance at Church (I'm now retired). I was surprised to read that Catholics mostly don't go to Confession any more. That trip into the confessional was a regular part of my growing-up years, along with taunts of the Protestants that "you Catholics go to Confession and get your sinful behavior forgiven so you can go out and do it again.". The "sanctity of the confessional" has been a part of so many movie plots (you know, where the priest refuses to divulge the bad guy's confession), what will Hollywood do without it? The schizophrenic attitudes about whether other religions should be tolerated are interesting too. Didn't I learn all those years ago that "outside the Church there is no salvation?" But in today's world, we've all learned to value diversity and accept other cultures and belief systems, so some members of the clergy have tried to soften this whole "we have the truth and you don't" thing. But considering what the Church actually teaches, don't Catholics have to condemn their Protestant, Buddhist and Mormon neighbors?It seems John Cornwell, like me, has been through a roller coaster of feelings about the Catholic Church. I have read two of his other books, so I know he is an honest, though conflicted, spokesman about Catholic matters. He seems willing to accept the Church and its leader, the Pope, as the authority on matters relating to a belief in God, Jesus as Son of God, and our relationship to the Deity. He accepts that the Church is the arbiter of what those beliefs consist of. To be a Catholic, you must "believe" certain things. You cannot be a seeker (and I DO call myself a Seeker; my website is theseekerbooks). According to the Church, there is nothing to seek. The Church provides all the answers. Nothing is tentative, nothing can be revised, all is known. Sign on the dotted line that you believe the items the Church tells you are so.John Cornwell is fairly eloquent in his summaries of what's wrong with the Catholic Church, and yet he apparently "believes." The most astonishing thing I learned from this book is the extent to which the Church is dying for lack of the priests, nuns and monks it needs to carry out the Pope's orders. The shortage is so great and the remaining priests so old that the day is coming when most parishes will not have a priest. Today, many parishes are served by an aging and no doubt tired old priest who must ride the circuit of several churches because there are no replacements and seminaries are failing to recruit new priests. The author says he heard an aged priest complain that the reason there are no replacements is because Catholic women are practicing birth control and not having the children who might have become priests.The author provides many statistics and surveys showing women who self-identify as Catholic practice birth control in the same numbers as non-Catholics. In the United States, the non-compliance with the Church's teaching on contraception and even abortion is almost universal. People attend church but do not accept what the Pope tells them. Not just on the matter of birth control, but in other areas as well. Isn't this the precursor to schism, or a break-up? There's also the matter of the sexual abuse scandal involving pedophile priests and the growing number of priests who are gay. If you take a longer view, you can consider the history of the Church, with its record of torturing non-Catholics through the Inquisition, the forced conversion of Spanish Jews, and the long dark history of Popes who used their power to manipulate European rulers and whose lives were anything but "chaste." For a good account of the history of the papacy, I recommendThe Vicars of Christ : Dark Side of the Papacyby Peter De Rosa.I am surrounded by evidence of the waning of the Catholic Church. Where I live, in Detroit, the list of closed parishes on the website for the Archdiocese of Detroit requires lots of scrolling. It is a long list. You can drive through the city and see the boarded-up Catholic schools, closed churches, and former Catholic churches now occupied by Baptist congregations. The Catholic Church is dying, and, while once I opened and started reading this book I could not seem to stop, so fascinated was I with the author's gloomy picture of the Church's future, I cannot mourn for this dying institution. This is the Church that told me my options in life were limited because I am female. This is the Church that tells me I cannot seek truth and look for God on my own, but must submit to a list of "beliefs" given by an "infallible" old man in Rome. If the author thought John Paul II would be succeeded by a reform-minded Pope he must have been disappointed by the election of Cardinal Ratzinger (Pope Benedict XVI), who he mentions frequently in this book, and not necessarily in a good way. Ratzinger's last job, before becoming Pope, was presiding over what remains of the Inquisition.Things are not going to get better for people still clinging to the Catholic Church. Rome still tells women they are not worthy to be members of the clergy or hold any important position and their contributions to the intellectual life of the Church (if there is such a thing) are not welcome. They are suited only to be mothers of many children, all of whom should be raised as good little Catholics. What a delusion, in an overpopulated, overheating world!Can an old and once-powerful institution like the Catholic Church actually die? If you believe that it traces its roots back to St Peter and has existed ever since, that might seem impossible. But even the author concedes that the early Christians did not have the same kind of top-down organization, nor did they rely on priests for all their liturgies. The Catholic Church developed over the centuries and became an autocratic, bureaucratic, patriarchal dictatorship. The papacy (under Pius XII who Cornwell wrote about in his book,Hitler's Pope: The Secret History of Pius XII) was ok with Hitler and his Nazis, whose tactics were so much like the Inquisition, but hated Communism with its concern for the common man. It has never endorsed Democracy, which it regards with suspicion.Face it - the Catholic Church is a medieval organization trying to stay alive in a modern world.
