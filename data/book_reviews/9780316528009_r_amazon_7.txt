When she was small, Sarah Jane (Sally) Copeland's parents sent her to a boarding school for children with disabilities, because Sally has cerebral palsy. For as long as she can remember, Sally has longed to live at home with her family, but when her parents decide to bring her home for good she learns her wish come true is more complicated, challenging, and frightening than she always believed it would be.This is the first book I ever read as a child in which the protagonist had cerebral palsy like me. In fact, I ordered it from my local bookstore based on that fact alone; I had met other kids with disabilities but theirs were much more severe, and those kids were (unfairly) sequestered in special education because of school policies. I had no idea that some kids with CP were like me. In Sally, I found my first literary friend who struggled with some of the same things I did. She couldn't button tiny buttons, or write legibly, or keep up when her teacher rattled off problems for a mental arithmetic test. She wanted to be independent and face fears, but the "mainstream," non-disabled world often seemed like such a frightening place. At the age of ten I remember reading Mine for Keeps and thinking, "Finally, somebody gets it, gets me."Sally's journey is well-written and refreshingly not focused on CP or making her an inspiration to others. Cerebral palsy is a featured part of a scene when it needs to be, like when Sally goes to a clinic for an annual orthopedic checkup. Otherwise though, Sally is a typical kid trying to fit in at a new school and make friends. Her friends, too, are refreshing. Sally isn't bullied, but neither are her friends overly nice or pitying. One girl, Elsje, is cold at first, but that's mostly because of a misunderstanding regarding the modifications Sally needs, and because of Elsje's own troubles.Sally gets what many of today's protagonists with disabilities do not get in books. She is afforded a chance to be independent, but also help others, thereby becoming interdependent. For example, she faces her fear of dogs to adopt Susie, a shy Westie terrier who becomes her sole responsibility. She reaches out to Pieter, Elsje's older brother who is hostile toward his peers and believes a medical condition has sidelined him forever. She even learns to stand up for herself within her family, gradually learning that she *can* do and be more than "that girl with CP," and expecting her family to treat her as such.This book was originally written in the late 1980s but I feel it was and is ahead of its time. Today, there is a focus on writing fiction that features protagonists with more severe disabilities such as autism or CP so intense the person cannot function well. That's fine; everyone needs to be represented. Unfortunately though, many of these characters are given no responsibilities, few if any friends, and no real life. What happened to disability fiction? I would recommend that all teachers, especially those with elementary or middle school students, assign this book as classroom reading or a reading list option to discuss what disability fiction should look like.
