In the late Bronze age, on the island Crete, temple scribes wrote on their tablets using a pictorial language which came to be known by the rather prosaic name of "Linear B". Over three thousand years later, a teenager by the name of Michael Ventris saw these tablets during a lecture being given by Arthur Evans, an old, venerable archaeologist, who was 85 at the time. Evans had discovered the tablets many years earlier. On being told that no one had been able to decipher the script, the young Ventris became hooked on what would be, to him, a lifelong obsession. But Ventris was no ordinary young man with a penchant for solving puzzles, he was much, much more.This book tells the story of Ventris, and his surprising success in decoding the mysterious Linear B. He possessed a unique mind, one which had an uncanny ability with language. By the age of 21, he spoke all the major European languages, plus Latin and Greek, new and ancient. Despite his gifts, he remained humble and somewhat aloof, dividing different parts of his life into separate, apparently watertight compartments. He became a successful architect, married Lois, a fellow architect, and raised two children. But Linear B remained an obsession. He, and a professional classicist Emmett Bennet, continued to work on it over the years.In 1952 Ventris announced to the world that Linear B was, contrary to the opinion of many archaeologists at the time, an early version of Greek, and not some unknown language.I enjoyed the book, although unfortunately many of the detailed passages relating the Linear B script were difficult to understand. I simply had to skim over them. However they did convey some of the complexity of the task. This was a language written in ancient Greek, and many of the symbols were of sounds rather than pictograms. The methods used for the decoding were combinations of inspired guesswork, luck and intuition, with many of the guesses hard to explain, even by Ventris himself.The author spends considerable time discussing the process of discovery itself, both in direct relation to solving Linear B, but also more generally. He lists several requirements for great discovery: First, a need for years of study to have large amounts of facts at ones fingertips; second, the imagination to propose new ideas; thirdl, the courage to make the jump into the unknown. And finally, the honesty to accept when one is wrong, and the determination to start again.Once Ventris had decoded Linear B, he seemed to lose interest in the language he had done so much to rescue from obscurity. He put his efforts into architecture, and was given a prestigious project to develop new and better ways for architects to access knowledge.Michael, the person himself, remains unknown. But just exploring the story of his decoding Linear B, is possibly the closest one can get to understanding such a complex character.There is one final mystery. Ventris died at the tragically young age of 34, in a violent car crash. At 1am in the morning, his car slammed at high speed into a parked lorry. No one, including his wife, knows where he was going, or what he was doing at that hour of the morning. In the months just prior to his death he had resigned from his post at the Architects, writing a disarming letter proclaiming that his efforts were not useful, even offering to return his salary. This seemed odd, to say the least.Was his death suicide?
