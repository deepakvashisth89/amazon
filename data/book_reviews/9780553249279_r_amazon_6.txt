I am not sure what the all the fuss is with Le Carre.  The story here is interesting although mid-way through it becomes clear how the book will end, even if the precise details aren't yet known until the very end.  Also, there is much to learn about the spy business, the "trade craft" of spies, the world view of a certain generation of Brits, particularly in the intelligence community, and so on.  Unfortunately, I find Le Carre to be difficult reading for many reasons.First, his use of slang and idiom distinctive to the spy business and the culture of the characters is confusing and distracting.  Second, both the narrator and the characters often make their points indirectly or in a kind of shorthand that seems to presume that the reader possesses all necessary background information or relevant cultural context.  Third, he tries to pack too much meaning, including subtle shades of meaning, into his sentences, often by employing idiom and shorthand in the manner I have just described.  The result of all of these techniques is that the reader often finishes a paragraph and wonders just what has been said.  When you add in the many characters and the author's tendency to refer back to earlier events, you get a book that seems unnecessarily perplexing, in my opinion.The book is worth reading, but I would strongly suggest that it by read in just a few concentrated sittings.  It is not at all a book to be picked up and put down.  Even in one sitting, I think that at least 25% of the information that Le Carre is trying to convey will simply get lost in his dense and at times jerky writing style.  He seems to write for people who share his background, which is a small audience indeed.  It's unfortunate because if written in a slightly more accessible style the book would be more rewarding to the average reader.  Complexity does not need to involve confusion if done well.This is my second Le Carre novel and will be my last.Read moreRead less
