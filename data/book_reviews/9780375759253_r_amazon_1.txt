I had never heard of this book before, but the synopsis caught my eye - mainly because I am just under Babbitt's age in the book and I can relate somewhat to his stage of life.  In my own life I notice my health declining, most of my own personal goals accomplished, my son considering where to go in life after high school, and the full realization of how little influence I truly have on the world.And while not all that much in the book happens that the synopsis doesn't cover, for some reason the extreme amount of detail remains engaging throughout.  The first 100 pages or so of the book covers the span of a single normal day, and that's it.  It very much establishes Babbitt's routine probably with the primary goal of painting it as mundane and give you an idea of why Babbitt desires an escape.But of course, escaping a life of stability does come with a price.  Some escapes from a stable life are quite noble.  I can't imagine anyone calling a firefighter's life stable with all of potential dangers faced there, but there's a lot of good that comes from that profession.  And then we have escapes from stability that are quite foolhardy and pleasure-focused to the detriment of those around you.  Part of the reason the prohibition of the time was enacted in the first place.  Then we have the arena of unstable radical politics, which may be noble or detrimental, depending on how the results turn out - and to this day our society wrestles a lot with.Of course, I tend to be a guy who gravitates toward routine and mundane things in general, and in particular old mundane things.  I really appreciate being able to read this relic from 103 years ago, and see what life was like back then.  Had I lived during that actual time though, I probably wouldn't have enjoyed it any more than I enjoy the life I have today.  I'm aware that I tend to romanticize the past, very much how Babbitt tends to romanticize anything different from his current life.  When he actually goes and tries different things like hiking in remote areas, they don't quite go as he planned or imagined.The dialogue of the city residents certainly is amusing, in particular when they realize their often-contradictory utterings - first complaining about the inefficiency of the trolley system and then admitting it's actually quite efficient and not worth complaining about.  I take these things more as an inherent trait of the human condition to complain than a political attack on the status quo of the time.  Even the treatment of guest towels in bathrooms is almost the same today as it was way back then - showing just how strange the human condition is.The introduction really is a good one and helps set the stage before reading the actual novel.  I think it's a much better analysis than my book review is.  But for anyone interested in what American life was like during the early 1920's, it would probably be difficult to find a better example than Babbitt to give you a clear picture.  I suppose my grandma could have done so when she was alive.  She was around 6 years old I think when this book came out.  But she passed away in 2005 and had really bad Alzheimer's a few years before her death.  So time marches on mercilessly, and even the mundane world of Babbitt has long passed us by - and changed into something that is in some ways that is remarkably similar, and in other ways entirely different.Read moreRead less
