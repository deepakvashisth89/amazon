This is a review of four books written by Lt. Col. Lee Lanning and his wife Linda Lanning.  All are available on Amazon:1) The Only War We Had:  A Platoon Leader’s Journal of Vietnam2) Vietnam 1969-1970:  A Company Commander’s Journal3) Waiting:  One Wife’s Year of the Vietnam War4) The Battles of Peace:  One Company Commander’s battle Against Drugs and Racial Conflict In The War To Rebuild The Post-Vietnam ArmyThe first two books are based on the journals Lanning kept during his tour in Vietnam in 1969-1970.  He reprints the entries and then comments on them with the benefit of hindsight and maturity (the books were written originally in the late 1980s but reprinted more recently.)  Waiting is written by Lanning’s wife Linda and is her story of being a waiting wife during her husband's combat tour.  The Battles of Peace is Lanning’s account of his tour as a company commander in Germany in the immediate post-Vietnam era when he inherited an underperforming unit and transformed it into the best in his division.This four-book series is a study in contrasts:  the gung-ho Aggie Lieutenant at the beginning of The Only War We had vs. the hardened veteran at the end of Vietnam 1969-1970.  The husband trying to survive in The Only War We Had and Vietnam 1969-1970 vs. the wife trying to survive the never-ending stress of separation in Waiting.  The company commander in combat in Vietnam 1969-1970 vs. the company commander in garrison in The Battles of Peace.Lanning’s experience as a platoon and company commander in combat is covered in the two-part series The Only War We Had and Vietnam 1969-1970.  As a 22-year-old platoon commander Lanning is thrust into combat leadership upon his arrival in country.  I remember myself at 22 and am humbled at the difference in our life experiences at that time.Lanning does a great job describing the effects of combat on a leader, officer and man.  The series contains myriad leadership lessons as well as many combat episodes.  He describes a fragging threat that mimics very closely the episode Anthony Herbert described in Soldier—makes you wonder how often these threats occurred during the war.  A blue-on-blue incident.  His company narrowly avoids a massacre incident due to the leadership he instilled in the unit and his willingness to listen to his team.It’s interesting to watch Lanning’s attitude change over his tour in country.  As he writes, “…any code of personal ethics or loyalty to the Army had been far exceeded by the love, respect, and responsibility I felt for the men of Bravo.”Waiting by Linda Lanning gives a new perspective to war.  I’m not familiar with another book that covers the stress of separation from the wife’s point of view, and it is eye opening.  She describes her experiences and emotions brilliantly.  It’s interesting to see the dichotomy in reactions between Lanning’s excitement to being promoted to company commander and Linda’s anxiety over the promotion, which will keep him in harm’s way vs. a safer job in the rear. Through the course of the book Linda’s life revolves in large part around receiving letters from Lee:  the waiting for the next missive, the time lag between the date of the letter and the date of receipt and wondering if Lee is still alive, the reading between the lines of Lee’s text to interpret his mental state, her read of how his mood and combat readiness changes through the course of the tour (“The fact that Lee had told me not to worry three times within two pages told me that it was time to be hysterical.  That he had admitted his nerves were shot warned that not only should I be frantic with worry but also that he was himself worried.  His confession to being a “little screwy” meant he felt a lot screwy.”)While not a leadership lesson, Linda describes an encounter that shows the bitter irony of being a waiting, pregnant wife.  She encounters a couple, friends of her roommate, that visit to receive a loan from the roommate to cover an abortion.  Linda and the friend do their best to talk the couple out of the decision, but the couple is resolute and, in the end, they get the check.  “Oh, the irony of it all…Mister and Miss Civilian All-Star American, son and daughter of the Establishment, are going to abort their child, and the protestors call our husbands “baby killers.”  There’s something wrong with the system.”Linda also must confront the attitudes at home about the war and defend Lee’s choice to serve to those who opposed the war.  There’s a great exchange that takes place at a Thanksgiving dinner in 1969 between Linda and a guest who opposes the war.“Exactly how is it you propose to stop the war? I asked.We must persuade the soldiers to put down their weapons.  If everyone does that, then there is no more war.  We are only trying to save the soldiers,” the woman said as she cocked her head self-righteously.I looked her square in the eye and asked quietly, “Which of us at this table do you suppose has the most interest in saving soldiers?...And what do you suppose would happen to our soldiers if they simply put down their weapons?  Do you honestly think the North Vietnamese would say “Oh, what a fine idea” and then lay theirs down?  Do you not know they would just blow our men away?”The Battles of Peace describes a US Army that reminds me in a way of the movie Stripes with Bill Murray, except with no comedy and a lot more illegal drug use.  The company Lanning took over was combat ineffective.  Lanning discusses the opinion of his peer officers to David Hackworth’s decision to retire (not quit—he kept his retirement) from the Army.  They felt like he walked away from problems that he could have helped solve.  In reading through Lanning’s description of the Army in the mid-1970s it does make you wonder what a difference Hackworth could have made if he had stayed in the service.  It also makes you realize how lucky we were that the Soviet Union never made an offensive move against the West during that time.  Just one example: Lanning’s company’s ammunition stores were 60 miles away in a central depot—across several bridges that likely would have been destroyed in the first phases of any attack.Through the course of his tour in Germany, Lanning transforms his unit into one of the top performing companies in theater.  He improves their living conditions, passes IG inspections, fitness requirements, and field exercises, essentially eliminates drug use and improves morale and discipline through many tried-and-true leadership techniques—even going so far to write that none of the techniques he employed were novel or his alone; “there simply was no reason to think up new ways to command successfully.  During two hundred years of experience by American soldiers and countless centuries by men in in other armies, effective leadership techniques had already been discovered.  All one had to do to be successful in the post-Vietnam army was to follow the basics and do them well and with enthusiasm, fairness and good intentions.” Simple but not easy.I think The Battles of Peace is the jewel of the four books, although all are outstanding.  Battles reminds me of David Hackworth’s Steel My Soldier’s Hearts, except set in peacetime vs. combat.Lt. Col. Lanning is a prolific author and has written twenty-nine books of non-fiction.  From his website (http://michaelleelanning.com/index.html): “three of his military history editions, the genre for which he is best known, have been translated into a total of eleven languages in thirteen countries. He has sold more than a million copies of his works in print, audio, and e-book formats. Various editions have been included in the Military Book Club. His books have been reviewed in publications as diverse as the New York Times, Infantry Magazine, and Soldier of Fortune. The Sunday Times of London dedicated a full page in review and analysis of one edition.”Can’t recommend this four-book series highly enough.  Read them all!
