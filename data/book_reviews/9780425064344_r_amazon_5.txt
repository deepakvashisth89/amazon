"Fear is the mind-killer. Fear is the little-death that brings total obliteration. I will face my fear. I will permit it to pass over me and through me. And when it has gone past - will turn the inner eye to see its path. Where the fear has gone there will be nothing. Only I will remain."- Frank Herbert, "Dune"Dune is widely known to be one of the greatest science fiction novels ever written and has sold tens of millions of copies all over the world, in more than 20 languages. It is to science fiction what The Lord of the Rings trilogy is to fantasy, the most highly regarded works in their respective genres. Of course, Dune is not just science fiction. It includes strong elements of fantasy and contains so many important layers beneath the storyline that it has become a mainstream classic.The novel was first published in hardcover in 1965 by Chilton Books, best known for their immense auto repair novels. No other publisher would touch the book, in part because of the length of the manuscript. They felt it was far too long at 200,000 words when most novels of that day were only a quarter to a third that length. Dune would require immense printing costs and a high hardcover price for the time in excess of five dollars. No science fiction novel had ever commanded a retail price that high. But, what does the world of Dune make it so unique?•DUNE CLEARLY PROVIDES AN ALTERNATIVE TO THE FERMI PARADOX: For the unfamiliar, the Fermi paradox poses a simple question. The universe is infinitely large, which means an exceedingly high probability for other life forms to exist. So if that is the case, where are the aliens?Unlike regular science fiction material, in Dune there are no aliens. There is seldom any mention of alien life forms in either the books or the movie. The truth is, Herbert envisioned humans spreading out so far and wide in the universe that they become alien among themselves. This is beautifully depicted in both movies where the Harkonnens living on Geidi Prime look seemingly closer to an alien life form than a regular human being. Of course, the reason for this, in a nutshell, is because Geidi Prime’s “sun” is a black sun and hence causes their skin to be exceedingly pale and their heads bald. This small change by Herbert to not include aliens in his world not only emphasizes the sheer size at which the human population has grown but also is the most realistic answer to one of the most debated topics of all time.•BENE GESSERIT TRICKS SEEM LIKE “MAGIC” TO US BUT AREN’T: ￼To the average viewer (myself included on the first viewing), watching the Bene Gesserit would have immediately reminded us of “witches” and “magic”. However, the back story for their powers is far deeper. Thousands of years before the events of the movie/book, an event known as “The Butlerian Jihad” took place in which humans destroyed all “thinking machines” (machines we call “AI” today). When humans soon realized that they could not rely on machines to go about their daily businesses, schools dedicated to “unlocking” the human potential opened up and soon people began focusing best on how to hone human senses and perception to cope with the loss of the machines.Thus, the “Bene Gesserit” isn’t really a bunch of women just saying a few funny-sounding words. It is the consequence of hundreds of thousands of years of carefully selective breeding and humans’ abilities evolving over time. Most sci-fi material focuses on machines and fancy robots, often forgetting about the human aspect of evolution. While Herbert doesn’t go into super scientific detail on how these newfound abilities can be achieved, he doesn’t need to — For it would certainly be naive to think that humans would not have enhanced natural abilities thousands of years from now.•SPACE TRAVEL IS SHOWN TO BE AN ARDUOUS TASK AND NOT YOUR AVERAGE WALK DOWN THE STREET: One criticism I have of science fiction material is how easy it makes space travel out to be. Dune, however, is the opposite. Thanks to how rare it is to find spice, space travel is shown to be an expedition that can only be undertaken by the privileged.This is beautifully depicted in the first movie in one of my favorite scenes which I call “Leaving Caladan” (with an epic score by Hans Zimmer to boot). The Atreides are preparing to leave their home planet of Caladan to the harsh desert planet, Arrakis. The scene lasts for 1 minute and 37 seconds and it has zero dialogue but it is a masterful portrayal of how difficult space travel is and how daunting it is to travel between worlds. Two particular moments from this scene stand out to me.1• Paul, touching water and seeing the sunset on ￼Caladan: The obvious interpretation of this is, of course, that Paul is trying to just feel water for the last time given Arrakis has almost none of it. But it also could be symbolic as in to suggest the sun is setting on his “childhood innocence”. Leaving Caladan is the straw that breaks the camel’s back for the Atreides and forces Paul down the golden path of “Lisan Al Gaib”. ￼2• Duke Leto placing a hand on lady Jessica‘s shoulder. In just a few frames, Villeneuve captures the enormity of the journey they are about to undertake, the love between Leto and Jessica, and also foreshadows Leto’s demise by placing his face just outside the frame. ￼￼Some would say that Herbert walked a fine line between science fiction and fantasy and they may well be right but throughout the book (and indeed the movie), Herbert doesn’t stray away from the laws of thermodynamics, particularly the first law - “Energy can neither be created nor destroyed. It can only be transformed from one form to another”. This fine line allows Herbert to keep Dune within “science fiction” territory but also unique enough to distinguish itself from regular sci-fi material.Dune is set in the distant future in a feudal interstellar society descended from terrestrial humans in which various noble houses control planetary fiefs. It tells the story of young Paul Atreides, whose family reluctantly accepts the stewardship of the planet Arrakis.  While the planet is an inhospitable and sparsely populated desert wasteland, it is the only source of melange or “spice”, an enormously valuable drug that extends life and enhances mental abilities. Melange is also necessary for space navigation, which requires a kind of multidimensional awareness and foresight that only the drug provides. As melange can also be produced on Arrakis, control of the planet is a coveted and dangerous undertaking.
