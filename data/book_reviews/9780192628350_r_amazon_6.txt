The history of cancer is a story of human tragedy on the one hand and on the other of human dedication and perseverance to understand, diagnose and treat that emperor of all maladies -to quote Siddhartha Mukherjee’ book title- that still claims the lives of thousands of men, women and children every year. It is a history replete with outstanding successes and breakthroughs as well as resounding failures. Mel Greaves’ book Cancer: The Evolutionary Legacy is one of those books that tells that history and much more. However, it is not your regular, run-of-the-mill, book on the history of cancer. That history as dealt with in the book is informed by Darwin’s Theory of Evolution. It should come to no one’s surprise that Greaves selected that approach to deal with his topic.Mel Greaves is a British cancer biologist who is noted for his research into childhood leukemia. He is well-known for his interest in the role of evolutionary theory as formulated by Darwin in understanding the development of cancer. In 2013, he founded the Center for Evolution and Cancer, which is part of the Institute of Cancer Research based in London, UK. The Center is dedicated to the application of Darwinian evolutionary principles to understand why humans develop cancer and to answer such crucial questions such as what makes cancer one of the most difficult chronic diseases to treat.I would like to warn the reader upfront that in the following lines I will be presenting an unsympathetic review of Greaves’ book. Not because I don’t endorse the Darwinian Evolutionary Principles-I do even though to a certain extent. But mainly because all those evolutionists believe that the Darwinian model can be used to study and understand not only the natural world but all spheres of human activity including the social, political, economic and cultural. Here's another evolutionist who wants to explain carcinogenesis in accordance with the evolutionary model. To those believers in evolution, Darwin’s theory is a theory of everything. I do not think so. I doubt if Darwin himself would approve of the all-encompassing and sweeping manner in which his intellectual descendants employed his theory.As an evolutionist, the author believes that cancer development takes place in accordance with the Darwinian evolutionary principles of natural selection and survival of the fittest. According to the author, since cancer is capable of surviving, or overcoming, selective pressures imposed by cellular regulatory mechanisms that control cellular growth and proliferation, it can be considered a legacy of evolution. So according to the author, the cancer cell is favored by the evolutionary process to survive since it is fit by its very ability to adapt to all cellular mechanisms aimed at suppressing it. However, this kind of interpretation of cancer poses a difficulty to the Darwinian framework. True, cancer does overcome, and sometimes subordinate, all sorts of cellular mechanisms designed to keep cellular growth under strict control but, paradoxically, the dominance of cancer results in many cases in the demise of the host along with the cancer cells.The author acknowledges this difficulty. He writes: “Ultimately, if they (cancer cells) succeed in dissemination and resist all therapy, they destroy their only environment-the patient. Dumb parasites?” Well, they are not dumb if we don’t look at cancer development through the evolutionary lens, but as a mal-adaptation phenomenon that wreaks havoc upon the human body. How could cancer develop through an evolutionary process-in the Darwinian sense- and then instead of survival it brings about its own destruction?The author’s zealousness for the evolutionary theory is very clear from his extensive use of such words as evolution, evolutionary, selective pressure and other terms popularized by the Darwinian theory of evolution by natural selection throughout the whole book. In explaining any aspect of cancer development, the author has to attribute that development to evolutionary forces. Sometimes this becomes a real distraction. You are trying to understand cancer and its mechanisms of uncontrolled growth and at the same time you are struggling to figure out how this is considered an evolutionary event? What is the evidence? It is not enough to say, for example, that millions of years ago, and as a result of evolution, a certain gene appeared on the scene and became responsible for the development of cancer without presenting proof to corroborate that claim.The book contains a lot of speculation and conjectural reconstructions that the author admits they are "untestable explanations" (146). Generally speaking, any story of origins, especially origins that go way, way back in history, is usually high on speculation and very short on facts and solid evidence. For example, the author talks about telomeres, which are chromosomal endings that are chipped off bit by bit every time the cell divides; he then asks how unicellular organisms, and our own germ cells-cells that produce sperms and eggs- for that matter, divide indefinitely when it is well known that once those telomeres are completely eaten away the cell dies?The author simply answers: “This problem was solved early on in the evolution of unicellular organisms by a gene encoding enzyme called telomerase, that could replace missing ends with each round of DNA copying.” (94) Early on? When exactly? How do we know that? What is the evidence? Why can’t we counter argue, without any proof or evidence just like the author did, that the enzyme has been in place the minute those organisms popped up into existence?There are many of those factoids in the book. The author couches them in words such as “probably”, “most likely” or “presume.” The latter the author uses when he tries to explain how parasites developed mechanisms that enabled them to become infectious; they did that “by exploiting blood-sucking insect vectors or by adopting…a very robust extracorporeal stage (muddy waters and the like)” (98) As an evolutionary theory enthusiast, the author goes on to say: “This, we presume, took a few million years of trial-and-error evolution.” Again, how do we know that? What proof do we have for that? Why few millions and not many?In chapter 13, the author puts forward a confusing argument for the explanation of cancer development, or evolution as the author insists on telling us. He maintains that cancer can be thought of as an “evolutionary penalty” (111) meted out to “two essential characteristics of successful-mind the word successful-complex organisms” (111). The first of these features is the body’s capacity for sustained regeneration and proliferation for survival purposes coupled with the ability of stem cells-repair cells if you like-to migrate freely via the lymphatic and vascular systems to tissues in need of that repair through the processes proliferation and regeneration.The second feature is one that the author views as an “evolutionary advantage”-I am not sure why the author does not consider the first of those features such an advantage as well- and that is DNA “copying and repair” (111). Let’s recapitulate this confusing, and confused, argument. The evolutionary process punishes successful-fit to survive-organisms by allowing two of their features-one of which is bestowed upon those organisms by that same evolutionary mechanism-go awry.But the confusion, if not contradiction, does not stop there. The author goes on to say that natural selection-big evolutionary principle-enables organisms to develop restraining mechanisms to “accommodate and severely limit” the life-long risk for cancer development. Of course, these risks are a result of the penalty directed at the successful organism’s two features mentioned above and, to remind the reader, one of which is granted to that organism by that same principle of natural selection. But those restraining mechanisms, alas, do go wrong as well, leading to tumorigenesis. I am not sure how the author has been able to pull off such an argument.I find the book’s premise totally indefensible. For every argument that the author proffers for understanding certain aspects of cancer basics, one can counter with only one argument which is as plausible, if not more. The author calls this one argument the “mechanical view”, which states that “cancer is solely a consequence of inherent design faults in a rather sophisticated machine.” (111). In philosophy, as well as science, there is rule of thumb called Occam’s razor. This rule can be summarized as such: if two models or theories explain a certain phenomenon equally well, the one with the least assumptions is the preferred model. In other words, the simplest explanation is the best explanation. To look at cancer as a failure of cellular mechanisms of regulation and control is a much more reasonable approach to the understanding of the disease than the pretentiousness of the evolutionists whose arguments sometimes verge on the metaphysical.What really irked me about this book is the author’s relentless need to remind himself, and the reader, that he is offering an understanding of cancer from an evolutionary perspective. He does that ad nauseam. Let me give one example. In talking about the relationship between human lifestyle and cancer development-what he dubs the social ratchet-he writes: “The [social] ratchet is only effective because the biological base upon which it operates is intrinsically error-prone and equipped with cancerous credentials-for sound evolutionary reasons as we’ve seen” [emphasis added] (118). But the most important question that should be asked: what value can one hope to get from looking at cancer from an evolutionary vantage point? How can this perspective help in designing better treatments? Understanding cancer requires looking closely at its molecular underpinning regardless of whether the cancer cell conforms to Darwin’s evolutionary model.This book is not for the lay reader who wants to understand cancer and its development. There are better books out there that can do the job more competently. The book that I highly recommend, the one that really got me interested in pursuing the history of cancer more deeply, is Siddhartha Mukherjee’s The Emperor of All Maladies: A Biography of Cancer. Mukherjee offers a sweeping history of cancer in a highly readable and enjoyable style. There is also Natalie Angier’s book Natural Obsessions: Striving to Unlock the Deepest Secrets of the Cancer Cell. This book is rather limited in scope in terms of both subject and time period. It is focused on telling the story of research of oncogenes-genes that when mutated precipitate tumorigenesis-done during the eighties and nineties of the past century.
