This book has three stories: 'Mudd's Women,' 'I, Mudd,' and an original story 'The Business, As Usual, During Altercations.''Mudd's Women' is an interesting story adapted from the episode from the original series. Like the episode, this story doesn't have any real threat; that threat wouldn't be Harry Mudd, who just comes off as a pathetic liar that causes trouble. The 'Venus drug' that he gives the three women--to enhance their beauty--he is 'selling' is confusing, since at one point it seems the drug is working...but all one of the women had to do was 'believe' she was beautiful. (The clichéd idea that beauty comes from within, and not always physically; however, that still doesn't explain the effect when Mudd gives the drug to the girls. So, was the drug just enhancing what they--the girls--thought of themselves? Why did the women--particularly the character Ruth McHuron--change from 'less' attractive to 'more' attractive in front of the characters towards the end of the story?)'I, Mudd,' also an adaptation of a classic Trek episode, has more at stake: Androids want to control the Enterprise crew in order to learn human emotions. Harry Mudd just happens to be on the planet that directed them to look outside their planet.'I, Mudd' is a fun, light story...but it sometimes gets confusing with the transitions, as we never know if characters are in the same room, or if they (and the actions that take place) are in another room or area.The final story,'The Business, As Usual, During Altercations,' is a sequel to 'I, Mudd.' The Enterprise crew discovers that scheduled deliveries to Starfleet fueling depots are not being made. That leads to Harry Mudd.This final story is another interesting read, but there are various grammatical errors, and redundancies that could have been taken care of before publishing. (One example that comes to mind is during the trial of Harry Mudd; his charges are being read to him, and it is said it took two days. However, as I read it, it probably took me under a minute). Also, like 'I, Mudd,' the author has the tendency to describe the actions of characters, then have them say some dialog, and we don't get a sense of their whereabouts; there are no transitions that tell us the characters are.There is also a weird scene in which McCoy puts Chekov into hypnosis because he and Kirk feel the navigator can put himself in Mudd's shoes, due to being swayed by lovely androids in 'I, Mudd.' This hypnosis has Chekov acting like a historical tyrant at various points in the story.The author even has Spock smiling at one point during Mudd's trial.I think the book would have been a keeper with a strong edit; and with that strong edit, it would have been interesting to see if the author could have worked in the story 'Mudd's Passion,' an episode from the animated series.
