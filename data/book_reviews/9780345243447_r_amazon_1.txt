I’ve read now this book as I had wanted to read the short story that has inspired 2001; The Sentinel, had therefore read Arthur C. Clarke’s short story collection The Sentinel, and had afterwards started to read the other short stories in this collection.The first short story, Rescue Mission, is actually a fun little story where the aliens rush to Earth that will be destroyed within few hours by the Sun going Supernova. This plot devise would then be used again in "The Songs of Distant Earth”, the only book by Arthur C. Clarke that I had read previously in about 2005. The aliens thus try to save as many of the indigenous inhabitants as possible, only, they are not able to find any of them. Lost for ideas, they realise that one giant observatory had had its antennas and parabolic dishes targeted into seemingly empty space. Following the signal, the aliens find many ships that try with sub-light speed to escape. They are rescued, hence the name of the story, and all would have pointed to a happy end, had not the last sentence hinted to the fact that in twenty years, the aliens would come to regret the mission. Nothing ever came of it; the story was never continued.The second short story in my edition then was Guardian Angel. It’s a good short story about aliens coming to Earth and forcing its inhabitants to keep peace with each other. Worldwide peace and prosperity ensure but some humans are resentful of be told what they have to do. They kidnap the head of the UN, the liaison that is the only human who talks to Karellen, the head of the overlord masters. But also this plan fails. In the end, the overlords promise to show themselves after fifty years, taking away ammunition from the rebels that used the overlord’s unwillingness to show themselves as argument that they were up to no good. They story moves to its final revelation and … I didn’t get it. Who were the overlords representing? A long-barbed tail is all we got. Happily, nowadays I can google it, I try to imagine who I would have felt before the internet, and I found that this story was actually used Arthur C. Clarke the first quarter for Childhood’s End. And that the punchline might have been added by James Blish. There might actually have been a depiction in the original publication of the story that might have made it clearer. But in this edition of the story on my Kindle, I simply didn’t get the end. Thus, I read this book.After its first quarter, which was basically Guardian Angel, the story progress here. Humanity eventually accepts the benevolent rulership of the overlords. Still, what are they up to? On the one hand, we have here a person who is still resentful that the overlords that basically stifled all spacefaring. He consequently makes up the plan to travel on one of the overlord’s supply ships as a blind passenger. The overlords fly at sub-light speed, a refreshing fact in science fiction, and it would take him eighty years if the overlords would directly send him back home. On the other hand, there is also another development with all the children of humanity. Suddenly on one day, all the children between new-born and 10 start to become telekinetic. They interact with each other and start becoming one organism. Hence the task for the overlords to safeguard their development.The rest of humanity apparently have no desire anymore to do much else with their lives, some kill themselves at once and some just entertain themselves until they die. This description of the “Last days of Humans” before the “End of the World” reminded me again strongly at "The Songs of Distant Earth” where we have the identical scene. In the end, our space traveller returns to Earth as the last human alive. And, against all my expectations, the overlords do explain how humans had come to recognise their appearance so that they had to hide it for the first fifty years. The explanation even made sense, so that I was quite satisfied in this regard.This was an “old fashioned” book and I mean this as a compliment. Star Trek and Star Wars have somehow changed the science fiction sphere so that nowadays we only envision science fiction as people in a star ship flying faster than light and visiting the planet of the week or fight against an evil empire or rebelling robots. At least that’s my impression but I read a lot of Star Trek books in my youth. Here, we have just the humans on Earth and what a world-changing development means for them. Well thought through and executed.If I would have to add some criticism it would be that the story had quite some plot holes that kept me thinking after the end. For example, the book states that after the under 10-year-olds had become removed from humanity, nobody wanted to have children anymore and so repopulate Earth. But why? Surely, the eleven-year-olds would ten years later not to be so traumatised as to not have any children? Moreover, I also came to recognise that Arthur C. Clarke seems to have reused a lot of plot points in his stories and books. I have already mentioned the end of the world and the last humans on earth idea. I also heard that the space elevator makes more than one entry in his oeuvre. And here, I felt reminded a lot of 2010: "We are helping to bring something new and wonderful into the world.” and "..it is something wonderful, and you will have created it." Had this statement been true in 2010, I guess in this context, one might argue if it was really so wonderful for humanity.All in all, this was a worthwhile read, already based on the fact that this book is a classic and I had to read it one day. I had already read 2010 in the past to see if the differences to the movie were really quite small; they were.
