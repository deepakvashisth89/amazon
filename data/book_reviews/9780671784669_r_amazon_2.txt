Graham Greene wrote "entertainments," and he wrote them during the Cold War era - "Our Man in Havana" was published in 1958.  But while many modern readers sniff at Greene (he commits the perceived modern sins of patriarchy, snubbing the Third World, etc.), you have to appreciate what a revolutionary writer he was.  Greene injected cynicism with humor in a completely new way, and he captured the inherent silliness at the heart of the Cold War's cynicism.Greene knew the world of intelligence, bureaucracy, and nationalism from the inside.  And he understood that ambition, pleasing one's superiors, and looking good in reports were forces just as powerful as patriotism, honor, and justice.  With "Our Man in Havana," Greene illustrates how crazy all this can be . . . where a vacuum salesman in Panama can spin a few little lies to please distant bureaucrats and unleash sinister forces that are only too happy to commit a little murder to advance their own ends.What makes Greene so much fun is that he skewers everyone . . . even the Little Guy who emerges as the novel's hero gets more than his fair share of shade from Greene's savage typewriter.  But he truly shines when making fun of the men (and they are all men) of power and influence.  To Greene, these men are no better than those who they command - they've just been around for a lot longer and are even more motivated to keep bad news under wraps.So why only four stars?  It's probably just that I haven't read enough Greene yet - his writing has an unusual rhythm to it that I haven't quite figured out, and so I had to go back quite a few times to decipher a paragraph or line of dialogue to figure out just what was going on.  It's a bit like reading a screenplay without the stage directions.But this is a classic of spy fiction, and it should be read.Read moreRead less
