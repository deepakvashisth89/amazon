I came across Alison Havens’ work via an online essay she has written about the Ancient Greek Muses. Her loose interpretation of these iconic figures at least produced a convincing metaphor for her oeuvre — she is also an academic teacher of creative writing. I read on, becoming only slightly disenchanted at her introduction of Christian symbolism — which of course was an anathema to the Ancient Greek religion of which the Muses were an instrumental part. Undeterred, and out of curiosity, I flicked through the reviews of her novels looking for one that didn’t ring in the bells of christianity and felt defeated until I came upon Lifestory. I quickly ordered a copy and settled down to read, only to find bibles on the very first page again!This you might say is not a big deal — they could pop up anywhere it seems: but the deeper problem is the way Ms Habens seems to rely on the rather basic device of alluding to one thing by mentioning another. This is rather sweet at first, inferring the intelligible from the material and vice-versa which eventually takes the turn of disembodied spirit inhabiting the physical — whether in her stories or in an autobiographic sense of creativity within being the real author rather than Ms Habens.The idea of course is perfectly valid — as far as it goes. It’s a very useful one — and hopefully for which she is fully recognised as a teacher of creative writing. Yet it is one thing to write beautiful sentences and another to use those sentences to produce a truly great story. As another reviewer has noted, the basic plot here has been done better elsewhere. One might hope that the Muse of Epic Poetry, one of the nine that inspired the great stories of Old, might allow Ms Habens to climb a little beyond the turn of deft phrases and truly ride to more Olympian heights. Alas, without real dedication to something greater, the ascent would continue to labour like a one-trick pony.
