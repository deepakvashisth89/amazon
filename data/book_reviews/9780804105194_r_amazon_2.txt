One wonders what the reaction today would be to Fong and the Indians, Paul Theroux’s 1968 second novel about an indigent Chinese storekeeper trying to eke out a living in a recently independent East African country, in which the very caucasian male author writes so satirically about a cast of mostly non-white characters (in fact, there is only one white character of any significance). It’s a book in which black characters generally come off as simple-minded and primitive, Southern Asians (mostly of Indian extraction) as reflexively corrupt and the Chinese—essentially just Fong and his wife—as clueless and naive.To be fair, the one white character, an American no less, doesn’t fare much better, and neither does his black American colleague. Theroux shows himself here as an equal opportunity satirist in the vein of Black Mischief-era Evelyn Waugh or A House for Mr. Biswas-era V.S. Naipaul. Theroux spent considerable time in Africa and clearly writes from first-hand knowledge even if he is not a native. As I’ve never subscribed to the notion that one is only permitted to write about one’s own demographic, I found his narrative mostly quite enjoyable.There were a few dead spots and episodes that went on a bit too long but Theroux was still learning his craft at this point.
