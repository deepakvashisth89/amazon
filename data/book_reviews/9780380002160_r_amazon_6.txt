In many ways, the movie "A Girl Named Sooner" is a masterpiece, what with the brilliant performances of the actors and the sets that recreated the Depression-era 1930s so perfectly. Unfortunately, I have banned the movie from being shown in my house. Why? Because I can't abide watching Sooner's beloved Bird die, pelted to death by a rain of stones thrown by a pack of ignorant, bullying children one of whom is Sooner herself, the little girl he'd grown to love and trust. Naturally, Sooner herself doesn't realize what she is doing since she'd never had a friend before, and she's merely copying the actions of the other kids to be "part of the gang." It wasn't until Sooner saw Bird's broken body on the ground that she realized what she had done. Elizabeth McHenry, the distant woman who reluctantly agrees to take Sooner into her home, makes matters even worse by accusing Sooner of killing her own pet. My heart is as irreparably shattered today as it was then, remembering seeing poor little Bird die a slow and agonizing death at the hand of his guardian. When I wonder what Bird was thinking as the stones ravaged him, my heart breaks and I cry oceans of tears.I realize people reading this might believe I'm a looney-tune, an overly sentimental fool who becomes inconsoluble over the death of a puny bird from a fictional story. Perhaps. But I do not apologize for my passion for all animals, nor do I for my insatiable anguish over incidents of cruelty, whether fictional or factual. Bird reminds me of the birds I cared for in the avian nursery at our local animal rehab center. They were wildlife birds in their infancy and juvenile stages that were too sick or injured to return to their natural habitat. I didn't realize that I could love so much until I began caring for these birds. Each had a distinctive personality all his/her own, and I delighted in witnessing they're playfulness, naughtiness and ability to tease. Most intriguing was their complete trust in us volunteers as we fed, cleaned and administered medicine to them. When I remember what had been done to Bird, I can't help but fear that something similar will happen to one of my birds once they've been released.If I could be granted a wish, I would wish for permission to rewrite the dreadful plight of Bird in the hands of Sooner. I know that in at least some states, holding a wild animal in captivity or keeping one as a pet is against the law. Sooner would lose Bird not by means of death but upon the order of the town game warden or forest ranger. In my rewrite Bird can fly, and I would have Sooner take Bird to the edge of the woods behind the McHenry house and release him. When Sooner sees how eagerly Bird takes to the air, flying deep into the forest carefree and joyous, Sooner becomes convinced that Bird wanted to get away from her b/c he didn't "love" her anymore. Sooner had been spending what time she used to spend with Bird going to school, studying her numbers and letters, and teaching herself how to jump rope and play the games that her schoolmates took delight in. Her "neglect" of Bird caused him to want to go away from her. As in the book, Sooner lies on her bed and strokes the top of the now-empty perch specially made for Bird. Both McHenrys try to console her, even Elizabeth who never understood Sooner's devotion to a wild bird just like she didn't understand a great deal about Sooner. But the little girl is inconsoluble, and at sunrise the following morning, she runs from the McHenry home to face her punishment at the harsh and hostile hands of Ole Mam, the grandmother from whom Sooner was taken away. The rest of the story would pretty much remain the same, with a distraught Elizabeth searching for the little girl she now realizes she loves so much while her depressed husband turns to the McHenrys' pretty neighbor for understanding and solace.Another significant change I'd make to the original story is to have Bird return to the McHenry home. Ridiculous as this may seem, it is an established fact that wild birds who have spent most of their lives being home-raised by humans are apt to return to these dwellings where they received love, food, and care. Bird would be no different in this context. Elizabeth sees him perched on the branch of a tree outside her kitchen window, flapping his wings and calling. Her joy at his return brings her to tears and brings she and her husband close together with new understanding and compassion. When the story concludes with Elizabeth meeting up with Sooner at the Fair, she'd inform Sooner over hamburgers (with lots of ketchup!) that Bird had come back and was missing her so much. After a tearful departure from the brash and brazen Ole Mam who was up to something illegal at the Fair (can't remember what, probably selling moonshine), Sooner agrees to have the McHenrys adopt her as their own. Then, as they enter the house, there is Bird on his special perch waiting for Sooner to come to him. The End.I realize my rewrite sounds sappy and sentimental, lacking the impact of the author's brilliant story in which Bird's death is the catalyst in getting Elizabeth to understand where she went wrong and resulting with her growing closer to Sooner with a renewed sense of love and appreciation. But for me, changing Bird's plight and ending the story on a happy note is my way of purging the pain and anguish I feel toward the real story.Three cheers for catharsis!!Read moreRead less
