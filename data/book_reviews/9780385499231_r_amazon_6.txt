Many people have been comparing the work of Mr. Hemon to that of Vladimir Nabokov. Frankly, the comparison does not fit. Nabokov's work, while sometimes cryptic, generally displays a more traditional cohesiveness than  the works of modern central European writers. The work of Mr. Hemon, if it  must be compared to any predecessor, should be considered closer to that of  Danilo Kis. Both exhibit the same structuralist philosophy - abandoning  traditional chapters for numbered paragraphs, displaying a disregard for  the connection of one paragraph to another. The idea being that the work is  independent of both time and space, existing as a thing in itself, entirely  disconnected from anything else. Naturally, such writing can easily become  tedious, academic, and unreadable. Yet Mr. Hemon's The Question of Bruno  does not fall into this trap.All critical theory voodoo put aside, Mr.  Hemon's work holds an appeal in its stories. Occasionally odd, many of  these narratives have the ability to hold the reader's attention. Perhaps  it is their occasional underlying philosophical message or their bluntness  in depicting the world that he knew transformed into a living Hell for all  who remained. In the end, what remains are the vivid images conveyed by Mr.  Hemon's prose.As would be expected, to judge this book by traditional  standards would be to reject it, as it fails on most traditional levels.  Yet Mr. Hemon's ability to capture an idea or an image is uncanny. On this  point alone the book cannot be ignored. It leaves the reader with a strange  feeling, having found the book interesting but unable to fully or  coherently explain why. The literary avant garde may well praise the book  with collections of critical terms, and, as usual, they will be preaching  to the choir and mostly ignored by the general public. However, the writing  of Mr. Hemon is too well crafted to be relegated to academic obscurity.  This would be a disservice to the book as it should be read by a wider  audience than just cloistered English professors.In the end, the book is  recommended with a considerable caveat emptor. If you undertake to read the  book, read it all (it's not that long) before considering it. By doing so,  you may very well be surprised by its effect. If you truly have no taste  for structuralist writing (and generally, who could be blamed) it might be  best to pass by this book as it will just annoy you. However, if you have  any sense of literary adventure, it is worth the time spent to read this  book.
