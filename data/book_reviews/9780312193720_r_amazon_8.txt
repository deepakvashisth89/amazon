This is a typical picaresque novel in which one damn thing happens after another and in which a rogue tries to survive and maybe even make a name for his hapless self, as all rogues do or are wont to do in any picaresque novel.  The atypical aspect of this picaresque story is that Donleavy makes it end happily.As a typical rogue, Stephen O'Kelly'O has no conscience and his thinking capacity is paper-thin.  He marries a young woman without knowing anything about her besides her body and her money, and he thinks that because she's rich, what's hers will be his, too, but like everything he relies upon in the story, the foundation is not valid, not even true or trustworthy when he goes to be with his wife's mother who is loaded like a Rockefeller.  The father-in-law cuts the daughter's inheritance off at the time of marriage without telling his son-in-law, and the mother-in-law wants him in her life but only as a sex-for-hire gigolo.No one in the novel is likeable, not even the rogue who is at the center of the story.  As a pianist, he's not even believable, which is Donleavy's fault.  The only positive contribution the rogue makes is just one minuet, and while the rogue’s references to music are numerous, none of them deal with piano compositions.Donleavy decided to use gerunds as sentences relentlessly throughout the novel, so that with no chapter breaks, there is a kind of stressful, breathless, rough intensity to the rogue's adventures, which just enhances the stress of reading this novel.  Walkng through the city.  Climbing the stairs.  Eyeing the girl on the corner.  Hearing the bleats of the emergency siren. Is tiresome, like listening to the chopstick song on piano for several hours.Finally, the story contains a few not-so-believable sob stories as well, deliberately created to pull on the heartstrings of the reader since the rogue's conflicts don't generate consistent emotional identification for the reader. Not a good choice that Donleavy chose to try to make his dislikeable characters make the reader feel sorry for them.  Bad art.Read moreRead less
