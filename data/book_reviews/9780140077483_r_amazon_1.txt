I read most of this work by Howard Frank Mosher over the long Thanksgiving weekend when I was at the Chiricahua National Monument in extreme southeastern Arizona. This work is composed of six short stories and one novella, which lends its name to the entire collection. Ain’t America grand, and a place that can still fill one with awe, I thought, despite the relentless chronicle of factional point-scoring, as chronicled by the coastal media? There remain so many still unexplored nooks and crannies, still retaining some regional quirks in the face of national homogenization. The vast majority of people in Albuquerque, a mere six hours to the north, have never heard of Chiricahua.  And to stay there overnight, one must camp, 30 designated sites only, with the closest commercial accommodations an hour away. At Chiricahua I am reading about another extreme, the northeast corner of Vermont, simply called “The Kingdom” by fellow Vermonters. Wikipedia attributes the name to a speech given by Governor (and Senator) George Aiken in 1949. Mosher goes much, much further back, commencing in the fall of 1759, when the first white man, Twilight Anderson, passed through the area. In the War of Independence, the border area was playing both sides against the middle, selling the pines to be made into masts to both Britain and America. It was Twilight’s son, George, who purportedly declared independence of the Kingdom territory for three years, from 1810 to 1813.Mosher is an excellent storyteller, who backs and fills, connecting the dots into a memorable picture of the area. Noel Lord and his nominal housekeeper Bangor are two characters that will hopefully be forever etched into my memory. Noel was born in the mid-19th century. The story is set in 1927. Noel lost his hand in an accident; a hook has replaced it, one that he uses to his advantage from time to time. Bangor must now gum her food, speaks of herself in the third person, and is a very plump shadow of her “glory days” when she undertook, for her at least, the pleasant labor of keeping the men happy, an attitude in marked contrast to her sisters who also toiled in the trade. Noel is faithful, in his way, and does not place her on the discard pile.They live in an alternate reality to that of so many plugged-in Americans today, who have no idea what the phase of the moon is. The ways of the natural world are so near… experience tells Noel which way the panther will break out of the woods (Mosher provides the regional pronunciation of “painter” for the animal). Noel is cantankerous in defending the old ways, from the city slickers who want to build a dam that will flood his property. He has his dreams to go to, summed up in one word, a bigger, less soiled version of the Kingdom, called “Oregon.” Overall, a poignant, even heart-breaking tale of devotion and accommodation in old age.The six short stories will not be as deeply etched but are all good. One takeaway was to disabuse the other 49 “staters” of their traditional colorful postcard view of Vermont: Hey, for one week!... and then the frost comes, and it is all brown. Bangor does her own disabusing in mentioning the eight months of the year that snow is on the ground.I want to thank a former fellow Amazon reviewer, who still regrettably shields his recommendations with a bushel basket, for providing this excellent recommendation. I’d love to see The Kingdom, even in its brown period, before I shed this mortal coil.I already purchased Mosher’s “North Country,” now safely ensconced on my Kindle. Regrettably, his books now are his legacy. The mortal coil departed in 2017.For those northern flowing rivers, going the very short way to Canada, 5-star, plus.Read moreRead less
