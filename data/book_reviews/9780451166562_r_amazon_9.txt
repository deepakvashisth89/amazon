Adler, Warren.  The War of the Roses‘Love, a temporary insanity, curable by marriage.’ Warren Adler’s The Wars of the Roses attests to the truth of the first part of this adage, but certainly not the second.  Marriage itself is at the heart of the brutal internecine warfare between Oliver and Barbara Rose over who owns their beautiful house in  Kalorama, ‘tucked behind famed Embassy Row,’ Washington.  The house is packed with treasures and convenience toys, has a splendid view over the White House and other monuments and houses personal momentos such as Oliver’s collection of Staffordshire china, not to mention his garaged Ferrari, almost never used, and his daughter Eve’s brand new Honda.  Goods aplenty, then, but almost out of nothing there develops a huge hole in their marriage.  Barbara want a divorce; Oliver is stunned.The problem seems to be that Barbara, who once declared she would die for Oliver, now finds him possessive and controlling.  She wants to expand her career as a hostess, arranges parties, has friendly contact with the embassies, organises (disastrously) a party of carefully selected collected big-wigs.  When she discovers that ‘love lies’ she blames her mother, Oliver, her successful lawyer husband, but not her acquisitive self. The wars of the Roses ultimately result in a booby-trapped house, the destruction of each other’s pets and the turning off of mains services.  Caught in the crossfire are Ann, the child minder, and Eve and Josh, the innocent victims who are sent to camp during the heat of battle. In fact the adults behave like angry children while the children conduct themslves as responsible adults.I found the book compelling reading, as will anyone who has been through the process of separation and division of property. The ending is a little sad, but appropriate.  Adler’s dialogue throughout is sharp and his insights into human psychology objective and penetrating.  If there is a moral to be gained it’s obviously that people are more important than things.  DH Lawrence would have approved; in some ways the book is a send-up of bourgeous values.Read moreRead less
