I'd been thinking about reading some of the beginnings of science fiction, and this seemed like a good place to start.  It's an interesting read.  Clearly it's very much in the realm of speculative fiction, taking an unknown and postulating a story inside guesses of what it might contain, with the refreshing difference that the world of the unknown is one that's located "down" instead of the usual "up".I was surprised to find, then, that despite the accolades that Verne has earned for over a century, I actually feel he's more successful as a scientist (or, more accurately, science enthusiast) than as a writer.  The characters never evolve from more than sketches - the timid protagonist, the obsessed and driven professor, their ever-taciturn Icelandic guide - and the plot really is as simple as "We will go under the earth for a long time, and then come back up."  It's notable that the most interesting parts of the story, to me, were those that were the most realistic: The amount of time it takes them to first plan their journey, then travel across the sea and land before reaching their entrance to below; as well as a scene later on when they're far underground, and the main character not only becomes separated from the others, and then lost, but also loses his only source of light!  This encounter was far more starkly terrifying, for instance, than the later scene when they're crossing the enormous underground ocean and narrowly escape a pair of giant, battling sea creatures.Other slight detractors were the somewhat unbelievable length of time spent under the ground (you're carrying on your backs provisions to last three men for MONTHS?), as well as the bewildering fact that the explorers keep going down, down, down, with nary a thought mentioned as to how they might get back, or whether they'll even have enough supplies for the return journey - which one would naturally assume to take as long as the descent.  The fact that (in the end) it does not strikes one as both wildly implausible in its execution, and a bit of a cop-out.Still, for all its faults the novel is rarely boring, and you really can see the beginnings of an entire genre in its wake.
