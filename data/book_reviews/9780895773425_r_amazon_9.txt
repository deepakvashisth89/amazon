I think Pasternak is a literary descendant of Chekhov, although they are a little different from each other. Chekhov despised people who were ambitious to show off , and so strong was the feeling that the thought of wanting to be famous never came to him, but Pasternak, who lived in a more competitive society than Chekhov, did not find it so easy to resist the temptation, and so he had to contain his desire to be famous by writing “It is not seemly to be famous” in his well-known poem. It seems to me as if he said the phrase to himself rather than to the reader.In the struggle against his desire, I suppose, Pasternak had Chekhov in his mind as the kind of person he would very much like to be.The following is Zhivago’s dialogue indicating it, which I personally like very much.“What I have come to like best in the whole of Russian literature is the childlike Russian quality of Pushkin and Chekhov, their shy unconcern with such high-sounding matters as the ultimate purpose of mankind or their own salvation.
