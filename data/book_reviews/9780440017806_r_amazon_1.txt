Deadeye, as the book description says, is about the death of innocence, and once you get into the book, it's clear that the book is also about our lives, and how we try to weave stories out of them. Some of us weave amazing stories. Others of us are frauds. A few of us will hurl ourselves into some kind of drama for a plot point. And yet others of us are neuters like the main character, Rudy--we do one or two "big" things, and then the rest of our life is epilogue.Rudy, a pharmacist, ends his story surprisingly early when he makes the fateful choice to shoot a Springfield in a random direction, ending a woman named Metzger and her unborn child. From there, it's either downhill or endless flat plains, depending on how you want to view it.And yet there's something compelling about Rudy, his "neuter" nature, and his failed play, Katmandu. The narrative voice is strong. You leave the book feeling that, yes, what you've read is almost all epilogue, but it's meaningful epilogue. Well, as meaningful as an epilogue can get.Ending in his obliterated hometown where there will, perhaps, be a ghostly aviator forever looking for his parachute, Deadeye closes with this: "You want to know something? We are still in the Dark Ages. The Dark Ages--they haven't ended yet."A slice of Deadeye is commentary on post-WWII and -Korean America, where it may be perfectly sensible to obliterate an entire town using a neutron bomb. (Although the government maintains the whole thing is an accident.) And it's here that Rudy reflects that nations might have a story, too, or at least try to tell one for themselves. Maybe that's a mistake, he contends.Now, whether these Dark Ages are part of the story or just the epilogue before the last page, Deadeye leaves us to decide for ourselves.A very sad book with a deceptively light narrative tone.
