I read this book because I wanted to understand why Saul Bellow disappeared so quickly from the American literary consciousness after his death in 2005. He was one of a handful of North American writers to receive the Nobel Prize in the 20th century, but unlike so many of the big names from the 50s and 60s whose influence persists to this day (Vonnegut, Salinger, Heller, Philip Roth, Nabokov and yes, Dylan), Bellow disappeared almost without a trace. Occasionally an overly-ambitious MFA will name-drop him as an early influence, but even then Bellow sounds like a sort of curio from a distant time and country.Why?Seize the Day provides us with an excellent case study. All of the characters are unlikeable, the lazy slob Wilhelm, the grifter Tamkin, the a-hole father Dr. Adler, the wife Margaret. Only Wilhelm’s children are spared Bellow’s cold eye, though they hardly appear in the novella at all. Because of this, Bellow fails to engage the average reader. After all, who wants to read a book about a bunch of nasty bourgeois, most of whom live in an apartment/hotel in New York?Not me.The novella is also poorly written. Compare Bellow’s flat dialogue to the masterfully rendered conversations found in Kingsley Amis or JD Salinger. Do it. Bellow, on the other hand, writes like an academic who wants to be a writer. He has so little feel for the American idiom that he cannot decide whether Wilhelm should call his father “Dad” or “Father” in conversation, though in fact Americans almost always use the moniker “Dad,” and never use both.108-109“Yes, Dad, I’m afraid I trusted him.”Dr. Adler surrendered his arm to the masseur, who was using wintergreen oil.“Trusted! And got taken?”“I’m afraid I kind of –“ Wilhelm glanced at the masseur, but he was absorbed in his work. He probably did not listen to conversations. “I did. I might as well say it. I should have listened to you.”“Well, I won’t remind you how often I warned you. It must be very painful.”“Yes, Father, it is.”(implausibly, Bellow actually suggests that masseurs do not listen to conversations. Not that they maintain a code of confidentiality, but that they do not listen at all!)The entire text is permeated with this sort of stilted nonsense; it can be found on practically every page.44“You’re probably right. I ought to try it one of these days. Yesterday, late in the afternoon, my head was about to bust and I just had to have a little air, so I walked around the Reservoir, and I sat down for a while in a playground. It rests me to watch the kids play potsy and skiprope.”Can you imagine if we could have forced Clemens to read this, seven decades after Huckleberry Finn? Americans do not “play skiprope” they simply jump rope. He watched the kids jump rope. And, no, it does not “rest me,” it “relaxes me.” Thanks, just had to get that off my chest.Paradoxically, Bellow is constantly at pains to illustrate his considerable gifts. For example, in order to “show” that Maurice Venice is from a lower class Bellow offers up the following gem: “It was about fifteen months ago right in this identical same office when I saw a beautiful thing in the paper.”See that? “identical same” A redundancy! My my, Bellow is a clever fellow indeed!And while he does offer up some wonderful descriptions, even here his writing often falls flat in the most curious ways. “On Broadway it was still bright afternoon and the gassy air was almost motionless under the leaden spokes of sunlight….”What exactly did Bellow have in mind when he wrote “gassy air?” Does it have something to do with flatulence?And then there are the “leaden spokes of sunlight.” How could it be? A gray sky, of course, can be leaden. But sunlight? Hmmm….I could go on, but Saul Bellow bores me terribly, and I am at pains to limit the amount of my life that I devote to his outrages. In its entirety, this novella comes across as irredeemably “square,” to use the jargon of that time. A sort of dull, earnest study of several people you don’t give a damn about.To summarize: If you want to write forgettable prose introduce unpleasant characters, stilted dialogue and awkward usage.It might even get you the Nobel Prize.Read moreRead less
