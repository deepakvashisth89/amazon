The author does a good job telling of the military espionage by US servicemen who betrayed their country: the terrible damage that can occur because of it, the inherent difficulties in investigation, building a case for the prosecution, and the consequences for those who choose to sell out their country for money. Unlike in the 1930s, when ideology provided the motivation for so many spies, including President Roosevelt's administration  who was saturated with red spies, most of these soldiers were in it for money mixed with inevitable ego nursing its resentments.Herrington does tell the story mostly from the first person, which some will enjoy since it reads more like a story. Others who are used to more of a third person historian will find many of the details perhaps tedious, while desiring of the basic premises backed by examples for which historians are usually adept. I like both styles, and the author does one thing quite well that historians do not often do well: he does not confuse the reader by bouncing dates around. Herrington does an excellent job of keeping the reader abreast of dates, although invariably the author must refer to past or future dates. There was one instance where I detected an error. On page 115 the timeframe is 1986 (114) and President Bush is mentioned. He would have been Vice President at that time.Although in a first person account one would expect an author's personal opinion to be inserted, Herrington mostly presented the facts; he did not rely on a persuasive argument. Nor did he need to since he chronicled the betrayal of war plans of the west and the other crimes of Conrad, Hall, and others.Along the way the author throws in some tidbits that may interest and help the reader to see more of the big picture of espionage. President Reagan's allowing the death penalty for espionage that betrays war plans (196) will come into play for certain defendants. The stubbornness of the Dept of Justice to take on a case despite a plethora of evidence is balanced against the case of Craig Smith, which they lost. As most always, the author did a good job telling both sides reasons for their actions.  He also chronicles quite clearly some of the difficulty in prosecuting subjects since often different countries have different laws for extradition and prosecution.For those interested in the method and technology used in spying, there are the dead drops, calculators with encrypted capabilities, hollowed-out tools for secret storage, water soluble paper, and a great deal of information on surveillance upon subjects.I thoroughly enjoyed the book and commend the author for his service to our country. As a former serviceman stationed in Germany during the 1970s, I am appalled at the betrayal of our country by these sellouts. The terrible horror that communism unleashed upon the world and we in the West fought so valiantly against cannot be dismissed by some Marxist professor or journalist saying "McCarthyism." An estimated 100 million people were killed because of Communism (Black Book of Communism), and many of them were Americans. Unfortunately, some Americans like Ames, Hansen, and the ones mentioned in this book either caused the deaths of others or certainly would have, if war had begun.
