I am a fan of the game, and have been so for the majority of my life. I am quite knowledgable when it comes to the application of statistics in player evaluation. I also understand how numbers should be adjusted to the context of era. Quite simply, the author of this book screwed up royally.You want an example of how he screwed up? One need look no further than the numerous instances where players totalled more than 100 doubles in a single season. There is no form of statistical adjustment where this is at all logical, especially when you consider that the all-time record is 67. Another thing is the fact that the author does not allow for a player who hit no homers in actuality to hit any in the adjusted season. What you are left with is the unrealistic situation of players who hit 35 homers one year, followed by zero homers, followed by forty. It is  worthless.Pitching-wise, the author screws up even more. When someone like Old Hoss Radbourn throws over 600 innings in 1884, to put him into 2000 context, you must take his innings pitched total down to somewhere in the 300 innings pitched region, however instead the author simply pro-rates out the 600 (which was accomplished in a shorter season) into an 1,100 inning performance, which is simply impossible, and most definately not statistically sound. He also does not adjust win loss records based on adjusted runs scored and runs allowed, but rather he simply leaves them the way they are in almost every case (he does pro-rate them to 162 games in any season where fewer than 162 games were played.) He does not take walks or on-base percentage into account, which most obviously creates a false context.I could go on and on listing the shortcomings of this book, which goes to show just how flawed it is. The fact of the matter is you should avoid this book at all costs, as it does not even have decent writing to balance out the poor statistical findings (The writing is infantile at best.)
