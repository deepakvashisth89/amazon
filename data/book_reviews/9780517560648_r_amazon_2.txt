Susan breaks Elizabeth's sixty-five years down into five parts: the Girl, the Woman, the Queen, the Goddess, and the Effigy. Throughout each of these periods, she picks the threads that stand out as the most interesting, both in developing her many characters and in giving a sweeping picture of history. Her genius is in the details: she doesn't get bogged down in them, but neither does she distort history to enhance her plot. Instead, she takes the raw materials of reality and selects the themes that will best draw the reader deeper into an understanding of Elizabeth's world and politics, as well as her intensely felt emotional life.Susan Kay does add one fantastical element to spice up the events as they happened: she has the unquiet ghost of Anne Boleyn hiding behind her daughter's psyche, urging her to get even with the world of men. Elizabeth is the more endearing in that she attempts to fight the urge, only giving in at times.I must say, this is one of the few novels that (almost) takes Elizabeth at her professed word on being a virgin. After reams of steamy speculation from romance novels by other authors wherein she has all kinds of secret progeny, Kay gives a very realistic picture of how completely, overwhelmingly public the sovereign's life is, from dawn to dusk.She also does a very good job with the supporting cast. Robert Dudley, William Cecil and his son Robert, the Earl of Essex, Mary Stuart, Philip II of Spain, her sister Mary I (Tudor), Thomas Seymor, both Spanish ambassadors, and the bloodless Walsingham are all drawn in ways that make their roles and motivations very clear. You come to understand what each of these men meant to the Queen, and why they hated each other.The religious ferment of the time and its political implications were beautifully covered; never too heavy, and always centered squarely on what each move meant to the survival of Elizabeth's rule or the welfare of the people.Elizabeth is shown brilliantly in the context of her times: the fragile claim she had on the throne and the comparative weakness of England, surrounded by powerful countries vying to devour her. How Elizabeth's wily, none-to-moral tricks kept all of them waiting to see who would marry her, or if she would die first and spare them the trouble, becomes very alive and engrossing.I highly recommend this book.
