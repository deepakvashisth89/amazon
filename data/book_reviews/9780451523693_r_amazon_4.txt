This is the fourth copy of Dana's book I have owned, and my sixth time reading it over the past 55 years. Having started floating on the ocean in boats at the age of five, when I read Two Years Before the Mast at 15, I was completely drawn into it. Unlike some writers, who would give a careful explanation of how sailships worked, and include a glossary of terms to interrupt the story every few pages, Dana has you learn as he did. If the Mate shouted to set the top royals, Dana would watch what the other seamen did, and follow. It didn't take him long to learn, and you will too.This is not just book, but a true story of a landlubber who signed onto a sail ship to go around Cape Horn - possibly the most miserable and dangerous section of ocean to sail - so they could sail up the West coast, before there was a California, bringing goods and collecting a cargo for the return trip. There would be no return trip until the hold was full.Dana had a gift for capturing the sights, the Mexicans who lived there and their unique, original culture, the animals, and the feeling of being there as cowhides were flung down cliffs to the beach, where sailors would load them onto boats and row them to be loaded on the anchored ship. Descriptions of how the people lived, how the sailors lived, and how the ocean could be friend or foe, made me visualize the scenes as if I was with him. There were no comforts, no excuses for being sick. There were no piers or conveniences in what is now San Francisco bay - one waded to shore shoeless in frigid water, carrying heavy loads for hours, and there were no dryers on the ship to dry your clothes when they were wet.When I look at the conveniences we have today, and I hear the snivelling of men asked to do things that are the slightest bit uncomfortable,  I think, How would these guys have lasted a single day going around the Horn? When you read of sailing around the Horn, with alternating rain, sleet, snow and hail, pounding at you 50 feet up the mast, barefoot, your only safety a rope below you that your frozen toes cling to, while you lean over a spar with both hands hauling at a sail in gale-force winds, you understand what men are made of.
