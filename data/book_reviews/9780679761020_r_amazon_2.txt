"I think," wrote William Maxwell, "it is generally agreed that stories read better one at a time.  They need air around them.  And they need thinking about, since they tend to have both an explicit and an un-spelled-out meaning." (p. x).  He gave this sound advice as a old man, and it is exactly the thing for approaching this collection.  However, readers may need an iron will to follow it rather than gulping down these 415 marvelous pages as close to non-stop as they can manage.  He is that skilled a writer and the stories in "All Our Days and Nights" are that good.Maxwell offers 23 stories, some as short as six pages ("Haller's Second Home") and some as long as 39 pages, almost a novella ("Over by the River").  These are followed by 21 short stories he calls "Improvisations" and we might call magical realism.  Many were bedtime stories told to his wife of 55 years, such as the title story, "All the Days and Nights" about a man who wonders "Where have all the days and nights of my life gone" and he sets off to find them.The stories come from Maxwell's middle (1954) and older years (1992);  his first novel, "Bright Center of Heaven," was published in 1934, when he was 26. The stories are loosely arranged around the two poles of Maxwell's long life: his family and young manhood in a small, turn-of-the-century Illinois town and his life in New York City.  There he served for forty years as Fiction Editor for The New Yorker, mentoring by day and writing by night.  His mentoring, his writing, his presence as a quiet but influential leader of America's literary life earned him many awards, respected eminence, and a devoted readership.This is background to why "All the Days and Nights: The Collected Stories" has such claim on our attention. These stories are readable and they are memorable, for their "explicit and their un-spelled out meaning."  Readers may be reminded of Stegner, of Faulkner, Welty, Oates, and Walker although Maxwell has his own voice.Among the qualities of this collection, as in much of his other work, including the six novels, are:--Characterization:  Maxwell, a reader may feel, cared deeply about people and observed them keenly.  The scores of people we meet in thse pages have lives of their own before and after this book is closed.  They seem so real it is as if Maxwell shared a telling moment, then they and he will go on.--The Events:  The telling moments in a first-rate short story can be revealing as a novel.  Many of these are.  For example, the writer is surprised, when visiting an elderly relative after many years, to see the hired woman who cared for him and his brother after their mother died suddenly.  He goes into the kitchen to embrace her and he is totally rebuffed by a cold, silent rigidity.  He retreats, and for weeks, tries to understand why she turned her back to him.  The answer comes to him with a finality that shakes his heart with shame and sorrow, as it may the reader's.--The Humanity and Wisdom.  The spirit of this man, expressed in these stories, is of compassion and a gentleness.  He does not forget, but he seems to forgive if not forget trespasses, and vice versa.  The events may be as taut as the loss of his brother's leg, the relationships muddled, yet the voice of this writer is something that I might hope to hear from the Recording Angel, of understanding.As an example, this is the kast paragraph of "The Lily-White Boys,""They have each other, a small bottle of Elizabeth Arden perfume spray said.  They will forget about what happened this evening.  Or, if they remember, it will be something they have ceased to have much feeling about, a story they tell sometimes at dinner parties, when the subject of robberies comes up.  He will tell how they walked home from the Follansbees' on Christmas night and found the front door ajar, and she will tell about the spoon and the silver tray the thieves didn't take, and he will tell how he stood on the stairs watching while she tried on all her favoroite evening dresses."  p. 219Any negatives?  Maxwell writes from his experience, that of a well-educated writer who has known the need to be thrifty, known sorrows and disappointments, but generally has lived comfortably, with the wife he dearly loved.  This and his Illinois world are part of the United States in the 20th century.  Clearly, only a part.  Readers who want an understanding of the World Wars I & II, the Civil Rights movement, the Vietnam War, and the economic changes of the last century may find this in other books.Highly recommended for what it is.
