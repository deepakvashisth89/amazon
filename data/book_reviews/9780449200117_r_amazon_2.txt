I first read this book back in the 1980s, when I was a preteen, not too long after it was first written. It made a strong impression on me, and I've reread it now and then over the years.From the perspective of 2013, the book is extremely dated. The world its characters inhabit no longer really exists. Maggie and Caroline, the protagonists, are high school seniors leading average middle-class lives in downtown Manhattan. (Nowadays, their families would probably have to be quite wealthy to afford decent apartments there.) Neither is much worried about college admissions or, seemingly, spends much time on schoolwork. (Now, they'd be involved in every activity/advanced class.) Other characters take part in 70s-era trends such as open marriage and home childbirth; two characters who have a child with severe learning disabilities place her in a long-term institution, whereas these days, she'd possibly be raised at home. Also, the book's casual approach to the subject of abortion is quite unlike the serious and conflicted discussion of it today.The biggest difference, though, is the book exists in a time when an affair between a high school teacher and high school student was still somewhat socially acceptable. In the book, Caroline starts babysitting for her high school teacher's kid, then falls into an affair with the teacher. This relationship, which leads to an eventual quickie marriage after Caroline graduates, would now be considered statutory rape and grounds for firing the teacher. Not to mention just completely socially unacceptable. Almost any parent today whose daughter fell into this situation would be shocked, disgusted with the teacher, and perhaps likely to take legal action. It is also virtually unthinkable these days that an educated, talented 18-year-old student in the Northeast would marry right out of high school and skip college OR that an educated professional in his late 20s/early 30s would even remotely consider marrying such a young girl. (Sleeping with her, possibly yes, marrying her, no.) So this plot is now quite unrealistic.Despite these problems, though, there is still something true and valuable in this book. What Klein does very well here is capture the deep ambivalence teenage girls feel as they approach maturity. In many ways, they rush to embrace maturity, especially within relationships, because it feels good to fall in love and be loved in return and explore the pleasures of sex. Yet in other ways, they are apprehensive and scared of the responsibility of maturity. There's a very telling and interesting scene in which Caroline and Justin, the teacher, have a romantic evening at his apartment. Later on that night, Caroline is overwhelmed by an urge to return home and get away from the intensity of their relationship. So she sneaks out and goes home--returning to childhood, metaphorically. Justin later shows up. He insists on joining her in her childhood bed, which makes Caroline deeply uncomfortable. This scene captures the conflicting emotions of a teenager in a very truthful way.I think this book is still worth reading for teenagers, but they'd probably benefit from discussing with their parents some of the themes and issues involved.Read moreRead less
