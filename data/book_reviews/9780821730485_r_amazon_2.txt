I'm a true admirer of Charlotte Armstrong's work at its best ("The Chocolate Cobweb" comes to mind), and while the style and social contexts may be conspicuously dated to a contemporary reader, the ideas, the plotting and the creative impulse behind the situations remain interesting and worth a read.This book seems an oddity in her output, having both a very compelling premise (with an absorbing setup in its first two "scenes"), but a writing style that sometimes works against the momentum and overall effect of the storytelling.The biggest "problem" I find with the book is that its main character, Sam Lynch, allegedly a pretty bright guy, seems incapable of putting coherent sentences or thoughts together.  My most generous spin on this would be: it may have been Ms. Armstrong's idea to make him seem so ill-at-ease with certain situations or socially clumsy that his ability to be coherent was challenged or that he is tongue-tied.  Or maybe it's just proximity to a pretty girl that does it.  But whatever the author's  intention, the result is to make the guy seem half-formed and unrealistic to readers.  At too  many key moments in the tale, further plot complications ensue primarily because he makes odd choices and communicates very badly -- and because he seems so unbelievable in this regard, he repeatedly takes us out of a story that otherwise is not without its fascinating moments.Ultimately Sam's character doesn't ring true at all -- he seems artificial and contrived to some extent because of the author's insistence on making him incoherent and indecisive.  This, to me at least, was a major liability of the novel, and one that was never quite overcome.  The writing in general seems adequate but not up to the livelier standard of much of Ms. Armstrong's other work.But the book also has several assets, most of which I don't want to detail here so as not to create spoilers.  The plot is unusual, and creative.  After the first few expository scenes, it moves along well.  There is a family and a fiance at the heart of the story, and they are painted vividly enough to hold our interest and entertain.  There are a pair of unusual villains whom, while slightly falling to stereotype, wind up generating a palpably sinister sense of risk and danger to more than one of our protagonists.  There is a sense of "a love story" here for the youngish girl at the center of the plot -- and there is a fairly satisfying arc of her growing through her unusual experience.Overall, I really enjoyed the premise of the story and many of the narrative details.  But I felt strangely let down by the sheer clumsiness of much of the dialogue, and the unusual (for Charlotte Armstrong) seemingly deliberate awkwardness of her writing style for this novel.It's worth a read, and certain ideas and images are quite memorable. It almost reads as a fable -- it certainly doesn't often feel very connected to any flesh-and-blood reality I'm familiar with.
