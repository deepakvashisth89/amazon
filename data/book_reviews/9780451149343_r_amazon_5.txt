An avid reader of contemporary steampunk, I came to this “classic” example of the genre with a given set of preconceptions already in mind. Knowing the kitschy tropes I might encounter—the goggles, the gears and the pseudo-Victorian trappings and language—this story was more than I expected, and challenged my very definition of the literary style that Jeter himself named, even if he didn’t invent it.First of all, Dower is not the stock protagonist you would expect. Jeter’s main character pushes the definition of “reluctant hero” far past the hesitant Bilbos of the literary world. Like Bingley to Austin’s Darcy, Dower is a beta-type man through and through. But, unlike Bingley, he has no Alpha to guide him (other than the occasional prompting of the mentally-deranged Scape), so his hesitant and frequently awful choices cause nail-biting angst for the reader. Of course, Jeter uses this weakness in his protagonist to great effect; and I suspect, as a critique of Victorian-era repression of human nature in general, seen through the lens of 80’s era excess in which Jeter penned this tale.This undertone of satire is further underscored by the climax at the end. Without spoilers, I will say that the dark humor built into the plot’s conclusion, along with the apparent fun the author has at the expense of Dower and his prudishness, is hilarious and very well done.  But Dower himself is satisfyingly dimensional and shows character growth the reader expects.Yes, there are some annoying typos in this edition.
