The crew of the Enterprise is exploring the core of the galaxy when it encounters a shuttle with a single occupant, Thomas Clayton, who once was in Starfleet Academy with Kirk. After being taken aboard, Clayton appears to be quite insane, warning the crew about the upcoming wrath of his god, Ay-Nab. Soon after, the Enterprise finds itself pulled into a Dyson Sphere, apparently built eons ago by an advanced race. Now though, the only intelligent life in the sphere is a race of ape-like creatures living an agrarian existence. Kirk and many of the other Enterprise officers must explore the sphere, find out what Ay-Nab really is and confront a group of captured Klingons and the mysterious "Strangers" before the Dyson Sphere encounters a black hole and is destroyed.For what would be a novella by today's standards (152 pages in paperback) Gordon Eklund simply tries to pack too much into this story. While it does have a very "episodic" feel, harkening back to the original TV show, so many characters and plot items are introduce that not all of them can be tied up by the end of the story, and they often end up being ignored after being introduced. The best example may be Thomas Clayton who, after being the focus for the first quarter of the book, is then mostly forgotten except for a brief appearance later (after which he vanishes again). Ultimately we are given brief looks at the world inside the Dyson Sphere, the natives who inhabit it, a group of Klingons on the run from their own government, the mysterious semi-undead "Strangers," Lt. Uhura's father (who's background probably doesn't fit Star Trek canon), and the god of the sphere, Ay-Nab and while many of these topics are interesting ones, none of them are every really developed and many are not dealt with by the conclusion of the novel.While this book was very early in Star Trek original fiction, the core idea of dealing with an "all-powerful" entity has been badly over-done in the Star Trek universe. Principal Enterprise characters were fairly well done, feeling mostly on-point with a few out-of-character moments, especially with Spock, who felt too human through out book.An interesting idea for a Star Trek novel that tried to do too much and failed because of it.Read moreRead less
