This book was published in China in English in 1991 by the Foreign Languages Press, a government-related publisher that seeks to bring Chinese fiction to a larger audience abroad. It contained 11 works by as many writers. There were 9 short stories and 2 excerpts from novels.The oldest writers were Lu Wenfu (1928-), Deng Youmei (1931-) and Wang Meng (1934-). The youngest were Zhang Kangkang (1950-), Jia Pingwa (1952-) and Wang Anyi (1954-). Others included Shen Rong (1935-), Zhang Xianliang (1936-), Zhang Jie (1937-), Feng Jicai (1942-) and Ah Cheng (1949-). These authors were called representative of China's literary flowering between 1976 and 1989. Four of the writers were women.As far as could be determined, the works included here were published roughly between 1980 and 1985. Each author was given a lengthy introduction describing his/her achievements. Wang Meng was said to have pioneered the use of stream-of-consciousness in Chinese writing, though this wasn't evident in his story published here. Zhang Jie was called an early supporter of Deng Xiaopeng's economic reforms and an early writer on professional women living independent lives. Zhang Kangkang was called the first contemporary Chinese writer to address the subject of mental illness.All of the writers were prizewinners in China, and a handful held high-level posts within the Chinese Writers Association or provincial-level organizations. At least four of the older writers had been labeled rightists in the late 1950s. Three of these had worked as manual laborers in Beijing and/or the countryside for most of the next two decades before being rehabilitated. Of the younger writers, at least three had either been sent to the countryside or had their lives otherwise disrupted after 1966, during the Cultural Revolution. One author, Wang Meng, after enduring these hardships until rehabilitation in 1979 and appointment as a minister of culture, lost his post during the government crackdown in 1989.Some of the stories in the collection focused on the suffering of innocent people during the Cultural Revolution (Feng Jicai, Wang Anyi). In one, for example, an intelligent man appeared to have lost his mind after being sent to the countryside where there was little food. In another, an urban couple who kept to themselves attracted suspicion in their apartment block and had to endure vicious denunciation; eventually they lost their apartment and one of them suffered a stroke. The motivations of their accusers were shown to be petty and selfish, not political. "For in that period of lunacy people took leave of their senses and cruelly made up groundless accusations in order to find some Hitler in their midst." A lighter story, by Shen Rong, proposed that 10 years be deducted from everyone's age because of the lost years of the Cultural Revolution. In several of the works, when the fact that this revolution was set in motion and guided by the ruling party was mentioned, it was blamed on the Gang of Four.Other stories depicted skillfully the inefficiency, backbiting and pettiness of committees and officials, or their "follow the herd" mentality (Lu Wenfu, Wang Meng, Zhang Jie, Zhang Xianliang). Many of the other pieces (Ah Cheng, Deng Youmei, Jia Pingwa, Zhang Kangkang), about a visit to a mental hospital, a chess player who was a man of the people, or the struggles of an old couple, were virtually unreadable, feeling overlong, formless and poorly edited.For me, this book, though disappointing, was a useful supplement to the Columbia Anthology of Modern Chinese Literature (1995, 1st ed.), since that anthology included only one of the popular authors from the 1980s who were published here, Wang Meng.
