June 7, 2018Notes on U.S. Grant’s memoirsTucked into a blow by blow account of military operations during the Civil War, prefaced by some notes on Grant’s youth, training at West Point, and service in the Mexican American war, are some piercing insights on the Nation, its future, and the origins and fate of the Civil War.  The endless slog through each military operation is virtually incomprehensible without detailed maps for each scene, so the memoirs quickly become tedious.  The best way to harvest the nuggets from these memoirs is to skim the bulk lightly, looking for sparse anecdotes and reflections, and reaching the Conclusion, which, to me, is the best part.Some of the salient thoughts of Grant and other observations in the memoirs:1) The biggest winners of this war will be the South when they lose it.  They are fighting for the cause of their peculiar institution of slavery, which most civilized people regard as abhorrent, and which devalues work and labor for everyone, retards manufacture of higher products, and causes a moral decay.2) The North remained a democracy throughout the War, with elections and a free press.  Some of the opinions printed in the free northern press bordered on treason.  There were lively and antagonistic tirades for and against the war in the North throughout its duration.  The North continued its inventions and industry, its pursuit of knowledge, and nurturing of arts and commerce.  In the North women and children were not forced to work in the fields and factories, and children were sent to school.3) In contrast, the South was one huge military camp ruled by martial law.  Almost every white male from age 14 to 60 was involved in the rebel army.  The press was controlled by the rebel government and fully on the side of the South.4)  Whereas there was a conscription of young men in the north it was not universal and limited to a narrow age range.  There were 18 million northerners and 12 million southerners, of which 4 million of the latter were colored, and non-combatants for the South (some did flee to the North and joined Colored brigades).  All the Colored people and woman and children were obligated to work in the fields and factories.  Education was neglected for the children so that they could support the war effort with food, forage, clothing, railroad, vehicles, boats, and military equipment.  A good number of southerners fled to the north and joined the Union cause.5)  The people of the North had no particular quarrel with slavery in the South, as long as it stayed there, but they were dramatically against being required to police it:  The Fugitive Slave Act and many other laws required northerners to turn over escaped slaves to their southern owners, to form posses to search for escaped slaves, and to otherwise abet the southern institution, to the great indignation of Northerners.6)  Lincoln told the South that if they agreed to just two terms – rejoining the Union and abolishing slavery- they could virtually write their terms of surrender on a blank sheet of paper he would give them.7)  Lincoln is a man of tremendous integrity, honesty, and genuine concern for the Nation and for his fellow man, in general.  Andrew Johnson, the vice president who acceded to the presidency upon Lincoln’s assassination was entirely the opposite.  The assassination of Lincoln was one of the most tragic events of the period (Grant and his wife had been invited by Lincoln to the theater the night of the assassination, but Grant was traveling, so had to decline the invitation).  For, had Lincoln survived, he would have seen to the most rapid reconstruction of the South and reconciliation with the Union, healing the grave wounds as quickly as possible, and avoiding any humiliation of the defeated South.  In contrast, Johnson was a spiteful man bent on humiliating the defeated South, whence he came, and as a Democrat.  The Republican controlled Congress passed a plethora of laws to limit his power as President and he was finally impeached, but the action failed by one vote and he was not removed.  His continued presidency inflamed the reconstruction process.8)  When Lee surrendered to Grant at Appomattox the Union soldiers began a great celebration, firing off cannon and rifles.  Grant quickly stopped them, for he didn’t want the soldiers of the fallen South to be humiliated, on top of their great loss, and their famished, exhausted, and miserable condition.9)  Grant recounts how he went to meet Lee at Appomattox not quite dressed for the occasion;  wearing the britches of a private, disheveled, and just with the shoulder straps with his Lieutenant General insignia to display his rank.  Lee was dressed in stunning and impeccable fashion, with a large, valuable, ceremonial sword at his waist, which, despite popular legend, he did not give to Grant upon surrender.  Grant reflected later on the contrast,  the surrendering General Lee cutting an extraordinary figure and towering over Grant in stature, who stood disheveled and unceremonious in dress.  Grant recounts how, upon meeting, they immediately recalled each other from the Mexican War, although Lee was 16 years his senior, and he didn’t think Lee would remember the junior officer Grant.  They then had a pleasant conversation for half an hour before Lee drew Grant’s attention to the reason for the meeting.  Grant had proposed honorable terms of surrender, whereby all southern arms would be yielded, but southern soldiers could return to their homes unmolested by Northern troops, and Southern officers could keep their horses and side arms.  Lee accepted the terms, and they returned to their pleasant banter for some time.10) During Grant’s tenure as President of the U.S. he pushed for making Santo Domingo a state, but failed.  It would have been free and its fertile land could support 15 million people.  He envisioned that many of the 4 million Coloreds would want to leave the U.S. to go there to become part of a government formed of people of their race.  It was clear that while Grant was for suffrage and rights for the freed slaves, he, like Lincoln, never imagined they would become assimilated into white society.11) Role of the Europeans:  Grant states France was always the U.S.’s most constant ally.  Grant detested Napoleon’s character but recognized his military genius and some of the civil institutions he founded.  He fully despised Napoleon III, his failures of conquest in Europe and his attempt at installing a European Monarchy in Mexico with an Austrian prince.  He considered Napoleon III to be a vainglorious and mediocre usurper of power in France, which led to France abandoning its traditional role of US ally during the Civil War.  He has few kind word for England, the mother country, as he says, which should be a natural US ally due to the common language, and ties of blood and customs.  He was exasperated that England did not support the Union, quite the opposite, but he believed there were popular rallies in England for the Union and none for the rebels.  He felt that England and the U.S. knew better than any other European nation how to build colonies and, while rigidly controlling populaces, yet allowed them many freedoms.12)  Coming out of the Civil War, having been the prevailing military leader and witness to the unspeakable carnage and waste, Grant claims that the U.S. has the most battle hardened and competent soldiery on the face of the earth.  He says that most soldiers, both Northern and Southern, were invested in their causes during the War, even though the Southern cause was a mistaken one.  He contrasts this to European armies, which he terms ‘machines’, the soldiery being drawn from a populace that is largely ignorant, and has no sense of the causes for which they fight.  Hence, while the European troops fight gallantly they are not invested in the wars.13) Grant admits that not all actions of the North were constitutional during the war, and in fact there is no provision in the Constitution as to how to handle armed resurrection and secession.  But he pleads, somewhat paradoxically, that, while the South had entirely divorced itself from the Constitution in its behavior, it was not feasible for the North to adhere strictly to it in order to preserve it.14)  Writing his memoirs twenty years after the War, and while suffering in the terminal throes of throat cancer, Grant laments the state of military preparedness of the U.S.  He advocates a strong navy and coastal defenses and worries that any fourth rate European power could best the U.S. if there were a naval invasion, considering the state of the U.S. military at that time.15)  The long military slog throughout the four years reveals the essence of the events.  Tens of thousands of men trekking hundreds of miles back and forth across the vast South, the armies fearing bloody encounters with each other, and trying to avoid them, while building overwhelming strategic and tactical advantages over each other, so as to make decisive wins once the inevitable confrontations occurred.  The armies were used more for manual labor than for fighting.  The entire existence of the army was predicated on continuous supplies of food, forage, arms, and munitions.  A principal task of the commanding officers was to ensure supply lines for the troops while trying to destroy supplies of the enemy.  Tearing up and fixing railroad tracks, building and burning bridges, obstructing and clearing roads, burning down towns and supply depots of the enemy, diverting the courses of entire rivers to create new navigable waterways, felling forests, foraging the land for supplies, and endless marching, these were the daily work of the troops, punctuated by intermittent, pitched and bloody battles.Grant tells how each side became experts in quickly repairing railroad tracks, even changing the gauge when needed.  He recounts how Sherman on his march from Atlanta to the sea, not only tore up the railroad tracks, but had the troops heat them and twist them like bow ties around trees, leading to a stunning forestscape.Throughout Grant recounts valiant, brilliant, and foolish acts by commanders, continuously assesses them as to their character, competencies, and valor.  Continuous communication with Washington and recommendations for and against officers’ promotions populate the narrative.  The general whom Grant most respected was Sherman, probably followed by Meade and Sedgwick.  Sheridan gets mixed reviews, some great, others awful, while Halleck is usually badmouthed.  Grant claims that while supplies, especially food and forage, were taken from southern homes, he attempted to keep an inventory of what was taken, so as to make restitution at war’s end.  It is not clear if this was ever followed through on.The narrative paints Grant as a true badass.  While shunning needless ‘effusions of blood’, once engaged with his army he could be merciless until the enemy surrendered.  When communicating with rebel generals on terms for their surrender, Grant typically replied that surrender would be unconditional.  While respecting the valor and intelligence of the enemy officers, most of whom he knew either from West Point or previous military service, he always considered the rebellion to be a traitorous cause that had to be suppressed, and he never recognized the Confederate Government as a legitimate authority.  In contrast, he considered the Mexican-American War to be shameful, and an arrogant and high handed usurpation of neighboring lands with no real justification. Yet, as an obedient young soldier at that time, he served as best he could and became known for several remarkable exploits; e.g. riding through crossfire in a Mexican village while hanging sideways off the flank of his horse.The memoirs do not include any details of his failed efforts at farming and other pursuits between his retirement from the service between 1854-1861, his presidency, nor of reconstruction, nor of how Grant took to writing these memoirs to raise cash, in light of his failed investments and deteriorating health and finances.These are the memoirs of an absolutely extraordinary man, competent and tenacious, hard-headed, pragmatic, and deeply violent in acts of war, yet with a good heart and expansive spirit, who sought justice (he founded the U.S. Dept of Justice), and never sought to humiliate or domineer over others.
