I found the first half horribly slow—endless, pointless description, it seemed to me; some valid observations about the human condition, but nothing most of us haven’t already taken for granted. Then, just at the midway mark, we had some action and that action made for fast reading probably to about the 85 percent mark; then it descended into more of what I described in the early going.Interesting (to me) that I come away from the book not knowing Eliot’s position on religion. More interesting because one of the central characters, Dinah Morris, is a Methodist preacher and very much a benevolent character. And yet, I don’t know that Eliot commits herself in this book to a position on religion.More of interest: The title character is a carpenter (surely a symbolically charged vocation)—hardworking, smart. His first love is a perfectly empty-headed lass, Hetty Sorrel. Eliot admits to the reader—I like the way she addresses the reader in second person—that Adam and Hetty seem mismatched. Eliot then tries to explain why these things happen and why it’s not bad, although she didn’t convince me. What a miserable life Adam would have had with Hetty.This was a long novel. I’d read Middlemarch, The Mill on the Floss, and Daniel Deronda. I recall getting through them without much trouble, but at a distance in time, I recall very few of the details.
