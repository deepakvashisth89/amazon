Sadly, this book is a "colossal Canadian failure".It had the potential of a great idea, had it been an analysis of why great (or goofy) ideas fail.  Everyone writes about success, no one analyzes why projects which seem like a good idea at one time turn into failure.  Instead, this book turned into little more than a rewrite of old history books and newspaper clips without ever asking, "Why?"It's disappointing because Richmond is a first rate writer.  His first book, 'The Orillia Spirit', is a wonderful example of how every small town history should be written.  Sadly, he followed that exemplary effort with a book that is a wonderful example of how a book should not be written.His account of the Avro Arrow is an example;  this first-rate Canadian aircraft included a number of technologies far ahead of their need.  The cost was stupendous, which meant the Liberal government planned to cancel it AFTER the 1956 election.  The Liberals knew if they were doomed if they cancelled it before the election;  and, if the Conservatives won, they'd have to pay a heavy political price of either cancellation or facing years of criticism for huge cost overruns.  The Conservatives won, it was cancelled, and Liberals successfully targeted every vulnerable Conservative in the next election.How do I know?  I've read C. D.Howe's biography, and I talked to Liberal strategists who used the Arrow failure to regain power after the defeats of the 1950s.  Richmond offers no such insights.There's a lot more to failure than "I came, I conned, I crashed".  Richmond describes several early Canadian automobile ventures, but completely overlooks the Tudhope autos built in Orillia -- the subject of his earlier book.  He ignored DeHavilland Canada and the Buffalo;  DHC developed one of the world's most popular commuter turboprops, yet DHC collapsed.His account of Canada's Ross rifle is shallow, cursory and superficial;  anyone who's heard of the Schmeisser, or the Kalishnikov, knows the perils of weapon design.  The Ross is an example of the Avro Arrow mentality which often infects Canadian projects by insisting on perfection instead of practicality.  Had Richmond looked into this Canadian passion for innovative excellence instead of rough-and-ready utility, the book might have been worthwhile.In other words, it's little more a very well rewritten collection of cut-and-paste snippets compiled without sense, meaning, purpose or thought.  It's the Ronald Reagan style of negativism;  always ready to criticize anyone who has the courage to innovate, always ready to cite government waste, but never able to offer constructive analysis or any correction.Don't get me wrong;  it is a well-written book.  His stories of the London Free Press follies, where he now works, are hilarious and contain the gems of a newspaper classic in the best tradition of Ben Hecht or Evelyn Waugh.
