Matthew Parris has written a very funny if somewhat self-serving volume outlining scandal in various branches of what purports to be the Christian Churches. Funny in the sense that the great and the good of the Establishment, of which the Church of England is a part, has rarely practised what it has preached. Self serving inasmuch as Parris, a non believer who carefully refrains from passing judgement on those who fall from grace, promotes homosexuality as a lifestyle, particularly in respect of the Church of England where the primary qualification for membership appears to be, with rare exceptions, disbelief in God. It could be argued that the Established Church is perfectly described in the Book of Revelation as being neither cold nor hot but lukewarm. Not surprising when it was established because Henry V111 wanted a divorce and would have had one had not the Holy Roman Emperor Charles V not been Catherine of Aragon’s nephew.There is too much emphasis on homosexuality notwithstanding its prevalence in the Church of England and its Roman Catholic counterpart. Some of the material is fascinating. Percy Joycelyn was the Bishop of Clogher not on merit but because of family connections. He was known by one of his contemporaries as the ‘most idle of all Reverend idlers who never wrote a sermon’. After a day in the House of Lords he was discovered in the back room of the White Lion with an effeminate soldier. He fled the country while the radical press used the scandal to attack the political establishment. It took 178 years for the Church of Ireland to finally release the papers. These days people enmeshed in activities they would rather keep under wraps turn to the courts instead. The soldier was flogged mercilessly. Over a century earlier an Irish Protestant bishop had been  hanged for homosexual practices.Parris argues that the Church suffers from moral confusion on the issue which is ‘characterised by intolerance, low censoriousness, circumspection, self-pity, sniping, whimpering and special pleading - and, always and forever, the grinding of axes’. He fails to draw the clear conclusion that all the characteristics referred to do not represent Jesus’s teaching exemplified in the story of the woman who was brought to him by the Pharisees having been caught in the very act of adultery (the contemporary equivalent of Channels Four and Five), ‘Let him that without sin amongst you cast the first stone’. A twenty-first century equivalent would have been followed by a barrage of self righteous politically correct verbiage. When the Pharisees had departed he asked the woman ‘Where are thine accusers?’ to which she replied there were none. Hence ‘Go and sin no more’. Not a question of Parris’s  moral relativism but a recognition that all have sinned and come short of the glory of God.Runaway Romeos and Feet of Clay are accurate descriptions in some cases, although the attempt to defend Judas Iscariot by some modernists anxious to find the origins of anti-Semitism simply ignores the  anti-Christian blood libel spread by Roman Jews in the eternal city. Parris does not try to distinguish between myth and reality which is sensible. The unlamented News of the World used the slogan ‘All human life is there’ and so it was, is and  likely to be in the future. Young women will remain susceptible to rogues and false Messiahs will continue to rise and end, as in 1848, with great disappointment although soothing words will have prepared them to be disappointed. The Agapemonites are an example of the susceptibility of the abuse of power in which a bogus priest claimed the right to deflower a virgin but denied any responsibility for the pregnancy which followed.American Television evangelists are prime examples of latter day Pharisees, donating their shekels, keeping their wealth while the widow gave the only two mites she had. Falwell, Roberts and Bakker betrayed the Christian faith and were parodied by Bobby Bare in his,  ‘Praise the Lord and send me the money’. The love of money being the root of evil appears to have been forgotten by the evangelistic trio. Of course their opponents were no differentVictoria Woodhull, as irrational a human as ever existed, resulted in the embarrassment of preacher Henry Ward Beecher. Like so many young ‘radicals’ Woodhull ended life as a rich person having married a millionaire banker and disowned her past.Which brings this reviewer to a fundamental point which is society’s inability to understand human nature. Those who advocated the overthrow of the Medieval order and its replacement by the ‘Enlightenment’ in which rational man, rather than any spiritual values, would be the engine of the future,  ignored human nature itself. Yet, as Carl Becker brilliantly pointed out in his ‘The Heavenly City of the Eighteenth Century Philosophers’, the names changed but the underlying assumptions remained the same. Enlightened humans have made the world more, rather than less, irrational. The underlying reason for this is the failure to admit that human nature is an integral part of existence. Hence the critique of the unfrocked is a critique of humans in general.As a Christian brought up in a group which did not recognise the artificial titles of Reverend or the frocks that went with them, this reviewer is not shocked by the contents of the book. Being cynical about those who set themselves up to be better than everyone else I adhere to the philosophy that the professions (clergy, lawyers, politicians, doctors, writers, companions of very little honour) are conspiracies against the public where the application of Christian principles in a world of moral relativism is long overdue. In laughing at the misfortunes of others we should be laughing at ourselves and judging ourselves rather than others. Fun to read four stars.Read moreRead less
