" 'Meat can't dance, that's what they say,' said a voice.  Behind it, a face: young, male, dreadlocked. Living.  'Too bound up by the inhibitions of living to be able to truly lose themselves. The dead have no inhibitions, no restraints, no limits, and so they can dance.' "  (From Terminal Cafe)What would happen if, when people died, they didn't really die?  This 1994 book, set in a futuristic LA that is both hilariously campy and spookily prescient in its predictions of where the city is going is about the effects (including mental, emotional and legal) of nanotech processes that resurrect a recently died individual to life in another type of body, very similar to that of biological humans, or "meat" except in the sense that it doesn't have needs for sustenance to meet and very few  things can kill it. Almost everyone is resurrected, but their new lives are so radically different from their biological ones that they quickly lose touch with the concerns and issues of the still-living (even their former human companions become hazy and unreal), and live their own lives, as best they can, under an oppressive legal system which consigns all but the richest of the resurrected dead to many decades of indentured servitude.So what's the plot?  Five meat-people, old friends, all around 26 years old (a strange age, everyone agrees) are planning to have their yearly reunion in a chic watering hole known as the Terminal Cafe, located in the heart of "Necroville," the area of LA (which seems to include most of the city, actually) consigned to the dead who live. All dead  must return to Necroville when the sun goes down...to do whatever it is one does in a body that never sleeps. And guards and gates keep them in. Things do not work out as planned for these five living friends who, for various fascinating reasons, do not make the rendezvous at the appointed time. Things also do not work out as planned for the rest of the world--or, at least, for those who run it--but that's a rather minor subplot in a story that is about five young individual's odysseys. The rest of the book covers the events over a span of about two and a half days at the beginning of a November in some unknown year in the not-too-distant future.The tale is very satisfying. There is as much inner exploration as there are depictions of the bizarre and unexpected outcomes that radical technological advances can have on our unsuspecting species. It's a cautionary tale, as well, about the foibles and weaknesses of humans, both dead and alive. In the end, these five complex paths of discovery resolve wonderfully. That is the best part of the tale. The second best part, for me, were the unique (but also recognizable) characters. My favorite was YoYo, the "everything it its right place" lawyer who cannot read (and does not need to, these days) hounded by a ghostly (and, to YoYo's dismay, very messy and flamboyant) Carmen Miranda drag queen NPC in her legal virtual-world software, who "loves her long time" but also destroys her biggest case.The most difficult part of the book, for me, was something other reviewers have mentioned about this author's books: they have many complex narrative threads and it's hard sometimes to keep track of who's thinking or doing what, especially if  you cannot read the entire book in one sitting. I was constantly having to thumb back to previously-read pages to remind myself of the storyline of a character I hadn't seen in a few days.  There was a lot of pleasure involved in this backtracking, however, because the storylines are incredibly complex and dripping in rich details that one tends to miss during the first reading when you're just trying to figure out what the heck is going on in this strange, strange land of the possible future.In conclusion: absolutely delightful story, if you like "smart" stories. Me Recommend This Long Time!(In case anyone is curious, this book was written at least five years after a certain talented musical group started up, so, no, they didn't get their name from the passage quoted at the front of this review--although it would have been very cool if they had.
