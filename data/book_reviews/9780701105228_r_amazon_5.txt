Jane Graham is a twenty-seven-year-old actress who finds herself ‘in trouble’ – as it was called in the 1950’s/60’s when an unmarried woman found herself pregnant – after fling with a fellow actor, Terry.  The one-night stand was disappointing, so Jane is determined to go it alone.  Her father, a middle manager civil servant and widower, who lives in a leafy house doesn’t take kindly to the news, so Jane moves out into a bedsit in a crumbling Fulham townhouse, which has tenants on every floor.  Next door is a Black guy, John, and on the floor below, a Jewish man who aspires to be a writer, Toby.  There is an instant spark between her and Toby, who happens to be similarly penniless.There is no doubt Lynne Reid Banks is a talented skilled writer who knows how to hook in her reader.  It is a page turner to the very end, with lots of fine metaphors, similes and scenes both amusing and heartrending.  She takes her character Jane down to the depths of despair and to the highest of hopes for the future.I know it was the social mores of the times  - the book was published in the early 60’s – but the offensive racism and homophobia the character Jane, her father, her boss, her landlady and the other tenants direct at John  who is described in animalistic terms (for example, as having a huge head and hands, smelling like a polecat, etc) and towards Jews – Jane makes snide remarks about his name and his nose – is simply jarring.  It is apparent that Jane, despite being unemployed and penniless herself and expecting a baby out of wedlock after casual sex, as well as living in a hovel (the L-shaped room) sees herself as superior to everybody else.  There is also a narcissistic claim of being special, with everybody wanting to do things for her and giving her gifts and inheritances, yet her sneering attitude towards others is simply horrid.  Ugh!Nonetheless, it is a good, long, absorbing read, hence four stars.  (Sorry I can’t condone the hate language.)Read moreRead less
