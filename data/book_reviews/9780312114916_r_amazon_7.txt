Published in 1899, the very threshold of 20th century, the prose and narration feels so contemporary, almost journalistic. Before getting this book I had not read any synopsis as they can be a bit misleading. Without giving spoilers nor a synopsis (just the beginning), is basically about a man that travels to fulfill his yearning for the discovery of terras incognita. Sounds like the wonderful 19th century literature of adventures, an age of exciting explorations, populated by heroes conquering with their will the wild nature and exotic lands. This is not that kind of story: instead is muddy, suffocating, feverish, corpses with flies and decaying meat, I feel it closer to Poe's horror in some way. The tangible reality to arrive to a place so different to home that either you adapt or you just get destroyed and crushed.Among the parts I liked were the reactions of the different persons to respond the chaos: the medic that seems to change into a chaman, interested by delusional visions; the functionary that tries his best to create in his clothes and books a sphere of rational civilization; the other functionary that seems to try his best to recreate the madness in his clothes; the parts in which Marlow and Kurtz seem to be the same person but in different times. At first I thought the book was excessively gloomy or somber, till the point to look artificial... but fortunately Conrad balances it, not with positive counterweights or comical reliefs, but with just enough humor to give human depth to an otherwise maddening nature. Of course "maddening" to the point of view of a visitor. A man from that jungle lands would suffer the same if is let to struggle with the moors of England.AmazonClassics editions are, in my opinion, the best editions to read classic works.
