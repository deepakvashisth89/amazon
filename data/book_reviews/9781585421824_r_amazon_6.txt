I am now reading this book for the fourth time and I still find it riveting. I cannot, of course, verify the author's visionary experiences, but I have had enough similar experiences to accept them, knowing that they are part of the landscape of the magical Other-worlds. Those who are disturbed by the idea of visions are not yet ready for the magical path. Magic is a right-brain journey, and Celtic magic seems to be particularly based on excursions into the faerie realms and other dimensions of reality that can only be accessed through visionary experiences. (By visionary, I mean that which is seen, heard, or felt.)I was happy to discover that this "shaman-like" tradition of the British Isles is alive and well in Brittan. There is much more mystery here than I pretend to understand, but I am grateful to the author for providing so many clues for further exploration. One reviewer mentioned the use of drugs to access visions, but drugs are not at all necessary for opening inner vision. In fact, I would strongly warn against them. If you want to perceive the Other-worlds, practice meditation and right-brain activities like music, painting, dancing, and especially communion with nature. In time we begin to remember what we could already see, though most of us have been taught to exclude the non-material from our perception of "reality." It is just a matter of relearning what we knew as children.This book is a journey. You can enter into it and begin to unravel the mysteries for yourself, or you can read it as an observer as you would read a travel magazine. Either way, the myth of Arianrhod is a fascinating clue to who we are and our place in the Universe. Wilde writes well and weaves a web of enchantment that includes the reader.
