The various men in "Dombey and Son" are quite certain that women are the weaker sex. At the center of the novel, Paul Dombey, a wealthy businessman, directs all of his attention towards the nurturing of his fragile young son and heir; his daughter Florence exists only at the edges of his life. When he chooses to remarry, after the death of his children's mother, he essentially purchases a beautiful woman whom he expects to be an ornament to his household and person. Every interaction he has with a woman, whether it is with his infant son's wet nurse,  his meddling sister, or his own daughter, involves condescension. As for his vaunted business affairs, these come to reside chiefly in the hands of his second-in-command, Carker, whose aim is to enhance Dombey's sense of himself as a powerful man, even as Carker, the 19th century equivalent of a rogue trader, places his master's empire at risk.Around this central drama revolve a number of subplots that make sport of the received idea of feminine delicacy. The retired old salt Captain Cuttle (complete with a hook for a hand) lives in fear of the indomitable landlady Mrs. McStinger. The meanest character in the novel is not a man but a woman, the child rearing "expert" Mrs. Pipchin. She meets her match in the well-named Susan Nipper, Florence Dombey's unsentimental nurse and companion. And no man (as more than one finds out in the course of the novel) can match the steel of Edith, Mr. Dombey's second wife.Of course, no one is tougher than Florence herself, whose powerful love for her father will perhaps strike some readers as idealized. In the novel, however,  it is mirrored by the love of the (unsuccessful) shopkeeper Solomon Gills for his nephew Walter, whom he has raised like a---well, like a mother. Solomon goes to the ends of the earth to find his lost nephew just as Florence goes to the limits of psychological endurance to find her father. In "Dombey and Son," money and power not only cannot buy love, they cannot exist without it. The women, who are anything but weak, know this; the men have to learn it. Don't miss this less-read Dickens novel, whose cast of characters is as wonderful as in any novel he wrote.N.B. I read this book in part on a Kindle.
