This book is emotionally devastating and intellectually challenging. Markson is known for writing in a disjointed, spare, postmodern style -- all his books are collections of facts and anecdotes rather than straight narrative. This technique is never more effective than in Wittgenstein's Mistress. This book follows the wandering, lonely mind of Kate, a painter who appears to be the last living being on earth. On a typewriter she discovers in a beach house, Kate recounts her ten-year journey exploring the world, looking for other survivors. But Kate is not a reliable narrator -- her mind wanders, she loses track of time, the facts she relates don't always line up, and some of her thinking seems fanciful, even impossible. And then there's Markson's distinctive style. Much of the book consists of Kate simply writing whatever comes to mind -- including tons of stray anecdotes about philosophers and famous painters. The result is a heart-rending, confusing, powerful book that really captures the frayed, lonesome state of its protagonists' mind. Without social contact, Kate is unraveling -- and so are her memories and her sense of self. This is a captivating and profoundly sad book, and it's one of my favorites of all time.Read moreRead less
