There's no doubt that master mystery writer John Dickson Carr, only 29 years old, lays out a persuasive solution to the murder of Sir Edmund. The solution is not original with him, having begun as a magazine article by J.G. Muddiman. But while Muddiman's piece was only a few pages long, Carr fleshes out his case to more than 350 densely packed pages.Alas, this is too much flesh for such spare bones. A hundred pages would easily suffice to make the full case for the Carr-Muddiman theory while disposing of the alternatives. The rest of the book — i.e, most of it — is an elaborate exposition of court intrigues and party politics in the reign of Charles II.True, Godfrey's death was heavily exploited as political propaganda, but, for purposes of analyzing the crime, we don't need a comprehensive account of the various partisans' moves and countermoves. Carr clearly decided to "publish his research" — to include every snippet he gleaned from his thorough study of the period. The massive overload of detail simply overwhelms the "mystery" plot, just as some of Carr's later historical novels, set in the Regency period, capsize under the weight of too much background detail.A shorter, tighter, more focused version of "The Murder of Sir Edmund Godfrey" would be a masterpiece.
