The cover of my edition of "Flicker" compares it to "The Name of the Rose." If the same ad copy were written today, I'd expect the point of comparison to be "The Da Vinci Code." This novel is another in that line of intellectual puzzlers, and it also involves codes of a kind (visually hidden in movies, in this case), but I can see why it didn't become the mass-market bestseller that those novels did: It lacks the additional "potboiler" elements like murder mysteries, chases, narrow escapes, a developing love interest, etc. In this story, a young film scholar gradually discovers that the works of a nearly forgotten German émigré filmmaker are a window onto an ancient, hidden conspiracy, the ongoing work of "Cathar" religious heretics thought to have been wiped out in the Middle Ages. The problem is that the long series of interviews and fact-finding missions in which all this is revealed -- and which make up most of the plot -- aren't driven along by anything other than the scholar's curiosity. Nothing really seems to be at stake, and his investigations never seem to encounter much resistance. As it turns out, a great deal may be at stake, and the resistance is fierce -- but none of this is known until almost the end, so it doesn't serve to create any suspense along the way. The ending is also very different from those of the more familiar mystery/thrillers, and that's something you should be prepared for before launching in: If your reason for reading a long novel is the thrills, the exciting climax or a satisfying outcome, then "Flicker" might not be one in which you want to invest your time.Read moreRead less
