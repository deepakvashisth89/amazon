schuyler has crafted a perfect satire, lampooning the colored and negro race leaders of his era, the 1930s, most recognizable, marcus garvey, dubois and booker t washington the most recognizable, for anyone remotely familiar with african american history of the 1930s.  the racial designations of the day, colored, the designation on official documents, birth certificates, driver licenses, and death certificates, and negro as the black progressives chose to be called. what no self-respecting person wanted to be called was black, a demeaning term with a sting, carrying with it everything negative. if you wanted to insult someone you called them black. black would become acceptable as a racial descriptive, earning entry into the culture of the united states as acceptable until the hard cultural battle of black pride during the 1960s with the slogan black is beautiful, the concept of black power, and james brown’s anthem, say it loud, i’m black and i’m proud.the title of schuyler’s book alone was provocative merely for the use of the word black.  if colored people would not have to hear the word, they would be happy. black had the status of being the era’s b-word.  whatever black americans were called, the designation, schuyler knew not change how they were perceived, voting rights, housing, racial violence, schools, and entry into eating establishments. racism was so woven within the fabric of the united states that to end racism something drastic was called for.  schuyler’s imaginative solution was not to change the designation or campaign against the ‘b word’ but to solve the problem by getting rid of the blacks altogether through an act of transformation of skin color from black to white by means of a machine by a colored inventor who becomes wealthy as the colored race line up as though in the 21st century deep discount tickets were being sold to beyonce concerts or the super bowl.as schuyler tells his outrageous story in broad strokes as his transformed blacks no
