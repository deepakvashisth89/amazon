McCarthy’s post-modern Western is both sprawling and meticulous. Throughout Blood Meridian, the narrative is, at times, deeply immersive, plunging you into the tactile horrors of a lawless world inhabited by idiosyncratic characters. At other times, the prose is caught in a torrent, a rapid succession of sentences that impatiently hasten the reader as the narrative traverses a vast sweep of events with incredible velocity. As a result, much of the detail can feel inconsequential, but the delivery, lean as it is, is almost akin to Hemingway. It might be easy to mistake these seemingly fleeting moments as banal, unaware of the detailed composition being laid out. When McCarthy decides to pull focus, we are gifted with straightforward and captivating storytelling.McCarthy challenges the traditional understanding of Western storytelling as we follow a band of miscreants, devoid of anything resembling Roy Rogers, and make no mistake: Blood Meridian is brutal and raw. However, McCarthy’s use of violence is not gratuitous; it signifies a separation from benevolence—that we are bearing witness to nature’s most primal indulgences.The introduction of our titular character, The Kid, wastes no time, and much of this urgency carries throughout the book with The Kid serving as our protagonist, if one is to be found. The minimalistic narrative is reflected though sparse dialogue, but when McCarthy does indulge us, he delivers with provocative monologues and sharp wit. With his verbose dialogue, figures like The Judge directly challenge your philosophical assumptions and many of the personalities we encounter, each possess a distinct appeal, existing within that space beyond the reach of morality and virtue.At around 350 pages, Blood Meridian is a moderate read through a violent and savage world, whose vivid scenes will linger long after. It is a modern take on the Western—a focus on brutality and human nature unbound by the restrictions of society, and a profound deconstruction of the tropes commonly found within the genre.
