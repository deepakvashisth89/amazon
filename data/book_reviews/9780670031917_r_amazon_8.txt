I wanted to enjoy this novel, “The 6th Lamentation,” more than I did.  In the end, I think I appreciated it but failed to really embrace it.  The first in what is supposed to be a series of “Father Anselm” stories (mysteries?), William Brodrick’s freshman novel exhibits beautifully crafted prose—the book falls into the category of “literary novel”—but has several aspects that impinge against the reader’s understanding: multiple timelines, indirect exposition, emotionally guarded characters, a tendency towards implying rather than stating, and the delayed revelation of identities.  Indeed, by the end of the novel I was still wondering, who was so-and-so again, and what happened to such-and-such a character?  Much of this is authorial intent, but too much ambiguity risks losing the reader’s interest and sympathy, which is quite damning for a book that has the Holocaust as the backdrop. A more patient reader will have a more generous assessment.
