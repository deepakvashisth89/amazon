As a blind person, I decided to read Blindness by José Saramago because I heard that it was highly regarded and that blind people had protested against the movie based on it. So I was curious to read it for myself.A city is struck by an epidemic of white blindness, and the blind are taken to a former insane asylum. White blindness is one of the weirdest apocalypses I've ever read about. These blind people are almost completely helpless. However, a doctor's wife pretends to be blind so that she can be with her husband, and she helps the other blind people and encourages them to maintain what little dignity they have left. God knows what would happen to them if she, with her eyesight, wasn't there to help them. For example, the doctor needs his wife to shave him and to clean him, and the blind internees get down on all fours to find their food containers.It's really quite strange that there isn't a single cane in this book. And when Saramago does mention canes, he says that they are used as some kind of echolocation. In fact, a cane is used to tell blind people what is in front of them. He also asserts that the blind have no sense of day or night and to the blind, a person is only a voice and names are not important. Later he describes bands of blind people roving the streets scrounging for food--because once the blind leave their houses, they can never find their way back home, so they just wander.While these assertions were not meant to be taken literally, it is clear to me that José Saramago really doesn't know much about blind people, but that never stopped anyone from writing about us. People write anything they want about blind people, without bothering to speak with us or to do any research. And this is especially true of José Saramago.The scenes of blind people defecating in the yard and in the streets were, to me, the most offensive part of the book. Blind people are a minority who have to deal with prejudice every day. It's just wrong to depict us this way, no matter how noble your motives are. People keep reminding us that this book is an allegory, but it is a flawed allegory, based on an uninformed, prejudiced view of blindness.I was not as uncomfortable with the Lord of the Flies stuff, the fighting and later the takeover by the blind hoodlums. The most harrowing scenes in the book were the rapes and the scene with the woman with the cigarette lighter.There are some moments of deep sensitivity. Examples are the scene with the blind lovers, the love scenes before the rapes, and the engagement near the end of the book (though rather unlikely). The protagonists were portrayed with a depth that is rarely seen in characters, blind or sighted. They were far more noble, and a little less helpless, than the other characters in the book. It must also be remembered that Saramago wants his readers to identify with the blinded characters.Certainly, parts of this novel have great power. But this book is very problematic for blind people. I can't tell people what to read and how to feel  about it, but in my opinion, this book should not be given special emphasis or literary cachet, such as being taught in literature classes, lauded by critics, and given literary prizes.
