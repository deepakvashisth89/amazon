There are a few ways to approach this book.  Dr. Heinrich in this narrative covers many aspects of his life with is rescued and adopted owl "Bubo" including some day-to-day life anecdotes, the impact on his marriage and the process of taming and finally releasing Bubo back into the wild.I prefer to look at it as the insights of a naturalist into the development of a great horned owl.  In particular, the author's recounting of Bubo's learning processes--what to eat, how to play and how to explore.  What we learn is that in many respects Bubo arrives hardwired by nature, but there are key aspects that are learned--such as how to fly and how to hunt.The unfortunate part of the story is the author's treatment of the personnel at the raptor rehabilitation center where Bubo was sent to learn the ways of hunting and survival.  They don't come off as particularly sympathetic lot of people and I think that is unfortunate.  They were tasked with undoing many of the mistakes that Dr. Heinrich made during the first summer with Bubo.  Specifically, an owl at the end of the first summer should have been self-sufficient.  Instead, Bubo hung around screaming and begging for food.  Ultimately, the author concedes that at some point he left the realm of voyeur and became a participant.  It isn't clear from the narrative how the second summer advanced Bubo given that he spent the winter in an aviary in the author's backyard.
