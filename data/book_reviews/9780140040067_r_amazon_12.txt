Barry Lyndon was indeed a difficult book to read from the 19th century, but it reaps the rewards. The picaresque novel about an Irish youngster who goes on a journey through Dublin after trying to win his cousin back from an English soldier in a duel and eventually winds up as a wanderer and, subsequently, becoming a soldier in the Seven Years’ War, and then a deserter, gambler, spy, and eventually a full time swindler, breaching the social conventions of aristocracy was much more endearing on the page than I expected.This was something I wanted ticked off the bucket list as a fan of the Kubrick film. So much stayed the same, but by simply changing the first person perspective from the book, and changing some of the character traits of Redmond himself and removing more events in between the story of the novel involving characters Redmond befriends and uses for his personal gain, it transmogrifies the story from one about a monster dolt who was a member of the Irish gentry trying to infiltrate a higher social sphere in the most ruthless, unsparing, and violent way possible into one about a young dolt whose feelings are much more ambivalent due to a lack of control over the narrative so we can’t hear his internal and selfish thoughts, and who may have the same kind of social and regal ambitions, but is marked by more bouts of sentimentality on family matters which the film Barry indulges in compared to book Barry, who fails to understand the need for paternal feelings or empathy.It is definitely worth a read for those interested in literature from the time, but the language is archaic, so be prepared that you might want to use a thesaurus when reading it. I had no clue what the plural word “calumnies” meant, which is a word used frequently by Barry in describing what the higher personages are supposedly spreading in tarnishing his “good name”.  Thackeray made a book that was later immortalised by Stanley Kubrick’s film adaptation of it 131 years later, but that still manages to stands on its own, and is completely relevant in its own right for studying, because of how unfiltered it is.Thackeray defies the usual conventions associated with a titular character by showing how small he is in the world he is trying to control in front of the reader’s eyes. It took a while for me to gravitate towards the book, which is why I am giving it 4 stars instead of 5, but I can’t deny the impact it has when you adjust to it accordingly, and I feel this book will be subject to a reread eventually.The fact that such a pompous and self-serving character can twist the narrative in his favour as he turns it into a story of triumph in the face of class warfare, and makes the latter half of the book where “his luck runs out” seem as though it was all the fault of the system and that he is totally blameless because his own shameless swindling of Lady Lyndon’s properties was justified as her husband, is the power of the first person perspective employed by Thackeray. The heroic autobiography is what Redmond Barry sets out to achieve in his memoirs, but even in trying so hard to show his gentlemanliness and sophistication, the brilliance of the novel is in how he can’t escape from the charges against him, especially as he has to establish he is writing the book from the Fleet Prison where he languishes, and that an outside narrator takes over for the epilogue of his story due to his untimely death in the writing process.I recommend buying this book on kindle if you can. The book makes references to historical figures from the 15th century to the 17th century, and information on landmarks, politicians, and philosophers of the time that really shape the style and structure that Thackeray conjures up in Barry’s autobiographical narrative. The use of prose to portray Barry as prodigious is one of the best uses of unreliable narration throughout the book, and appreciating that is to appreciate the wonders and enchantments of the archaic language and time period that the book posits. It is like a Time Machine that Barry’s eloquence allows us easy access to, but despite his sense of authoritarian control, Barry is forced to confront his own demons, especially as his story nears its end.
