This novel is purportedly about artistic genius, and it also has some of the power of a good Conrad yarn. But one unavoidable element is the pervasive sexism on a gargantuan scale. The role of woman in human existence is clear: Love and be loyal to your man, but only if he is man enough to beat you and otherwise be indifferent to you. I'm not exaggerating. It's not just the genius artist but the "worldly-wise" narrator and even most of the female characters who bear testimony to this ethos.Now, being a good liberal, and the very sort of man who isn't a man by these criteria, I cannot accept this point of view. However, being also a philosopher I recognize that I may be suffering from wishful thinking and a distaste for reality. Our views on how things ought to be is largely a function of how we would like them to be, which in turn is largely a reflection of the way we ourselves are. So I can hardly rule out that those who are otherwise constituted from myself may have a different view of the matter, which has as much validity as mine.You of course can judge for yourself on the nature of men and women (in the heterosexual mold). It is also a separate question whether the merits of this novel as literature can transcend any shortcomings in the attitudes it conveys and apparently endorses. I found the writing moderately absorbing. If the novel is a classic, it is for the theme and the extreme portrayal of a type.And the intended theme is surely a significant one. It is not only art but, really, anything that can become the object of passionate pursuit, and the question raised by the novel is whether moral judgment is relevant to such a pursuit when it seriously conflicts with moral norms. A moralist has two options: to condemn the pursuit in such cases, or to make some kind of moral allowance for it. There seems to be a bit of both going on in this novel.But there is also a third option, namely to carve out a part of ethical space for passionate pursuits regardless of their moral or even prudential implications. This happens to be the topic of a new book in philosophy by Matthew J. Dennis called "Cultivating Our Passionate Attachments." I credit the book for its identifying this option and considering it in scholarly and analytical detail. In fact it was this book that steered me to Gauguin and Maugham's novel. (Alas, I cannot recommend this book to a layperson, since the writing is dense; in fact, it's a hard slog even for a philosopher, since the writing is awful, rife with both syntactical errors and repetition to an unconscionable degree.)Maugham's novel can serve as a case study for pondering this issue. So can the 1942 film based on the novel, which is quite faithful to it and very well done. Indeed, it is like watching the novel turned into a play, which makes sense given the many "good lines" in the novel.
