Robert Caro's comprehensive, magisterial "Master of the Senate" focuses a detailed eye on LBJ's years in the Senate (1949-1960). But, as the Senate itself is as important a "character" in this tome as is the Texas politician himself, the first section is a brief history of the Senate. The author takes off from Washington's description of the Senate as a place to cool things down, which gives him time to explain the concept of unlimited debate, so vital to the Senate's way of doing things, and to paint a portrait of the early greats of what he calls the Golden Age of the Senate--the age of Calhoun, Clay, and Webster.And then, when LBJ finally arrives on the scene, the author depicts LBJ's rise to the leadership and vividly demonstrates the Texan's ability to fashion legislative sausage. There, the book is absolutely brilliant. LBJ spent his early days in the Senate sitting at his desk, watching how things were done, attaching himself to Richard Russell of Georgia as a mentor (Mr. Caro includes a mini-bio of Russell, and shows, too, how the Southern leader cooled down the hysteria created by Truman's sacking of MacArthur). Finally, with Russell's sponsorship, LBJ rose to the leadership. The author emphasizes how LBJ could almost instantly size up the way things would go just by watching, listening, and listening especially to what had not been said. And finally, as he desperately sought a compromise Civil Rights bill in 1957, he noticed a Senate colleague just sitting at his desk, watching. And when he went over to talk to him, he found the opening he needed. This is great stuff.However, Mr. Caro, who seemed to have been desperately seeking answers to questions that have no answers as he wrote this volume of his ongoing series, spends too much time trying to figure out whether the Master believed in civil rights, or was simply a cynical exploiter. And that turns those chapters of the book into something on the order of a Hollywood bio-pic, as we see LBJ (inevitably played by dialogue-coached George Clooney) sitting on a chair in the pool outside his ranch house back in Texas, in late June 1957, in despair over his failure so far to have effected the compromise he wanted. (String music in a minor key playing on the soundtrack of course.) Maybe so, or maybe he was just staying out of the spotlight, playing possum.And of course this Hollywood aspect is impelled by Mr. Caro's insistence on making all of LBJ's liberal enemies (he had a few liberal friends, notably Hubert Humprhey, too) heroic. There is a section in which, in order to win favors with Texas oil interests, LBJ demolished a federal energy commission chairman, Leland Olds. The latter is portrayed as a knight with a shining countenance and overlooks the fact that had Mr. Olds been a bit more sharp politically, he might have been better able to defend himself (he certainly had a case).But what does it matter in the end? Johnson was what he was. His liberal enemies were what they were. The civil rights bill was, of course, eventually passed, and Mr. Caro shows us how LBJ put that sausage together, and does so brilliantly.Notes and asides: There are >40 illustrations.
