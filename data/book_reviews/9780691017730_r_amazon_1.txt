Only Neumann could have written this book... I've reread it several times over the years and am continually amazed at how succinctly the inner struggle of the artist is captured; how profound and important his role in the culture is, even though he's acting against the cultural canon--against the dominant values of his time. As a result, these Great Individuals, as Neumann calls them, lead tragic and lonely lives. Their destiny is to cultivate a renewal of culture through their creative productions but to be misunderstood in their own time."The creative power of the unconscious seizes upon the individual with the autonomous force of an instinctual drive and takes possession of him without the least consideration for the individual, his life, his happiness, or his health. The creative impulse springs from the collective; like every instinct it serves the will of the species and not of the individual. Thus the creative man is an instrument of the transpersonal..." (p.98).The thesis of the book is this: Emerging changes in the culture--seismic shifts in consciousness--are felt first by the artists and great creative individuals. They have an acute sensitivity that allows them to tap into the collective unconscious; but the best they can do is to express it symbolically or dramatically. They're not yet able to articulate it consciously, yet they feel it in the deepest part of their soul; and their genius enables them to transmute it into a great work of art that encapsulates it. Through this creative process, the artist individuates; but to the extent that he is exceptional, his work also has a transpersonal dimension to it. He's fulfilling a collective need--by giving expression to something that his surrounding culture is not yet able to recognize.I suspect that one would have to be a "creative" type themselves to fully appreciate this work; but for those who are--you will see something in what Neumann describes of these Great Individuals, in yourself--albeit on a smaller scale.Read moreRead less
