"His rich and brilliant baroque writing style is developed in the extremely long sentences embedded with complex subordinate parts."  That's actually a description of Faulkner but it also describes the writing style of John Muirhead, author of "Those Who fall." Honestly, during the first few chapters I was about to hit the silks! I was coming off recent reads of several Adam Makos books which are a well researched, literary version of comfort food.  This book presented more of an acquired taste. By the 4th chapter I was hooked.The writing style is indeed Faulkneresque. Take this passage for example; "The sky and all the contours of the land were now consumed by the darkness. But it wasn't night. It was like a sorrow. The sorrow of a final tired day of a ruined world. And the last to perish before us was Max's cigarette which he flipped away in an arc of brave contempt that died in a tiny spit of anger on the muddy road." Wow! But like I said, it grows on you. Or at least grew on me.As some other reviews have pointed out, lots of profanity. You might expect foul language in a book about war but you might be surprised at the sheer volume of "spicy" language in this book. Again, it grows on you. Or perhaps you become numb to it. Like you became numb to the use of the F-word in The Big Lebowski. At first, shocking. Then sort of an annoyance. Eventually barely noticeable. But the dialogue is great. The story is fascinating. The incredible experiences of the author should be told and retold. I wouldn't call it a "page-turner" but it held my interest and I feel as though I had been a stow-away on many of those bombing missions. All of that aside, the story immediately dumps you into the middle of the war. You don't really know where you are or what your objective is. You certainly don’t know what the big picture is. Your perspective is limited to the view of a single man's day-to-day struggle to survive the horrors of war. I've been in a war and that is very much what war is like. You plod along with tunnel-vision one day at a time only seeing, if not understanding, the small part you are to play in the bigger scheme of things.
