King embarked on the quest for the Dark Tower back in 1982. The Gunslinger is a fantasy with a Wild West setting (though it’s not set in this world). The protagonist, Roland, is on the trail of a mystery ‘man in black’, following him across a desert. Along the way, there are shootouts and mutants - and a Lazarus-style resurrection. And a boy called Jake who appears to have died in our world and found himself in this mysterious alternate dimension, if that’s what it is. Roland is on a mission to kill the man in black and find the Dark Tower - the nexus of all worlds. I read The Gunslinger as a teenager, probably in the late 1980s or early 1990s. I was (and am) a King horror fan. I then read some more of the  Dark Tower series - which at that time was still developing - but somewhere along the way I stopped. Like Roland’s world, I had ‘moved on’ - with a vague intention of revisiting. It’s taken me until now (2024) to do so… I remember being impressed by King referencing Hey Jude (I’m a big Beatles fan) and TS Eliot’s The Waste Land (I think the latter must come later in the series, though my memory had been that it was mentioned in The Gunslinger). I then became fairly obsessed with Eliot, going on to study his work at university. These novels were formative and memorable. It’s also a commitment of time to reconnect with this universe - assuming I go on to read the whole series, which is my aim. King is a little disparaging about his Dark Tower debut. He offers an assurance in the foreword that it’s not representative of the novels - and that later titles were an improvement. That said, there is something about Gunslinger which continues to fascinate and compel. I vividly remember certain sentences and passages (though I couldn’t recall a lot of the plot, and King has added in more material since the original publication). Clearly King was drawn back to The Dark Tower and has now finished off the cycle, we assume, unless more spinoffs are in the works. The writing style is a little more ornate and portentous than we’re used to from King (who says this is plainly a novel written by a young man). But it does broadly suit the material.
