I first read this about forty years ago, and pulled it off the shelf to re-read in this week’s visit to Florence.  Set in that city at the end of the fifteenth century, it’s a dense, compelling tale about the proud, compassionate Romola and her relations with her embittered father striving to be remembered for his scholarship, the attractive, talented Tito who “would like no-one to suffer” [p168], and the brilliant but flawed Savonarola.  The story encompasses real historical events and people (including Savonarola, who was the de facto ruler of the Florentine republic in 1494-8), and cleverly uses the fictional characters to give the reader an insight into the impact of events.In the story, there's a tension between Romola and her father, who - despite her helping him with his work - complains about the "wandering, vagrant propensity of the feminine mind" [p97].  Tito appears as the salvation of them both; he puts his scholarship at her father's disposal, and professes his love for Romola: "when she was near him, and looked at him with her sincere hazel eyes, he was subdued by a delicious influence as strong and inevitable as those musical vibrations which take possession of us with a a rhythmic empire that no longer ceases then we desire it to begin again" [p170].  That love is requited: "each was so beautiful to the other; each was experiencing that undisturbed mutual consciousness for the first time" [p235], although the reader knows by now that Tito is not all he seems to be.Perhaps it's the style and trends of the time, but his descent into deception and betrayal is skillfully rendered with some general observations from the author - e.g. "people are apt to think that resolutions taken on their own behalf will be firm" [p242], as is the effect on his relationship with Romola: "hard speech between those who have loved is hideous in the memory, like the sight of greatness and beauty sunk into vice and rags" [p393].  Tito is suave in his dealings with collaborators, and hides his lack of respect, saying things like: "'my time, which I could wish to be eternity in your service, is not entirely my own this morning'" [p603].  We're told he "meant always to conduct himself so ably that if he acted in opposition to the standard of other men they should not be aware of it" [p560] but, in extremis, when confronted by Romola about his schemes, he seizes her wrists "with all his masculine force" and whispers bitterly: "'I am master of you.  You shall not set yourself in opposition to me'" [p483] and, more levelly, says things such as "'Your attention is thoroughly awake to what I am saying?'" [p490].Later, Romola encounters Savonarola in a powerful scene.  His first words to her are "Kneel, my daughter, for the Angel of Death is present, and waits while the message of heaven is delivered: bend thy pride before it is bent for thee by a yoke of iron" [p213].  Later, she meets him again, and is struck by the way his face "conveyed to her of interest in her and care for her apart from any personal feeling [...] a gaze in which simple human fellowship expressed itself as a strongly-felt bond" [p429].Once again, perhaps it's a manifestation of attitudes of the times (when the book was written, and the period in which it's set) but, considering the three men in Romola's life, Tito invariably calls her "my Romola" (an epithet which her father uses occasionally) whilst Savonarola calls her "my daughter" (no doubt reflecting his pastoral role).  These small tokens of ownership and dominion in the personal sphere represent how women were viewed by men (it's a little way off the point, but recall that one of the reasons the author adopted a male non-de-plume was because she wanted to escape the prejudice associated with a female author's name, wanting her work to be taken seriously).I greatly enjoyed this story once again.  Some parts of it work better than others - for example, the sweet, trusting Tessa is a character with little depth or understanding, and it's always been difficult to introduce historical figures into a novel without them appearing gratuitous - thus, on p258, we find a "figure of a young faun playing the flute, modelled by a promising youth named Michaelangelo Buonarotti".  And some of the political struggles are more difficult to fathom, although they're a good illustration of the invariability of the struggles between (inevitably, male-dominated) factions for control and power.  But it's a good story, with an evocative sense of place and time, particularly if you're on the spot where these things happened.  Recommended.Read moreRead less
