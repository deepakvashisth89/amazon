I used the above motto of the US Navy SEALs as the bottom line in my review of the film version of "Return of the King" seventeen years ago.  That is because the hobbits in this book are essentially Special Forces.  While Pippin and Merry embed with indigenous forces and play crucial parts in their victory over Dark Lord Sauron, Sam and Frodo go behind enemy lines to destroy the artifact from which derives all his power, the One Ring.The book doesn't necessarily stand out among books the way "Return of the King" stands out among movies, but any flaws in Book Five, covering Pippin, Merry and the forces they are embedded with, are redeemed by Book Six, the first three chapters in which cover Sam and Frodo's heroic journey to cast the Ring into the lava that continually pours forth from Mount Doom.  The rest of Book Six covers Aragorn's crowning and marriage, the return of the hobbits to the Shire, the scourging of the Shire (it's true this should not have been left out of the movie) and the Ringbearers' final leave-taking from Middle Earth.As I read Tolkien's descriptions of the lands through which the hobbits move, I was reminded of Robert Silverberg's Majipoor series.  Silverberg was only one of the many writers of the following generation who were obviously deeply influenced by Tolkien.  The narrative part of "Return of the King" is a short 278 pages.  The appendices (which I did not read) cover an additional 104 pages.When I came out with a qualified declaration of my Christianity on Facebook last month, I named three theologians who had influenced me to do so.  An ex-girlfriend asked me why Tolkien was not among these three.  Now that I have read "Return of the King," I venture to guess is it more influenced by Norse sagas than by the Book of Revelations which some evangelicals would like to think he was really writing about.  This of course is not to say that Tolkien's Christianity was unimportant to his writing, only that he was not saying what some twenty-first-century Americans might wish he was saying.
