Life is full of death: the large, permanent, literal death of a loved one or self, and a million little deaths that happen in between.  The end of childhood.  The loss of a way of thinking about the world.  Breaking up a relationship.  Anything, any end, is the death of something that existed before.  And with each change there comes the sensation of being separate from the world, because every death – large or small – is an individual experience that cannot be wholly shared with someone else – they are too busy experiencing their own loss to experience yours, as well.  They can sympathize, later.  But the moment is yours and yours alone.  Sometimes the moment is always yours alone, even later when you can sympathize.  How do we deal with this loneliness?  How are we not always destroyed by it?McNally writes in answer to such questions.  Almost Home illustrates with compassion this human condition known as loneliness.  As a disease, it destroys us – Patrick’s mother fought the battle of loneliness and guilt and lost.  Sometimes, we cope by ignoring it – Elizabeth’s father fights the battle of loneliness every day by putting his energy into his work and his daughter and ignoring himself.  Or perhaps we do not realize we are lonely at all, fortunate to have a buffer – Patrick has his dog and the unconditional love that soothes away the sharp edges of loneliness as his mother spirals downward and his sister leaves in the wake of his father’s suicide.But it is Elizabeth who owns her loneliness and recognizes it is not a disease but a part of the self.  She knows she feels lonely even while with those she is closest to.  She wanted a dog to buffer the pain and make her feel loved but was never allowed to have one until she learned how to buffer it on her own.  Elizabeth, who felt lonely every day, was able to see the loneliness of others because of it and knew herself more truly as well.  McNally, in the world of fiction that allows us to have the most intimate and honest of conversations with ourselves and others, offers this: loneliness will not always destroy us because loneliness is how we learn to connect with the hollow places in others – and then, maybe, we can properly mourn all the personal deaths that brought us home, that place we feel at last not alone, and let it go.Read moreRead less
