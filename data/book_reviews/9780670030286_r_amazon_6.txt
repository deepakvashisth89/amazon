Synaptic Self: How Our Brains Become Who We Are, explains how the small gaps between the dendrites of our neuronal cells define how we act, feel and what we do. The author of this book is a professor at the University of New York. He teaches neurobiology. I was afraid that he would uses too difficult of terms for me to understand. This book uses, in general, terms that the average biology student would understand and anything else LeDoux usually defines. I feel like the format of his writing style was somewhat easy to understand. The way LeDoux laid his topics out over the course of the book was a little bit confusing. He seemed to jump around a lot and never fully explained the connection to the topic. Overall, I would rate this book a three stars out of five based on level of difficulty, the vocabulary, scientific experiments and basic comprehension of the points LeDoux was expressing.In order to explain how the synapses define our self personalities, the concept of `self' must be defined. LeDoux begins this journey by doing just that. Once a basic understanding of the self is laid down, the author describes how the nervous system works. He describes briefly the different theories of the cells in the nervous system to help the reader understand the existence of synapses. Then the author describes different nervous system communication methods including neurotransmitters. After a description of the nervous system, LeDoux uses experiments in his lab to describe how memory helps to build our personalities. He describes some of the brain structures and their roles in the formation of memories. Since LeDoux is interested in the fear mechanism, he uses that as an example. Next, a few mental disabilities such as schizophrenia and depression are discussed along with some drugs to treat them. Finally, LeDoux attempted to tie all these ideas together to conclude that the self is a result of the actions of the synapses in our brain.The basic chapter layout is as followed. LeDoux starts the chapters off with a quote that has to do with the topic of the chapter. There usually is a section about some background of the topic, say for example brain development. A brief explanation of the steps in brain development follows a description of the topic. Then there would be a picture trying to help with the understanding of the topic. The author then explains how the chapter topic is related or supports the previous topics.The book starts off trying to define what `self' is. LeDoux's plan was to first make the reader understand what self is and then explain how the synapses define the self. The chapter title was "Seeking the Self" giving the notion that the author is trying to reveal his views on what the self is. LeDoux explains the idea of the soul, which defines each human and he goes off explaining something about the physical and mental self being different objects. To me at least, this part was a little confusing. It is unclear if he was supporting the soul idea or just saying that it was a common belief. Next, the author pretty much says the same thing but instead of explaining the soul, he explains the mind stating that mind and body and different things. It seems very repetitive.  LeDoux explains that even animals have their own definition of what self is. He states that humans can never understand the definition of a dog's self because we cannot become a dog. These are empty words because he still has not given an understandable definition of what self is. It seems like this and many other chapters keep jumping from many different subjects all weakly trying to describe the self. The psychology to the philosophy and some experiments are all tied together to try to explain the self. Almost all the chapters of the book have excellent information in them but it was difficult for me to connect them all back to the topic of the book; the synapse defining who we are.  It may be the author's writing style or just the overall difficulty of the topic to understand, but I did not fully comprehend the intentions of the author in this section.Even though the first topic was difficult for me to understand, the next section which describes the mechanics of the nervous system, was much clearer. There are two obstacles when tackling this subject. Since LeDoux is targeting an audience that does not have much background in neuroscience, the vocabulary must be limited to some degree. This is the first obstacle; using words that most people in his target audience will understand.  I believe he assumes that there is a little background in brain anatomy and overall biology because he mentions the hypothalamus without clearly stating the function or even that it was a structure of the brain. Later on the author does talk about it as being a brain structure but not initially.  Most of the time however, he defines the unknown word quickly after saying it. For example, when he describes the dendrites for the first time a picture was used to help with the description. LeDoux includes many pictures to help with his context. When he described the neuron, he had provided a simple but effective picture of a neuron with labels of all the features he mentioned. There are also complex pictures to help the reader understand more challenging ideas that he introduces when words alone may be too much. One example of this is when he is talking about divergence of neurons and how they branch off in many different ways.  There are many examples in the book where LeDoux uses a complex picture to supplement his text and ease the reader into a better understanding of the topic. This leads us to the second obstacle which pertains to the author explaining new concepts in such a way as not to overwhelm the reader with too complex of ideas. There is a lot to the concepts briefly introduced in this book and LeDoux takes great care not to go too much into detail and cause the reader to lose sight of the topic.  By utilizing pictures and a somewhat limited vocabulary to elaborate on his ideas, LeDoux keeps the readers interested but does not exasperate them with too much information.Overall, LeDoux's book Synaptic Self: How Our Brains Become Who We Are was relatively easy to understand vocabulary wise. The author avoids using elaborate vocabulary so that the average person will be able to understand. Another positive aspect is that LeDoux touches momentarily on the topics discussed and does not go into too much detail. The frequent use of pictures to help illustrate the author's words helped immensely in the understanding of the more complex topics. However, the author's writing style did get confusing for me because his topics and how they all connected to the book topic. Every chapter seemed to be a new topic and was weakly connected to the general topic of the synapse creating the self. The information in the chapters was easy to understand and I found to be intriguing. Despite this, I failed to see strong evidence that all this information could be united to help explain how the synapse defines the human self. I feel this book deserves three stars out of five because I loved the information, the experiments and the way he explains within each chapter. The final chapter was somewhat difficult to read because there was a lack of understanding of how the chapter connected to all the previous chapters. I could not fully understand the connectivity of these chapters to the overall book topic.
