I had never read George Eliot - something about titles like "The Mill on the Floss" put me off, perhaps.  They are also big books, which means that the motivation to start has to be higher than for, say, "Animal Farm." But I found myself with only a copy of Middlemarch for the Kindle reader on my phone, so I opened it up.What a revelation! George Eliot is a great writer.  Her pages are packed with the most piercingly acute observation of human personality, and her commentaries extend from the psychological to the philosophical, and are a delight to read.The novel was written between 1867 and 1872, to be serialised in Blackwood's Magazine in eight bi-monthly instalments. When the first instalment was published in 1871, only three had been completed.  What a difference a deadline makes.  I'm sure that she had a pretty clear idea of where the novel was headed by the time of that first episode, though.It is set in 1832, at the time of the First Reform Act - one of the greatest triumphs of Parliamentary democracy - although that has little direct bearing on the story.  The central character of the story is Dorothea Brooke, and revolves around her family of sister Celia and their uncle Arthur; her fascinated dedication to the dry and scholarly Rev. Casaubon, whom she marries to general dismay, only to find that he is both emotionally and intellectually barren.  Following her husband's early death, her story focusses on the mutual attraction between her and Casaubon's cousin, Will Ladislaw. A parallel stream of the narrative concerns the Vincy family: Fred and his sister Rosamund, children of Walter and Lucy.  Within this narrative are two stories: Fred and Mary Garth, and Rosamund and doctor Tertius Lydgate.  The Vincy story was the original focus of the novel, which only shifted to Dorothea some years after it was initiated.These intertwined streams of the story wend their way around a wealth of minor characters.Dorothea, Ladislaw and Lydgate are all Romantic characters. The link to Romanticism is drawn explicitly when Will Ladislaw, aspiring at the time to be an artist, has Dorothea pointed out to him by a decidedly Romantic German artist friend, entranced by her beauty. Will has already met, and been disturbingly impressed by, Dorothea in Middlemarch, but this chance meeting is in an art gallery in Rome, where Mr and Mrs Casaubon are honeymooning.Will abandons art for an accidental career as a radical writer in the political context of Reform Britain.  Lydate has a vision of major medical advances, which await discovery by a such a dedicated medical scientist as he sees himself to be.  These aspirations fall prey to circumstances; even as Dorothea's expectations of a married life of mutual dedication to the great work of Casaubon's scholarship fall prey to the sterile reality.In contrast to these dazzlingly failures is the bucolic virtue of Mary Garth and her parents Caleb and Susan.  This family is the moral rock on which this novel is built; but from this rock, the most astonishing Romantic fireworks are launched.The Romantic stream may have evaporated into the pitiless sands of today's post-post-modern paganism, a desert incapable of sustaining, or even of desiring, life; but for those like me, whose childhood playground was the 50s, the influence of Romanticism was still pervasive, and it is painful to see so much our own self-delusion and disillusionment presaged and dissected so clinically and yet so sympathetically.Read this book.Read moreRead less
