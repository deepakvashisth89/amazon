I loved reading Rebecca by Daphne du Maurier. Definitely five out of five stars. It’s one of those books that sticks with you long after you put it down and doesn’t feel dated. From the very first line I was drawn into Manderley and the way the past seems alive there. The presence of Rebecca is everywhere, even though she’s dead. You feel her in every room, in every memory, and in how everyone around her remembers her. At first, I too fell in love with Rebecca or the memory of who she was.The narrator, the second Mrs de Winter, starts out so shy and insecure of herself that she doesn’t even have a name in the book. She feels small and invisible compared to Rebecca, and I think that’s what makes her so relatable on a psychological level. She spends so much time comparing herself to Rebecca that she loses sight of who she is. That insecurity, that constant worry that she’s not enough, made me think about how social expectations and ideas about what makes a woman “worthy” can shape someone’s identity. She is surrounded by people who either idolize Rebecca or remind her of how different she is, and that makes her internal struggle feel real. We travel with her as she struggles to form an independent identity as the story unfolds.Maxim de Winter is a deeply conflicted character. At first he seems like this mysterious, brooding romantic hero, but as the story goes on he reveals complicated emotions and a heavy burden from his past. The way he withholds the truth about Rebecca creates tension and distance between him and the narrator. His silence holds power, and it isn’t until he shares his secret that the narrator begins to finally see him and herself more clearly. Mrs Danvers is one of the most chilling characters I have ever read. Her loyalty to Rebecca and her cruel treatment of the narrator turn Manderley into a psychological battlefield. She makes the narrator feel like she doesn’t belong and like she can never live up to Rebecca’s memory.Rebecca herself is fascinating because she is never actually alive in the story except in memory and description. She becomes this mythic figure that everyone clings to, and through those memories we see how reputations and stories can control people even after someone is gone. The way Rebecca is talked about makes you question whether the idea of a person can be more powerful than the real person ever was.The ending of the novel left me thinking long after I finished it. Not spoil the ending, but ending revelation makes the moral center of the story complicated. They lose Manderley in the end, and watching the estate burn feels like a kind of punishment or consequence for everything that happened. It isn’t legal justice, but it feels like emotional and symbolic justice, and it leaves the future of Maxim and the narrator uncertain.I also love Alfred Hitchcock’s 1940 film version of Rebecca. The movie is haunting and suspenseful in its own way, but because of the Hollywood censorship rules at the time Hitchcock had to change how Rebecca dies. That change softens some of the psychological weight of the story, but the film still keeps the eerie atmosphere and mystery of Manderley. The way Mrs Danvers is shown in the movie, with her cold stare and obsession, is unforgettable.Reading Rebecca felt like watching a psychological case study unfold inside a Gothic mansion. The narrator’s transformation from someone insecure and unsure to someone who begins to find her own voice felt powerful. The novel’s exploration of memory, identity, love, jealousy, and power made it so much more than just a haunting love story for me. It blended suspense with deep insight into how people are shaped by their past and by the people around them.
