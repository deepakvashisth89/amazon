I had been wanting to read The Education of Henry Adams for several years, but couldn’t find an in-print edition. Not even our normally excellent Billings Public Library had a copy. But recently I checked back, on Amazon, found several available editions, and ordered the Library of America option. I’m glad I did.However, I certainly can’t go along with that (apocryphal?) scorekeeper who termed the tome “the greatest American non-fiction book of the 20th century.” Adams’s professed aim was to construct a new, dynamic, more scientific theory of history. In that, he failed miserably. Here is my take on why that is.First of all, he grew up a Boston Brahmin, surrounded by and in the extended families of what might well pass for “the New England conservative ‘aristocracy’.” I vaguely remember a New York shoe salesman, in the film The Late George Apley, making some disparaging remark about a recently served customer. His fellow worker asked him, “Bostonian?” whereupon he nodded. Henry was simply too insulated from the man-in-the-street. In spite of visiting Paris annually, if not more often, he never mentions the Paris Commune of 1871. Was that beneath his professional historian’s notice?Near the end of the book he extols the diplomatic service of his old friend John Hay, Secretary of State under McKinley and Teddy Roosevelt. “He [Hay] had brought the great Atlantic powers into a working system, and even Russia seemed to be about to be dragged into a combine of intelligent equilibrium . . . For the first time in 1500 years a true Roman pax was in sight, and would, if it succeeded, owe its virtues to him.” Adams is writing of the situation in 1905, a scant nine years before the guns of August put the lie to his hopes. I suspect that the various ententes and treaties and deals that Hay had constructed, his legacy, were if anything counterproductive to sustained peace.This lack of realism when it counts, together with so much (male) Bluestocking blather about Unity and Multiplicity and the Virgin Mary, convinced me that Henry Adams’s education was – in spite of his knowledge of literature, music, architecture, and history – a dismal failure.I was out of town when I finished the book, earlier this week, and was glad to also have Tom Apostol’s Mathematical Analysis with me, to clear my mind and give me an antidote of clear sentences constructed of words with definite meanings.Read moreRead less
