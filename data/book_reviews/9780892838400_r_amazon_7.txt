First, a little background: I am a convert from atheism, and in my three years as a Catholic, I have seen little difference between what I see going on in the various parishes I have attended and the religion I left. I have spoken with many Catholics, and almost all proudly state that they do not believe everything the Church teaches; I can count the exceptions I have met on one hand. Few priests I have heard are any better, and those who are truly orthodox are restrained from speaking the full truth by a hyper-liberal laity. And don't even get me started on the rampant liturgical abuses! There have been times in which I was seriously tempted to look elsewhere to have my very real spiritual needs met. I hoped this book would provide some answers, or at least a way to deal with all this in a more Christlike manner.Boy, was I wrong.The book shows promise in the beginning by providing stories of people (not just liberals) who have been let down by the Church, and then talking about how we should focus on the four last things (death, judgment, Heaven, and Hell). So far, so good. But then the author talks for a long time about how we need to acknowledge our own sins (anyone reading a book like this instead of just pointing fingers probably already knows this), take time to pray instead of being busy all the time (which is an obvious point), and learning what we believe and why (again, a Catholic who does not know his faith is not likely to have a problem with the modern Church). So far, I've described half the book. I'm not exaggerating.The author then tells us to work for reform in the Church. This sounds like a good idea until we actually try it. One method the author suggests is evangelizing. I spent two years evangelizing to many dozens of people of all kinds, using airtight arguments I had spent many hours learning from the world's leading apologists. Nearly all of them told me straight out that they preferred to keep doing their cherished sins rather than converting, and the few success stories turned into failure stories when they, too, decided that sin was more important than salvation. Not everyone has the skills to evangelize: "And his gifts were that some should be apostles, some prophets, some evangelists, some pastors and teachers..." (Ephesians 4:11)Another suggestion is to talk to the priest, or other leaders in the Church, about liturgical abuses and other problems. But often, this action is ignored, leaving us even more frustrated. As an example, one such incident was when I heard a cashier at a Catholic bookstore that was part of a church tell a blasphemous joke, and I tried to speak to the manager; all I got was laughed at, and as hard as I pushed, nothing changed. Perhaps someone with better social skills could have done better. But the point here is the same: not everyone can do this.For someone like myself, the book incites anger against the Church without providing a solution.Read moreRead less
