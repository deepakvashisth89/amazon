My favorite parts of this book was the short story in the beginning, Miriam of the Garden, and the chapter about symbols in paintings and medieval watermarks. The rest of the book does have some interesting points, but in general I found Ms. Starbird's conclusions far-fetched and trying too hard to make things add up. Just like the theories in Holy Blood, Holy Grail, a book that she bases her believes on, there's not many facts (or ideas) that believable connect Jesus and Mary Magdalene to the royal families of France and to the Freemasons.Having said that, it's clear that Ms. Starbird has done tons of research, knows her bible (and believes every single word of it!), and has thought deeply about how to connect medieval symbols to Jesus and Mary Magdalene.It's just that this book (like Holy Blood, Holy Grail) assumes that all the legends of the 12th and 13th centuries are true and that they knew exactly what had happened in the first century. It's like believing that we who live today would know exactly what had gone on in the 9th century. One thousand one hundred years had passed between the time that Jesus and Mary Magdalene lived and when the Cathars, Templars, and Freemasons wrote their stories. That's a long long time.In addition, why would it matter that someone was a descendant of Jesus and Mary Magdalene? There would have been thousands, maybe tens of thousands of descendants by now, and they would just be ordinary Joes and Janes. And if it's the spiritual message that matters, they have desperately failed in keeping it alive.Still, I recommend this book to people who are fascinated by the possibility of a surviving bloodline, and especially to those who love conspiracy theories.Read moreRead less
