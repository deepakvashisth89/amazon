Reading “Shakey” has been an odyssey. Nearly 800 pages covering his life and career from the start through 2000 (McDonough hints at the collaboration with Booker T and the MGs that yielded “Are You Passionate?” even though Young would still release “Silver & Gold” prior to that.)What makes this book essential is its level of access, and McDonough’s commitment to painting a full and sober portrait of Young. In between narrative stretches, the book inserts long interview transcripts with Young that look like they could have been scripted by Cormac McCarthy. And often these interviews serve as contradictory counterpoints to the bigger story, which is often sourced with interviews by the most important figures in Young’s world.The result is an overwhelming read that tests your patience, but ultimately rewards your perseverance—much like Young’s work. I’m thankful I waited until now to read this tome because of the simple fact that Young has finally been releasing his archives, which are teased throughout the book. It was a true joy to go through the archives (volumes 1-3, plus several live albums) as McDonough lists tracks and takes very few would have been able to hear back in 1999. Like McDonough, the mid-70s run (“Tonight’s the Night,” “On the Beach,” “Homegrown,” and “Zuma”) recordings were the highlight for me. Although, close second was revisiting the 80s Geffen run, which no longer felt like a middle finger to Geffen, but Young’s clumsy efforts to escape the horrors of his life during that period.Which leads me to the takeaway of the book. Neil Young is a truly complex individual. He is maddening, yet inspiring. I wanted to hate him, but like those in his orbit, I never could. His vulnerability and honesty, contrasted with his aloofness and mercurial nature, had me falling deeper in love with the music that has soundtracked my life.So, yeah, I recommend this book—but only if you’re a real head. 800 pages is a crazy investment, but it is worth it.Read moreRead less
