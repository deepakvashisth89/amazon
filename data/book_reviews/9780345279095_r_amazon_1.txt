I first read this book in 1985 or so. I remember how delicious some of the phrases were, how rich the writing was, how real everyone seemed to me, though it was a world I was far removed from.Years later, a sentence from the book came rushing back to me: "I swung my legs into the car as if they belonged to someone else," spoken by the troubled protagonist, lapsed Catholic Isabel Moore. She describes, in such a brilliant way, the feeling someone might have when they are doing something they know they shouldn't.It struck me as such a brilliant sentence, and even though I am not a woman, I am not a Catholic, and I did not live the life of the character in the book, I understood a lot of her pain.In essence, "Final Payments" is about a woman coming to terms with the loss of her father (after caring for him as an invalid for more than a decade), the loss of her 20s (as she took care of him) and her struggles to embrace what she might become. As a woman of that era (perhaps mid-1970s), the concept of "women's liberation" was foreign to her. As such, she did not really think things through with men, as evidenced by the two she chooses to spend time with in this book.She also must deal with, once and for all, an obnoxious, horrid woman from her past, and until she can, she cannot move forward, hence the title of the book. It is the combination of these things that create a rich story, most of which is Isabel's observations of herself, life around her, and others. And Mary Gordon's prose is intelligent and alive and, on occasion, incredibly funny. I had to stop myself from laughing out loud when I read the book on trains or buses.I am probably as old as Mary Gordon; I lived through this era. She captures the voices of specific people you might have met during that time, and you feel you are in the same rooms with her on every single page. Part of the story includes Isabel sinking into a deep, deep depression. As someone who (barely) survived 20 years of depression, I almost slipped back into it again as I read Mary's brilliant writing. It's very distinctive and in many ways I didn't want the book to end. But I did want Isabel to forgive herself, to move on with her life, and to realize that there are people in the world that she could interact with and that she could, in fact, have a life other than the one she had when her father was alive, a good life, a great life, maybe. I think, in the end, that is what she realizes too, and with the help of her incredibly well-drawn friends, Liz and Eleanor, I hope that's what happened to her.You may shudder from how she allows herself to be treated by men, but in my experience (I had many female friends when I was younger), the things that happened to her did not seem unusual, even if they were upsetting. So, one must view the story in the context of the times; if you can do that, you will have a rich, rewarding reading experience.This book, at the time I bought it, was not yet available on Kindle. I wonder if it will ever be. But as it wasn't, I purchased the book and had it sent from the US to Japan where I live. Holding a fine paperback book and reading it was such a wonderful experience that I'm not sure I ever want to read a Kindle book again.Highly recommended.Read moreRead less
