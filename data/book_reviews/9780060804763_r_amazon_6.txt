What is it about Sci-Fi writers and Native Americans? Expect some pretty heavy-handed stereotypes of native people being very mystical, noble and stoic while the middle-class palefaces go embarrassingly hysterical at the ecological disaster they've caused.  III it is meant well, it often comes off as pretty patronising with vague allusions to "harmony with nature" and "the primitive wisdom" of other cultures: we British used to write a lot about "the noble savages" whose countries we had colonised in the time of empire, and it's really not cool.Anyway, Kate Wilhelm's heart is in the right place, and this book is that rare beast; science fiction with something to say! There are confrontations of many "grown-up" concepts: the worth of individuals to society; our implicit trust of institutions authorities; the potential for anarchy lurking in society; the economy of sex and security that so many human relationships boil down to. It also needs to be said that there are some shocking and stark depictions of violence in this book that make it less suitable for the younger end of the young-adult audience.The plot is hard to describe; two childhood acquaintances are reacquainted by bizarre circumstances: their fathers were both pioneers of man's first tentative steps into space colonisation, a mission that collapsed when a mysterious drought began to sweep the globe. A son seeks to re-ignite interest in his father's space program, succeeds and discovers a mysterious artefact orbiting the Earth. It is at this point that his fate becomes re-entwined with his childhood friend, who is a language expert. She must be found, but America is suffering economic and environmental collapse, so finding one woman isn't easy...It's a jam-packed story with lots of interesting themes, but sadly, for all this, it's a bit of a sluggish novel. Paradoxically, "Juniper Time" manages to feel as it's got too many ideas packed in and at the same time feels a bit slow in pace. Also, Wilhelm's descriptions are prosaic, which works well when describing the starkness of concentration camps and the tedious politics of academia, but less well in evoking the majesty of the mesa. It's worth a look for Hugo-nomination completists and fans of feminist or environmentally-conscious science fiction, but as usual, Wilhelm aims for the stars... and it all turns out to be a bit of an anticlimax.Read moreRead less
