Monica Fagerholm is, genuinely, the - What adjective to employ? - creepiest, the most unsettling, the most brilliant writer who is completely disregarded in the Anglo-American press.  Her novel, The American Girl, is, on my own list, one of the four great novels published in the first decade of the 21st Century.  She is a genius - a very disturbing, incisive genius.But her books are very difficult to review.  I decided to read this book, her first novel, before delving into the sequel to The American Girl, The Glitter Scene, which has now become available in English translation.So, what is this book about?  Why is it, like The American Girl, so sui-generis? On the evanescent, photographic surface, it's about two families, or actually, three, though the third doesn't play as prominent a role, who, through the early to mid 1960s go on holiday to the beach for the summer, "believing that they could buy the roar of the sea and the happy laughter on the beach, glowing skin, cool evenings, and hot days when the sun has gone down behind the trees, shimmering mosquitoes in the last rays of the sun trickling through the trees over the bay."But the narrator of the book, until the last thirty pages, is Thomas, the prepubescent only child of one of the families. or, rather, he is NOT the narrator but the one through whose eyes we witness the events in the book until the last thirty odd pages.  Who the actual narrator is , throughout the book, is a very tricky question.  A great deal of this beach life is given over to photography and the obsession with it.  Indeed, the title of the book is a teasing name given to a snapshot of Bella and Rosa, the wives/mothers of the two families, lying together on the beach.But underneath all these lovely photographs and home movies something entirely different is transpiring.  A scene between Bella and her son Thomas:"Thomas looks away.  Suddenly he understands something:  She is not really laughing, it just looks as if she were.  What's happening is something else."  And what is the something else, one wonders.  Indeed, it's this wonder that keeps the reader in a sort of existential suspense throughout the novel.There are several passages that hint at what it might be about.  Yes, things are falling apart for the families, but why?  What is happening in the world?  There is a passage that hints at it which deserves to be quoted in full:"Thomas scrambles to the top of the boards and looks around.  It is a windy day.  The wind is all that he can hear.  Thomas stands still.  When he stops thinking about the wind, it is very quiet.  Really quiet.  The kind of quiet when everything vanishes - time, too.  The sky is still overcast, and the light is such that it could be any time of day.  Any day, any year.  Thomas suddenly has a feeling that he is falling out of time.  Becomes outside time, alongside time.  Time is going on somewhere....but in another place.  The feeling is not pleasant."No, it is not pleasant.  The book can not be described as pleasant.  Perhaps life can't.One final quote from the first pages of the book: "If we're to have any hope of understanding one another, we have to speak in metaphors, in images."  But perhaps we can't really understand one another.  Perhaps metaphors and images simply do not suffice.  As suggested by the Götterdämmerung conclusion, which I shan't give away, perhaps we are all constantly falling out of time, imprisoned in our own private universes, alone.Read moreRead less
