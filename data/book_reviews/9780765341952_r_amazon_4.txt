As a kid, I was thoroughly entranced by Azimov's Foundation series. Later in life, I was thoroughly disappointed in Azimov's own extensions to the series.  Kingsbury's "Psychohistorical Crisis" grabbed me from the first page.  And here, 50 years later, I find myself immersed in the universe of Azimov's Foundation.  I savored every single paragraph.  Beginning millennia after the original series, Kingsbury's rich construction is more credible than Azimov's.  Kingbury's knowledge of history and human society puts mine to shame.  And his prose is very easy to read.As an adult, I reject Azimov's premise that society cannot be trusted to determine its own destiny, which must be secretly controlled by a group of intellectual who know better, and who must secretly manipulate the future.  This is the same philosophy which brought Lenin and Mao to power.  All benevolent dictatorships ultimately fail because the people at the top do not have the information necessary to provide for the needs of the people.  A bureaucrat in Moscow has no idea of what kind or how many pair of shoes a farmer in the swamps of Georgia will need.  I hope that we have outgrown our need for benevolent dictators.
