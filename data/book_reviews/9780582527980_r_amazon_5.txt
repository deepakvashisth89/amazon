Chances are if you've encountered "The Thirty-Nine Steps," it was in one of its other two most notable adaptations, the most-often performed play on the regional theater circuit in 2010 (so says the New York Times) or Alfred Hitchcock's classic action thriller, released in 1935 and still timeless for its action packed chases and breathless acts of derring-do.Then how about rounding out the trio with the entertainment that started it all and serves yet today as a prototype for the modern-day espionage thriller? Unlike either the stage or movie versions there is no female love interest, but in the 1915 novel the 39 steps of the title actually are integral to the plot.Richard Hannay, 37, a Scott by birth, engineer by profession and recently returned to Britain after earning his fortune in faraway Bulawayo, Rhodesia, finds his life in the Old Country as "as flat as soda-water that had been standing in the sun." Then. Then, this self-described "best bored man in the United Kingdom," returns to his London flat after dinner to find his new houseguest lying sprawled on his back "skewered to the floor with a knife through his heart."It's enough to make Hannay drop his cigar and break into a cold sweat. The corpse in the hallway has a name, Franklin Scudder. He was an American journalist turned freelance spy, a chance acquaintance of Hannay's, who had important information about a plot to assassinate the Greek premier and drive Europe into war. Hannay had given Scudder temporary refuge in his flat after the plotters had failed in their first attempt to murder the American.So the chase begins, when in a classic case of mistaken identity, Hannay is now the suspected murderer. He is being pursued by the police but also by foreign espionage agents who believe that Hannay is privy to the assassination plot.By train, by stolen motorcar, by horse and wagon, the chase careens and bounces across the English Midlands. Hannay is pursued on foot across down and through hedgerow, across stream and river and from the air and it all ends up on the East Coast of England and the location of the stairway with 39 steps leading down to the sea.The pace is frantic, the timing and circumstance incredible and it's all a great deal of fun in a small tightly wound bundle. It's something easily read in a rainy or wintery afternoon in front of the fireplace. "The 39 Steps" also is something Buchan called in the book's introduction "an elemental tale, a dime novel where the incidents defy the probabilities."
