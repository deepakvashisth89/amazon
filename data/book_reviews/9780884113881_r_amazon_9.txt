Last seen making off with the victim’s dog in Dumb Witness, Captain Hastings takes a trip down memory lane recalling early cases with Poirot.Published in 1974, this is a collection of 18 short stories little altered since they first appeared in periodicals printed between 1923 and 1935. At around 20 pages each, they’re barely more than ideas and I was a bit dubious about reading so many. However, I liked Hastings being back in charge of narration and thought it might be fun working out whether any provided inspiration for subsequent novels.The most obvious candidate is The Submarine Plans. The plot to steal top secret blueprints from a future Prime Minister’s desk is virtually duplicated as The Incredible Theft from Murder In The Mews. The two are so similar it makes you wonder why bother changing a good story at all. Another, which involved a bit more thought, is The Plymouth Express. This time, a train bound for the English south coast is rerouted to continue its murderous journey across France as The Mystery Of The Blue Train. Sadly, turning The Plymouth Express into such an infamous novel doesn’t feel like an improvement either but there’s plenty of other examples where developing these early plots worked magnificently.For example, a recurring theme in Poirot’s cases is the locked room murder mystery and Problem At Sea might well be the Daddy of them all, potentially inspiring classics such as The Murder Of Roger Ackroyd and Hercule Poirot’s Christmas. Another familiar device is Poirot receiving a request for help shortly before that person either dies or disappears. Falling firmly in the former category, How Does Your Garden Grow could well have led the way for The Murder On The Links, Murder On The Orient Express, and Dumb Witness with Third Girl offering a possible variation.Perhaps less obviously, If you liked the deceit used in Lord Edgware Dies then The Veiled Lady could be of interest, or if poker’s your game, then The King Of Clubs might have influenced aspects of Cards On The Table. Likewise, The Market Basing Mystery is a potential source for the ending to the title story in Murder In The Mews. The possibilities are as endless as Hastings’ imagination.As an unexpected bonus, there’s also opportunities to learn things about Poirot you  won’t find in his other stories.First up is The Double Clue, Poirot’s original encounter with the flamboyant Russian Countess Vera Rossakoff. Possibly best known for her descent into Hell via the London Underground during The Capture of Cerberus in The Labours Of Hercules, she’s widely recognised as the only person he ever admitted being attracted to. From this story, it’s quite obvious that Poirot’s head was turned from the beginning, but despite an early mutual respect, the Countesse’s eyes are clearly on the rubies in this case and their paths are going in very different directions. No wonder Poirot stayed single.Next, Poirot makes a totally out of character attempt at self-abasement in The Chocolate Box. In it, he describes the one time he got things wrong and was deceived by an old lady with cataracts. This unique story of failure takes place when Poirot was still a detective in Belgium. It’s not his finest hour and it’s the only time he admits his mistake to a slightly amused Captain Hastings but I appreciated his honesty.And finally, The Third-Floor Flat reveals much more than just a murderer’s name. Friends returning from a night out find the body of their neighbour in a block of London flats where Poirot intriguingly lives under a false identity. Normally, the possibility of Poirot having a secret life would keep me entertained for weeks. However, it’s his sexist revelation that he “once loved a beautiful young English girl - but alas - she could not cook” that blew me away. Every fan knows Poirot’s affection for fine food and the Countess Vera Rossakoff but I never knew there was somebody else! The man is such a dark horse.It turns out I needn’t of worried about reading 18 short stories. This collection was collated from cases written early in Poirot’s career and is full of great moments you won’t find elsewhere. It’s fun, informative, and nostalgic in equal measure and perhaps the best preparation for what lies ahead in Curtain.Read moreRead less
