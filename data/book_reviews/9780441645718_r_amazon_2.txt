The Oxygen Barons — ReviewThere are lunar novels that focus on rockets and engineering.And then there are novels that ask a far more unsettling question: Who owns the very air you breathe?The Oxygen Barons is Gregory Feeley’s remarkable debut and the final volume in Terry Carr’s celebrated third series of the Ace Science Fiction Specials. Set on a terraformed Moon where oxygen itself has become the foundation of wealth and political power, it is both hard science fiction and an incisive meditation on economics, freedom, and colonial society.I spent a cool, fog-softened afternoon at the Eye of Bodhidharma while Edgar the raven watched steam rise from a fresh cup of tea.He inhaled deeply.“People notice fresh bread. They rarely notice fresh air—until someone starts charging for it.”The story follows Galvanix, a citizen of the independent Lunar Republic, where control of oxygen production has become inseparable from political influence and economic power. As competing interests struggle to dominate the Moon’s future, Galvanix discovers that survival depends not only on engineering and science but also on understanding who truly controls essential resources.What especially impressed me was Feeley’s intellectual ambition.The science feels carefully considered.The politics emerge naturally.The economics drive the plot rather than serving as background.The Moon itself becomes a believable society with its own customs, conflicts, and evolving identity.Rather than relying on spectacular action, Feeley builds suspense through ideas.Who controls life-support?Who controls commerce?Who controls freedom?Those questions give the novel lasting relevance.Feeley’s prose is measured and elegant.The pacing rewards thoughtful reading.The world-building is meticulous.Like the finest hard science fiction, The Oxygen Barons succeeds because its speculative ideas remain grounded in recognizable human motivations.It is easy to see why the novel became a finalist for the Philip K.
