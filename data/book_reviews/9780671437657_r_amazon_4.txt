Unlike the first seven books in this series, The Mind-Murders is not really a novel. Instead, it’s two connected novellas. In the first, Grijpstra, de Gier and the commissaris investigate the apparent disappearance of a man’s wife. In the second, they investigate the mysterious, but wholly natural, death of a businessman. Some characters do overlap the two stories, but the investigations themselves are totally separate (and in fact the book is divided into two distinct sections). The book doesn’t suffer from this, as Grijpstra, de Gier and the commissaris working on two short cases is just as entertaining as the trio working on one long case. One interesting aspect of the book is the sudden introduction of lust into the character of Grijpstra. Detective de Gier is an established womanizer, and his activities have generally provide whatever sex/love we see in the books, but now we have Grijpstra thinking lascivious thoughts and noticing young women on the streets of Amsterdam. It’s an aspect of the character I haven’t seen before this, and I’m wondering what prompted the author to add it to the mix.
