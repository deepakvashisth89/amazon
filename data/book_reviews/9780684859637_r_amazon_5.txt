I am a big fan of the "intelligent thriller," so when a friend loved this and loaned it to me, I looked forward to both reading it and discussing it with him.First, yes, this is a cracker of a story. The story explores unexpected dark alleys and unrelated (or not?) relationships. It's perhaps more graphic than it needs to be - it could stand on the thriller aspects alone without so much grit (and blood) - but it's not worse than others in the genre.The problem for me starts with the "intelligent" part. Susan Shader is meant to be a brilliant psychiatrist, so the author spends pages and pages of pontification to over-explain material that is rarely beyond the basics of pop psychology. I did not read _Eyes_, but I don't know if I could stand more pages of Susan Shader pondering about her brilliance - or rather, as it reads here, of Glass pondering about his own perceived brilliance. Some of this excessive musing comes out in dialogue, and then it becomes almost as painful than the torture described in the book. The dialogue is terribly stilted, which leaves the characters as only cut-out cardboard figures rather than real, relatable people. I do not know what "Joseph Glass" usually writes, but my guess is that has nothing to do with fiction; he could stand to attend a workshop or two on making dialogue work.I call Glass a "he" because this is also painfully clear. This is a man trying to create female characters and it just doesn't work. This goes beyond the old saw that men can't write women and vice versa. It sometimes can be done. But it isn't done here. With the clunky dialogue, it is clear that these are not two real women conversing. The habits of the women in the books are simply not those of women. It becomes downright creepy to have this obviously male author studying women's bodies in ways that women don't study themselves. This is a man with a fetish for small, delicate, smart women, and also a man who clearly wants to showcase how much Freud and Shakespeare he has read and his knowledge of the injuries of professional athletes.If the author had used this story with a male protagonist with more study in how to write believable dialogue, this would be a great read. Right now, I am trying to slog through the second half so I am not insulting to my friend.
