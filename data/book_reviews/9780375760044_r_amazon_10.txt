'Redburn', according to Melville himself, was "...one of the two books I wrote for money". He wasn't proud of it; but contemporary Melville fans do keep making a case for it. In essence it was a book designed to appeal to 19th C. fantasists - people who liked the idea of adventure, but were too sensible to pursue it.It's a story based on Melville's first voyage as a trainee ship-hand from New York to Liverpool - to which he superadds descriptive passages plagiarised from an 18th C. travelogue and a dusting of plot elements that foretoken his short story, 'Billy Budd'.This is not, at all, the Transcendental Melville who prefigured modernist symbolism and postmodern irony in works like Moby Dick, The Confidence Man or (the deeply weird) Pierre. Nor is it quite the wild, 'Boys Own' Adventure writer whose Typee and Omoo remain glittering jewels of their genre. The historical voyeur gets a taste of life on a 19th C. trading ship - but there are many better examples ('Two Years Before the Mast' is an obvious one) and Melville is ALWAYS less than trustworthy with facts.In truth 'Redburn' should be a hack work - except that Melville's obsession with the abuse of innocence (even when he's stealing other writers' words and blatantly lying through his teeth!) is so heartfelt that it seems to verge on the metaphysical.
