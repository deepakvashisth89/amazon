Donald Kingsbury's Psychohistorical Crisis is a very interesting book, and a fun return to the ideas and worlds of Isaac Asimov's Foundation series.  The book is set in the Foundation universe, though it essentially ignores the events of Foundation's Edge, and Foundation and Earth.  Here, Gaia never materializes, and instead the Psychohistorians of Splendid Wisdom (Trantor) have succeeded in creating a second galactic empire built upon the principles of psychohistory.  With the ability to predict the future, the regime is all powerful.  Potential rebellions can be dealt with and marginalized before they even materialize.  Indeed, it appears that the second empire will continue forever.  But it only appears that way.The book follows Eron Osa through two storylines, separated in time.  The first line deals with Eron, a fallen psychohistorian who has his familiar "fam" removed.  The fam is a computer device that works in conjunction with the human brain.  Without his fam, Eron is severely handicapped and can barely function and survive.  Worse, he has no knowledge of his crime.  The other storyline tells Eron's story since childhood, when leaves his backwater planet to travel to Faraway (Terminus), to learn the secrets of the psychohistorians and become one himself.  Along the way Eron spars with his tutor, who has his own galactic agenda, becomes enmeshed in various plots on Splendid Wisdom, and travels to ancient Rith, the birthplace of humanity, to engage in a baffling archaeological project with one of the galaxy's greatest psychohistorians.Psychohistorical Crisis is a very idea driven book.  There's plenty of plot here, but at its heart the work his an extrapolation of how Asimov's second galactic empire  would have functioned, and the potential crises it would have had to endure.  Kingsbury's book is not as accessible as Asimov's- that is to say that the latter had a flowing, easy-to-read style.  Kingsburg, a gifted writer to be sure, nevertheless has produced a dense book that is packed with mathematics, theory, and ideas.  It is both the work's strength and its weakness.  One feels like it easily could have lost a hundred or so pages and been all the better for it.  Still, the story is engaging and characters generally enjoyable.  The ending is very satisfying and makes you rethink Asimov's reasons for creating Foundation's Edge and Foundation and Earth.
