DH Lawrence is considered to be a writer of worth only because, I believe, he took on the topics of women in society, free relationships between men and women, and the effects of modernity on people’s lives. Important topics all, but “Women in Love” is hardly worth trudging through, considering that these topics are not unique to Lawrence’s works.This novel is poorly written, bombastic, and pretentious. I could hardly make my way through it, except that I wanted to finish it to write this review.First, Lawrence’s women seem frequently to be hysterical, frequently showing shrill, “high repellant voices.”After trouble between 2 characters, Gerald and Gudrun, Gudrun knows “The world was finished now, for her. There was only the inner, individual darkness, sensation within the ego,  the obscene religious mystery of ultimate reduction, the mystic frictional activities of diabolic reducing down, disintegrating the vital organic body of life.”I read that Lawrence’s wife used to hit him with crockery, and I am guessing that may have been because he asked her to review this manuscript. (He supposedly beat her as well.)In another scene revealing feminine emotion, a young woman is dancing with Gerald. She was “dying of virgin excitement…fluttering, flushing, bewildered…She shrank convulsively…violently, when he must throw her into the air.” (Yes, Lawrence used “must” there.)For my last example of Lawrence on women, Gerald asks another male character, Birkin, “What do women want…?”Birkin said, “Some satisfaction in basic repulsion…they…creep down some ghastly tunnel of darkness, and…never satisfied till they’ve come to the end.”Apart from the unflattering things that an author wants to say about his characters is how he says these things. Below are some quotations from the book.“And he smiled as to the note of the trumpet.”“Gudrun smiled sardonically with her mouth.”“Ursula was aware of the bright moon leaping and swaying, all distorted, in her eyes. It seemed to shoot out arms of fire like a cuttlefish.”“Ursula went straight to the station, hastening heedlessly on winged feet.”I read a professional critic who said we should excuse Lawrence’s poor writing in that he wrote so hastily (and without care) because he had so much to say. With that level of tolerance, we could all be great writers.In this passage, Ursula and Birkin are driving at night. “But with a sort of second consciousness he steered the car towards a destination. For he had the free intelligence to direct his own ends. His arms and his breast and his head were rounded and living like those of the Greek, he had not the unawakened straight arms of the Egyptian, not the sealed, slumbering head. A lambent intelligence played secondarily above his pure Egyptian concentration in darkness.” I don’t believe that this passage is intentionally funny.Last, I have to mention his racist views. In the following passage Lawrence mixes racism and gibberish quite energetically.Birkin “remembered African fetishes he had seen…a statuette…a woman…her face crushed tiny like a beetle’s…short, ugly legs, with such protuberant buttocks…She knew what he did not know. She had thousands of years of…sensual, purely unspiritual knowledge…Thousands of years since her race died, mystically: that is, since the relation between the senses and the unspoken mind had broken, leaving the experience all in one sort, mysticaly, sensual.”“The white races…having the arctic north...would fulfill a mystery of ice-destruction knowledge, snow-abstract annihilation. The West Africans, controlled by the burning death-abstraction of the Sahara, had been fulfilled in sun-destruction, the putrescent mystery of sun-rays.”Much of Lawrence’s pontifications are incomprehensible and, I believe, probably not worth trying to decipher.Lawrence also has characters say that the Japanese “repel and attract…like eels.” And, he compares Jews to rats. Lawrence’s characters are openly racist, without any sort of admonition from the author.I have not mentioned lesser crimes - that Lawrence is overly wordy, doesn’t seem to have a feel for the flow of words, doesn’t know how to use commas, and has no idea when to use the subjunctive mood. I expected better in a writer who is considered to be important.All in all, a dreadful slog.Read moreRead less
