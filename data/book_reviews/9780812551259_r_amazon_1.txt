This is the second book in a four-book series. It follows “Lagrange Five” (1979) and is a prequel to it. It is a better book than its predecessor, but that merely pushes this up to “awful.” It’s just another bad book in an ocean of bad books. It’s not an awe-inspiringly bad book like the first one.This time out, Rex Bader, Private Eye, is hired by a CIA goon to look into the various attempts on a Professor’s life. There have been two or three. Back in the USSR, meanwhile, we meet a soviet agent. The Soviets can’t compete with the L5 project, so they send in the spy to destroy the first colony in order to turn American public opinion against space.The story ping-pongs back and forth between Rex Bader and Soviet Agent, and honestly the Soviet Agent totally owns this book, despite being a stock character from any number of cold war thrillers. Why? Well, mostly it’s just because Rex Bader is such a total void. In the first book he did nothing approaching detective work, then simply knew the answer because the book was almost out of pages. Here he’s hired as a bodyguard, and he’s utterly incompetent at that. At least the Soviet DOES something. He’s got some forward momentum. Bader’s just there. He’s about the worst protagonist I’ve ever seen. I was pretty much rooting for the Soviet to beat him up. Alas, no: Bader is such a terrible character that he immediately encrapulates everything he touches. From the moment he meets the spy, the Soviet instantly ceases to be interesting, and then the problems are all promptly resolved because again the book has nearly run out of pages.The idea of an incompetent detective is a pretty good one. This is not the author’s purpose, though. Rex is fully intended to be the equal of Sam Space and Philip Marlowe and Mike Hammer. Alas Mr. Reynolds has no clue how to do that. He appears never to have read a detective novel, or even seen a movie.As with the previous book, the author has absolutely no interest in the story he’s telling. It is simply a means to an end. The raison d’etra is to show how great life in a Lagrange habitat would be. That said: his wild-eyed enthusiasm did not make me want to move there. Actually, it made me want to argue with him. “You’re making an overly-glowing portrait. There are serious engineering obstacles, and the repeatedly-stated idea that a man could start his own space colony for roughly the cash it starts to build his own airplane is just the stupidest thing I’ve ever heard.” In short, his preaching has exactly the opposite desired effect.The characters are all ciphers – the sainted scientist, the sexually aggressive 1970s chick who doubles as a girl Friday and gives exposition, the spy, the moles and fifth-column agents in the US. We also meet the Mafia folks from the previous book, for no good reason. It’s implied in the rushed ending that the Soviets had nothing to do with the previous attempts on the Professor’s life, those were all the work of a Christian televangelist who opposed space exploration.In lieu of plot, we get a guided tour of a Stanford Torus style space colony. Once again, as in the first book, we’re told that this is all pretty hardscrabble and in the early days of development and dangerous roughneck work, but we really don’t see anyone actually working.The author displays a shocking lack of understanding of human nature and the way societies function.  For instance: he states that there is no crime anymore since everyone has ATM cards, and those can’t be cheated or stolen or hacked or anything. Oh, and we get another bizarre screed about how everyone should stop speaking English - which is a mongrel language, despite this being an American space station – and learn to speak “Interligua.” This same rant turned up in the previous book.The plot is resolved not owing to any logical progression of dramatic elements, or any sense of completion, but just because his hand got sore, and he decided to stop writing.Bottom line: this is a crappy book. It fails as entertainment, as propaganda, as schlock, it even fails at being a failure, in that it’s nowhere near the giddy superlative awfulness of its predecessor.Read moreRead less
