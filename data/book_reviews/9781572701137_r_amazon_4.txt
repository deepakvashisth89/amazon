This is not a typical review by me. Because this classic Gothic novel has so many thousands of reviews, I’ll just say what I want to say, starting with some gripes, nitpicks, and trivia. If I have any serious commentary, it’s at the end.I enjoyed the book very much, and it deserves the 5-stars I gave it. But if I could grade the print job, I would give it only 1-star! While shopping on Amazon, I was attracted by the beautiful, deep-red cover with the huge, stylized, cream-colored “R” in the title. I purchased the Kindle edition that was copyrighted by Amazon in 2024. Don’t do it.There are numerous typos such as “July an” for “Julyan,” and inexcusable troubles with paragraphs. In dialog, two or more speeches by different characters often appear in the same paragraph. It’s up to the reader to decipher who’s talking. One is unable to bookmark any pages. And some paragraphs are broken up by irregular line lengths. Here’s an example in Chapter 24:‘You damned half-witted liar,’ said Favell, ‘are you going tostand thereand say you never saw me, last year, walk through thosewoods with Mrsde Winter, and go into the cottage? Didn’t we catch you once,peeringat us from the window?’Yuk!The book was originally published in 1938. Naturally, I’ve seen it recommended on many lists over the years. But I’m a novice in the Gothic fiction genre, so when I had seen the author’s name, I had always assumed it was originally written in French and sited somewhere in France. And even though the main male character is named Mr Maximilian de Winter, he is English, the book was written in English, and it is sited primarily in a fictional estate, Manderley, near Kerrith and Lanyon in Cornwall in southwestern England. Some important action does occur at the Hotel Cote d’Azure at Monte Carlo, Monaco. And Rebecca’s boat was named "Je Reviens." So, there is some affinity between the three nations here. It does make me wonder when the du Maurier family immigrated to England, from where.The boat’s name translates as “I’m coming back,” which is one of the major themes in the novel. The memory of Rebecca haunts everyone associated with Manderley, even guests from the surrounding area, but especially Maxim, his second wife (the narrator), Mrs Danvers, and Favell (her cousin and lover). Even her death does not erase her. Maxim bemoans her coming back again and again in different ways and times. In the final chapter (27), Maxim speaks of how Rebecca seems to have finally won the game, tragically, that she had so skillfully played with people throughout her life. And truly, she did inspire the arson that consumed the way of life at Manderley.Several conventional guideposts for the reader are omitted in the novel. The narrator is unnamed until she marries and takes the name, Mrs de Winter; we never know her pre-marital name. Her age is never explicitly mentioned either, but we can infer from one sentence that she is 21.No dates are given, only the seasons and months. The tale occurs during one unnamed year, probably in the mid-1920s, judging from certain descriptions and events. For example, there were numerous staff still in service at Manderley, with no talk of downsizing through layoffs. There are trains, automobiles, electricity, telephones, and indoor plumbing. Also, in Chapter 21, “This was the pain I had known when I was very small and the "maroons" [emphasis added] had sounded in the streets of London, and I had sat, shivering, not understanding, under a little cupboard beneath the stairs.” That sent me to internet dictionaries. Maroons in this context were rockets that made a loud bang and bright flash; they were fired by spotters during World War I to alert and warn people of approaching German bombers. But there are no allusions to events in Germany in the 1930s. All this suggests the beginning of the Inter-War era. Hence, I’m guessing the events in the book probably occurred in the mid-1920s.I’m always on the lookout for anachronisms. So, when I read in Chapter 21 that the temperature was ‘over eighty” and “over ninety,” I went to the internet again to see when England converted from Fahrenheit to Celsius—the 1960s—no anachronism.I can't remember ever reading a novel in which the tale surreptitiously segues from one thing to another in the way this one does. In Chapters One and Two, the narrator reminisces about how Manderley used to be before it was “no more.” But some of it is real and some of it occurs in dreams. We switch back and forth between the present and the past. Sometimes, it is difficult to distinguish what is happening from what is being remembered.The last reference to remembrances that I could find is in Chapter Three, where the narrator writes, “I can see her as though it were but yesterday…” But the next sentence begins, “I could tell by her abrupt manner…” There it is—the subtle segue. I believe the narrative then stays in the past tense through the remaining 24 chapters.On my first reading I was unaware of the switch as it happened. Later, I realized, “We’re not remembering things anymore. We are now being told a story in a straightforward manner, as things happen chronologically. When did that happen?” I could not pinpoint when it occurred until I examined the first three chapters. The author didn’t signal she was switching by a chapter title, new paragraph, or other device—she just did it right there in those two sentences, and it didn’t bother me. I have to believe it was done deliberately. Either I was a sleepy reader or that’s skill. I’m impressed.Read moreRead less
