Dr. Miklós Nyiszli's account of his experience in Auschwitz is interesting, fascinating, harrowing, disturbing, and unsettling.  I am impressed that the account of his story first appeared in serial form in a newspaper early in 1947.  Therefore, the experience was still rather foremost in his mind.  I am amazed that he was able to retain much of a rational mind having lived through the ghastly experience he described.However, I wonder as to the motivation of his writing.  Was it in fact his attempt to chronicle the monstrous violation of the Nazi SS on human-kind so the world would be well informed of the atrocities?  Was it an attempt to rationalize his actions which were by all account monstrous in themselves, regardless of the fact he was under the shadow and sentence of death himself? Or, perhaps it was a way to assuage the guilt he surely must have experienced for having fared so much better in the extermination camp by escaping the death so cruelly with certainty dealt to others who like him were transported to that place in cattle cars? How tormented he must have been to be separated from his family, knowing they surely would be shoved into the raging incinerators of Auschwitz?Regardless of the reason which motivated him to place these events in print, the resulting telling of his story is powerful and very moving.  It is told from the viewpoint of someone who had been there and now shares the story without the desire to follow literary form and style.  His chapters are focused and devoid of much commentary other than the facts.  We get no offering of philosophy or postulation of theories of why and how men can do such awful things to other men and women.  He provides an accurate historical account, as he remembers it, of the horror of that moment.There have subsequently been researchers who have cast doubts on Nyiszli's accuracy of detail.  And, by so doing cast doubt on the veracity of his story.  I believe those who do so are missing the point.  Dr. Nyiszli was not concerned with secondary detail.  He was concerned with exposing the atrocities he experienced as well as his memory had retained it.  In that account, I believe he has been successful.  The horror of his story is so great and the trauma so magnified, that any superfluous detail is lost in its insignificance.I am stunned at the scope of the atrocity.  The fact that over one million people were herded into the gas chambers and then cast into the ovens is beyond comprehension.
