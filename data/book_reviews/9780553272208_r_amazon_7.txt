As a great fan of Brand's Green for Danger and looking forward to reading more of her work, I was very disappointed to read Danielle Hart's and Rebecca Haden's reviews' mention of the anti-semitism in Heads You Lose. I just don't bother reading books by anti-semites any more. No more Christie or Sayers. I even gave up newly-discovered Wentworth. Life's too short, and it spoils the fun. But Green for Danger seemed like the work of a thoughtful, empathetic writer who would not take that kind of mean-spirited shortcut. In fact, in Victims or Villains, his work on anti-semitism in Golden Age mystery fiction, Malcolm Turnbull singles out Brand, along with Ngaio Marsh, Nicholas Blake, and Anthony Berkeley Cox as notable for sensitive portrayals of Jewish Britons caught the upswell of anti-semitism before and during WWII.It turns out I was misled by the reviews: The anti-semitism belongs to a couple of characters, not the author. The one Jewish character, Henry Gold, is portrayed as a sympathetic, complex, loving character, beloved by his wife--who is a reliable moral focus of the book and ridicules anti-semitism by delivering a mock tirade that could have come out of Hornung or early Christie. Brand goes out of her way to show up one of the anti-semites as a bigoted fool, perceived as such by the other characters. The other instance is a complex one and discussing it might be a spoiler.As I said, I misread the reviews, but I did want to clear that up. There is nothing in the book like the casual or vicious authorial anti-semitism that is so prevalent as to be a component of the "coziness" of many Golden Age mysteries.The criticism of "classism" is more apt: "illiterate" pronunciation and class resentment seen from the privileged class's point of view, not just unpleasant and unnecessary but weakening the story. On the other hand, the working-class characters themselves are portrayed as complex and developed, not mere stereotypes, and I feel Brand outgrew this shortcoming by the time she wrote Green for Danger.Read moreRead less
