Redburn: His First Voyage—Being the Sailor-boy Confessions and Reminiscences of the Son-of-a-Gentleman, in the Merchant Service (1849) was Herman Melville’s fourth book. According to critic Hershel Parker, it was not a work viewed favorably by Melville (August 1, 1819 – September 28, 1891) who regarded it “as a step backwards to the earlier picaresque, quasi-autobiographical level of Typee [1846] and Omoo [1847] and at best as an interruption of the progress toward greatness which he had made in his third book, Mardi [1849], a metaphysical and political romance.” Started “under financial compulsion” and written “hurriedly,” Redburn tells the story of a young man whose father is dead and whose family is struggling. Knowing “the necessity of doing something for myself” and having “a naturally roving disposition,” Wellingborough Redburn, long fascinated by the sea and recalling his father’s frequent crossings of the Atlantic on business trips, decides to set out to become a sailor. The youth knows nothing about being a seaman, is totally inexperienced, and unprepared for the life on a merchant ship but gets booked as a cabin boy, the lowest level of seaman at three dollars a month with the world before him.Melville utilizes Redburn as the book’s first-person narrator and the novel can be divided into three sections: the voyage of the Highlander from New York City to Liverpool, England, a six-week lay over in Liverpool, and finally, the ship’s return voyage. There is very little, if any plot to the novel. The first third recounts Redburn’s education at sea. He is poorly equipped with improper clothing and not even the wares a sailor needs from which to eat his meals which the shipping company does not provide. When given duties to fulfill by the First Mate, Redburn stares blankly, not understanding the terms the veteran sailor uses or what to do. Hunger, seasickness, lack of respect from the experienced seamen, and self-doubt plague the lowly cabin boy.Melville writes in detail about a sailor’s life at sea, all of which is far from the romanticized or visually delightful time the naïve Redburn expects. As Redburn gets to know the crew, who he vividly describes to the reader—telling of their individual character and roles on the ship—and begins to prove to be a fast-learner and capable fledgling sailor, Redburn slowly befriends crew members to the degree possible except for the “overbearing” “dregs of a man,” Jackson, “the best seaman on board” and bully “all the men” fear.A slow-moving ship, the Highlander takes thirty days to make the crossing when many packet ships make the trip in fifteen or sixteen. Once in Liverpool, the men are free to leave the ship and Melville begins a lengthy travelogue-like description of the town with Redburn frustrated to find his father’s guide book which he brings with him is hopelessly outdated. Although descriptive and a fascinating depiction of the area including “the lowest and most abandoned neighborhoods frequented by sailors,” the middle portion of Redburn: His First Voyage lags at time and appears to reflect Melville’s rapidity in which he wrote the novel, with anecdotes popping up at random, interrupting the flow of Redburn’s revelations of his roving.Ironically, considering the pace of the Liverpool section of the novel, it also contains more plot than most of the novel and may very well include elements critics and biographers of Melville have frequently pointed to as possible evidence Melville was gay or bisexual (incidences in Moby-Dick have also given rise to such speculation). Redburn meets a skipper, “a bachelor… a very sociable, comfortable old fellow, who had an eye to having everything cozy about him.” They spend the evening together in close proximity “like a couple in a box at an oyster-cellar,” but Redburn turns down the man’s insistent request to spend the night. More importantly, Redburn meets and befriends Harry Bolton, “one of those small, but perfectly formed beings with curling hair, and silken muscles, who seem to have been born in cocoons. His complexion was a mantling brunette, feminine as a girl’s; his feet were small; his hands were white; and his eyes were large, black and womanly; and poetry aside, his voice was the sound of a harp.”  Redburn is “charmed with [Harry’s] appearance, and all eagerness to enjoy the society of this incontrovertible son of a gentleman—a kind of pleasure so long debarred” from the narrator. The two become fast companions, spending several days and excursions together, strolling about “in perfect abandonment,” arms frequently locked together. At one point the two visit a sumptuous private club, Aladdin's Palace, which some critics (i.e. Elizabeth Hardwick) have deemed to be Melville’s portrayal of a male brothel.There is an air of mystery which surrounds Harry as he makes a solo trip to London and upon his return tells Redburn “as you love me” to never speak of or ask about Harry’s excursion. Steadfast friends, Redburn is able to get Harry, who is suddenly bereft of funds, a position about the Highlander, during which their friendship continues but is less conspicuous and Redburn describes his friend as “effeminate.”Using textual content as evidence of a 19th century writer’s sexuality without any firm biographical support is always risky and there is also the fact that male companionship during Melville’s time was more openly free of restraint without fears of threatened masculinity which would become the norm in later years. However, Melville’s loving descriptions of other males is suspect. On the voyage home the Highlander carries more passengers than on the trip to Liverpool. One emigrant, Carlo, “a rich-cheeked, chestnut-haired Italian boy… not above fifteen,” is said to have eyes that shine “with a soft and spiritual radiance, like a moist star in a tropic sky.” His “naked leg was beautiful to behold as any lady’s arm; so soft and rounded… his whole figure was free, fine, and indolent.” Harry is immediately taken by the boy and Redburn refers to the Italian boy as “my Carlo” on more than one occasion. As with many public figures of the day, Melville’s sexuality is likely to always be subject to speculation but remain a mystery.Although the return passage to America is a longer trip “ascribed to the prevalence of westerly winds,” Melville picks up his narrative pace and imbues the last third of Redburn: His First Voyage with more incidents that in the ship’s first voyage including numerous tragedies, one of which is similar to a phenomenon in a scene in Charles Dickens’ Bleak House (1852).Clearly in part semi-autobiographical and calling upon his own experiences at sea, Melville’s Redburn: His First Voyage is an exhaustive and at times (especially in the first third) comical look at life at sea in the early 1800s devoid, except for an occasional philosophical observation and some minor drama, of what Melville would later produce in his magnum opus, Moby-Dick; or, The Whale (1851) or the tightly-constructed, dramatic, posthumous Billy Budd, Sailor (1924).
