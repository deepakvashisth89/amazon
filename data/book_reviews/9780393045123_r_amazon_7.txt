The book discussion group met at The LGBT Center in NYC in March 2016 to read poetry by May Sarton for National Poetry Month. We had a small but interested group. Everyone agreed that it was a treat to read the poems out loud and to hear the poems read by others before we discussed them a littleThe general consensus was that Sarton was able to create an interesting turn of phrase and had plenty of high ideas. She's especially interested in ritual and hours of everyday precision. She's concerned with loneliness and solitude, and uses a lot of Christian imagery (which may be alienating for non-Christians) but her major imagery comes from her gardens and nature.Her poetry can be a bit formal, which is not always a good thing. While she's concerned with nature, the progress of the seasons, and the process of writing, she's limited to clever language. Nothing ever seems especially memorable or striking.There's a slightly distant, academic feeling to a lot of her poetry. Even the "feeling" poems seem very tightly controlled, which inhibit the expression of her feelings, too. She just never managed to strike us as a major poet.We chose Sarton because she was a lesbian, but there was no obvious (or subtle?) lesbian content in the poems. I'm glad one member of the group read from a few essays to give some feeling for her lesbian life.Suggested poems:"Myself to Me": This is a sad little break-up poem. Very small but rather affecting. Again, her concerns with loneliness and home ritual guide her feelings."Burial": This collection of poems contained a couple of poems about the death of her father. This was moving, but slight."Humpty Dumpty": A poem about loss. Many of these poems are about nature but many are sad, about devastating events."At Lindos": This is in the next-to-last "Invocations and Mythologies" section, which relies on classical mythology rather than Christian imagery, for the central conceits of her poems. It helps if you know about the site of Lindos but even if you don't, there's enough in the poem to give you an idea of what it's about. (Did I mention that a lot of these poems are rather academic? But somehow Sarton's academic references are not as rich as the mythology found in Auden, for example.) There are some lovely images and the ideas of the two groups in the poems provide some interesting thoughts."A Village Tale": Wow. Read it out loud, and then let it sink in and read it OUT LOUD again. Note the ambiguous Christian imagery. Wow, again. Maybe it seems especially shocking because it has some real blood in it. Or maybe it's all just a Christian metaphor.I'm anxious to read a novel by Sarton, maybe to read a few essays, to try to add some sense of her life in these poems.Read moreRead less
