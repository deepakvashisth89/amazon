Rarely has a book been more aptly titled than Henry James’s The Portrait of a Lady. Like one of the old masters, James uses auxiliary characters, aristocratic settings and the thinnest of a narrative to paint a detailed portrait of a young American heiress in fin-de-siecle Europe.The interest in the book is not just about character, it’s about one character. There are of course her cruel husband Osmond and the unfortunate Jamesian stereotype of a shrill liberated woman. And there are also the stock Victorian characters: a young man with disintegrating health and a crone scheming on behalf of her unwed daughter. But all are put to use in eliciting the tones and figure of the protagonist, Isabel.The setting also changes from an English manor to Rome to an English manor again. James was a keen observer of Europe as an expat and describes the feel of Rome and England with aplomb.But, like any good portraitist, the setting is merely there for the sake of framing of Isabel. His early readers may have been more attracted to his contrast of European and American mores (and his contemporary readers as well).James, however, saw himself as more of a fine artist—writing under the gaze of eternity. And, in this reader’s opinion, he was largely correct.If you haven’t read Henry James, and you are willing to read a character study that is over six hundred pages long, then you are in for a stimulating read that is beyond delightful.
