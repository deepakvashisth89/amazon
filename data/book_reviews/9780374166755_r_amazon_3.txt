A fan of Shirley Hazzard's novels (particularly of her sweet slender work THE BAY OF NOON), I started her memoir of knowing Graham Greene on the beautiful Neapolitan island of Capri more than ten years ago, soon after it came out, and found its somewhat smug tone, its snobbiness, and its long overwritten sentences hard to take. It also bothered me that while she and her husband, the great Flaubert scholar Francis Steegmuller, clearly adored Graham Greene, the book displayed almost no genuine feeling for the ideas of his books or for their milieux. Trying it again some twelve years later before a trip to Capri, I found that all these faults were still very much in place, but that the book is much more charming than I had seen before, and that as a kind of travel book about Capri and the many writers and politicians who have lived there over the centuries; indeed, it succeeds much better on those terms than as a memoir of Greene (who just does not emerge as a living breathing man from these pages, but rather as a cherished enigma). It reads very much like the kind of memoir the literate wealthy used to write much more than half a century ago, when literary quotations and foreign phrases are constantly trotted but to prove one's intellectual bona fides; apparently life on Capri with Steegmuller and Greene was actually much like this because they were constantly discussing Tacitus and Ben Jonson. (It does all get a bit insufferable when Greene asks why no one reads Statius anymore and Hazzard points out, "We do"). But the stories about Capri and its odd inhabitants and visitors--Tiberius, Norman Douglas, Henry James, Gogol, Massine, etc.--are often quite captivating, and though the sentences may be unwieldy, they are at least the way Hazzard likes to express herself naturally.Read moreRead less
