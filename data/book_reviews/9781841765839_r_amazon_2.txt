Turnbull starts with modern popular views of the Mongol warrior, and gives some historical narrative and context (pp. 4-8); The chronology covers the years 1167-1301 (pp. 8-9). Next comes their familiarity with the horse and bow and mobilization (pp. 9-11, 17-19, 28), organization (pp. 11-12, plus 28), discipline (pp. 12, 49), clothing, gear, and mounts (pp. 13-17, the 8 color plates with commentary, and many of the photos scattered throughout the text), attitudes and beliefs (pp. 24-25), and unit training (pp. 25-26). The role logistics played in Mongol logistics get a lot of coverage (most of pp. 18-24, 25-42, plate C’s commentary, plus a lot of photos of tents and animals scattered throughout the text), particularly the under-discussed Bactrian camel. A lot of the logistics discussion is really a cultural study, since families provided so much of the Mongol warrior’s support. Then, some discussion of tactics and operations (pp. 41-42, 45-59), including examples of how they adapted to various conditions e.g. sieges, amphibious warfare, jungles, elephants, and the use of atrocity as psyops (pp. 42-44). Turnbull touches on command and signal (p. 42, plus plate D’s commentary), pre-campaign staff-work (p. 31-32), intelligence (pp. 32, 41), and medical care (p. 30, plate G’s commentary). Includes an extensive bibliography (but mostly secondary sources, pp. 60-61), a brief glossary (p. 60), and an index (p. 64).Positive: 1) Turnbull discusses the written sources and archaeology that provide his evidence, and acknowledges that much of what we know from the 13th Century Mongols comes from outsiders; 2) Turnbull highlights how Mongol intelligence gathering emphasized gathering information on logistics (i.e., mostly grazing land), which in turn effected campaign planning (p. 32). He convincingly argues (p. 29) that the recall of the Mongol’s invasion of Europe in 1242, normally attributed merely to Ogodei, was actually due to a lack of grazing land (Hungary, later famous for its grassland, was mostly woods and swamp at the time. Generally, the coverage of supply and maintenance issues here seem to be thorough.Quibble: 1) Mongol operations at this time were quite far flung. A map would have helped; 2) Turnbull claims that “fresh training was hardly necessary” (p. 10) for Mongol warriors on mobilization. That might have been true at the individual level, but to turn individuals into units would have required group training, probably done during annual winter hunts (which Turnbull mentions on pp. 25-26).Despite a few quibbles, 5 stars.
