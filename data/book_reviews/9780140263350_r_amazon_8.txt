Notwithstanding the quality of writing in this book, its main issues are that Keller claims to provide a historical account of the horrors of the comfort women system but 'Comfort Women' is full of historical inaccuracies and sometimes flat-out fabrication. Although it can be appreciated that this book raises some awareness on the subject, it is not the perfect introduction to the history of comfort women it has been described as. The custom of drying cow dung for fuel presented in the book is not Korean. The use of the term P to describe comfort women comes from the Chinese term pi (slang for genitalia) rather than the Korean word 'poji'. The book shows a clear lack of understanding for Korean customs and many of the events or buildings (Soon Hyo's mother attends Ewha college who was not created until 1952, years after the conflict ends). Some of the descriptions of the 'recreation camps' belong solely in the realm of creative literary licence and not as an accurate description of what the camps were truly like; women within the camps did not wear stenciled numbered uniforms as the author describes. Keller claims to have based her book on thorough historical research but that is not the case; the book's success as historically representative exploits a general lack of knowledge on the subject. Fiction books who would misrepresent the nightmares of the concentration camps would not be heralded as good literature. Rather, this book appears to use the horrors of the comfort women system as a convenient backdrop for a tired rehash of the mother-daughter tear-jerker story.
