Pursuing Dublin conversations in his brilliantly observed memoir "I Am of Ireland," Richard Brown, an Irish American in the midst of his own identity crisis, charts the ebb and flow of commitment and disdain. At a cocktail party, a banker who calls for blood. At an O'Connell Street bar, a barrister puts warns after rubbing elbows with some rebel on the run "whoever you meet in this fair city, you'll hear a lot of rubbish."  Among the 'Mad Men' professionals of Manhattan, Brown (even bares a striking resemblance to Don Draper) first became aware of being Irish when his mother pinned a green ribbon on his sweater for St.Patrick's Day... "Up until then I thought I was Jewish because I was born in New York."Talking to skeptical middle-class Dubliners and living with the embattled Catholic poor in Ulster, Brown captures the breathing paradox of the Irish conflict. "I am of Ireland" is a series of vivid, sometimes terribly funny scenes from his travels. On a train to Belfast on the morning a car bomb explodes killing shoppers on Donegal Street, a girl reading "The Godfather" tells him she'd be afraid to live in America. Brown tries to identify with the IRA as a logical extension of his search for his own Irish identity but violence repels him. Yet you'd be hard pressed to find a more graphic account of the massacre by British paratroopers in Derry on Bloody Sunday before U2 ever sang about it.
