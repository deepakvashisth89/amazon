It seems there were originally two stories (both involving unhappy marriages) that Eliot decided to combine in this VERY long novel. Published in 1871, it depicts small town life around 1830. In short, it is a historical novel, describing a world now almost 200 years old. I didn't feel that the story itself aged all that well. At the center are two couples: An idealistic young woman who marries an older, dried up clergyman/scholar, and a young, ambitious physician who marries a sweet but spoiled young one. Around them a pretty standard provincial scenery is drawn: Jovial gentry, clergymen of various shades of orthodoxy, happy and not so happy families, wayward and upright children, small town politics, lots of gossip etc. And then, of course, there are the usual excitements: Inheritances are speculated on, dubious outsiders appear, long simmering scandals are finally revealed. In short, the general outline of the plot seems pretty predictable.--But then there is, of course, the author and what she makes of the story she tells. No doubt, Eliot writes beautiful English prose and probes all characters, motives and outcomes with immense learning and deep psychological insight. She reminds us at almost every step that there are always more ways to view any issue than the first or even second impression might convey. In the process she makes sure that none of the characters remains one dimensional. Not satisfied, she frequently interlaces these analyses with more general asides on the human condition. In short, she is not hesitant to let her authorial voice be heard. Initially her style and her uncommonly astute reflections impressed me with their rich novelty and surprising insights. But after a few hundred pages, I must confess that they had an increasingly deadening effect on me. It all began to sound very didactic, even preachy. --  Middlemarch is very long, but I love long novels.
