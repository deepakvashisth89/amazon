This is not the type of book to be reading when Chicago is not in the news enough for being nicknamed Chi-raq. This isn’t the type of book to read while getting rejected for unspecified reasons. Saul Bellow is a spinner of white-collar crime and an old guy trying to sound like he’s still aware of what sex is, trying to be appealing to young people. This was a very slow un-edited read about pretentious characters who think the word “poet” has any financial or intellectual gravity.The timeline is difficult. “No, the most action Emil ever saw was during the 1968 riots in front of the Hilton.” That’s on page 87, but I thought I was reading a trench-coated, derby hat era. On page 90 Charlie Citrine identifies himself as being born in 1918. Bellow is generation-less in a bad way.On page 242, just about the only thing worth marking: “From this Humboldt went on to insist that in the unconscious, in the irrational core of things money was a vital substance like the blood or fluids that bathed the brain tissues. Since he was always so earnest about the higher significance of money … had cost me 50 shares of IBM stock … a deductible loss … I owed publishers about seventy thousand dollars in advances for books I was too paralyzed to write.” He could sell his Oriental rugs. This is the book in not much of a nutshell. I’m glad the standards of literature have changed from self-importance to something less intellectual, but that doesn’t make a character who has had privilege very intellectual in the first place.I could not help but mark page 447 for topical reasons. “Last time I took a ride on the Jackson Park El, two cats were slicing a guy’s pants pocket with razor blades while he pretended to be asleep.
