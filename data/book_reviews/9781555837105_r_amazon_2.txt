It's a cliche to use the term "master of the written word," isn't it?Oh, well.Once again Hansen proves he is a master of the written word.(There. Actually, that entire sentence is a cliche. Oh well. It's true nonetheless.)Usually I cannot stand an overuse of metaphors, similes...analogies...you know, stuff. JUST SAY WHAT YOU MEAN, PEOPLE. Whenever I read about the crashing waves of love along the shores of...of...I don't know, I can't even do it. But when I come across such writing, I feel like I can see the writer hunched over their computer, crying as they write cause it's just so awesome.My point is that when Hansen writes the "surf lipped pale sand beyond a stagger of red dune fences", I barely notice. Out of context it seems just as overwrought as "love's crashing shores," but while reading it's effortless. It adds to the story, to individual characterizations, instead of jarring the reader. Whether you're 10 or you're 40 and you want to be a writer when you're all growed up -- study this man.Despite the beautiful writing I just didn't find "Troublemaker" as compelling as the first two books in the Dave Brandstetter series. In "Fadeout" & "Death Claims" Dave was struggling with different romantic relationships; I loved how his musing over his personal life effected his view of whatever mystery he was solving and how whatever mystery he was solving effected his view of his personal life. It's not really the romantic subplot I missed (cause, as previously stated, NOT A ROMANCE), but the effect that subplot had on the story and Dave overall. Dave seemed less personally effected by this mystery and as a result I felt less emotionally invested.But I did love the mystery. Books about dead people are my faves. Sometimes I wonder if there's something seriously wrong with me. And by "sometimes" I mean "all the time."3.5 starsRead moreRead less
