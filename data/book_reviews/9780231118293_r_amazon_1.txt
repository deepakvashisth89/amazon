I confess/admit that part of the reason why this kindlebook that is In Their Own Voices; Transracial Adoptees Tell Their Stories by Rita Simon and Rhonda Boorda appealed to me is because I too was a transracial adoptee and I was curious about the stories of other transracial adoptees.  My late adoptive mother was an American born woman of  Caucasian descent with partial French Ancestry and my late adoptive father was also an American born man of Mexican and Aztec Indian Descent. The author interviews transracial adoptees who live in multiple places and work in multiple careers such as Vermont, a woman who spent her early childhood years with her adoptive family in Washington DC, another adoptee who is a social worker by profession, another adoptee who is a nurse by profession, an adoptee who resides in Hollywood California and works as a production assistant in animation, an adoptee who lives in Sacramento California, an adoptee who is a professional negotiator by trade and lives on the East Coast, and more. I came to be brought into my late adoptive parents lives when I was around 7 years old some time after my late maternal biological grandmother gave me up for adoption. I am actually glad to have been adopted and I’m fortunate that by the 2012/2013 timeframe my late maternal biological grandmother confirmed it to me herself on why she willingly gave me up for adoption. Naturally, my late grandmother’s admission fostered increased gratitude within me towards my adoptive parents. I am fortunate to have lived in diverse environments within the US (in the Midwest, Japan, California, Florida, Virginia and Maryland) during my lifetime and be married to an amazing husband of 14 years that has helped me feel increased acceptance of my own racial backgrounds. However, my need to resolve the totality of my full racial background has little to do with being brought up in a transracial adoptive family. Rather, both my late maternal biological grandmother was of Menominee Indian Descent and my late biological mother was of Menominee Indian and Puerto Rican descent and I found out by the year 2012/2013 that both women were listed before in the Menominee Indian tribal rolls. However, to this day I only know for sure from my late biological mother’s side what races are part of my heritage because of the type of lifestyle she lived when she was alive(she was a prostitute). I tried and got some success in seeing and speaking to my rumored biological father who confirmed some of the accounts I heard from others how my late biological mother lived. To be fair for some time he made himself available to talk on the cell phone when I wanted to talk. However, by the time I worked up the courage to ask my rumored biological father to take a paternity test with me  to make sure if he was even the father (not for money but more to confirm my racial identities)contact with him became scarce.  Yes, this happened when my late adoptive father was alive and I am regret-free about keeping this attempt a secret from him. However, this obviously helped remind me how fortunate I am that my late adoptive father took me in as his daughter even though we were of different racial backgrounds. My loving husband of 14 years felt compassion for my attempts to piece together the totality of my racial heritages and he provided emotional support and encouragement after I shared with him what happened.  My point to mention this has little to do with garnering sympathy, it is more or less to admit  why I speak only sometimes about my heritage until the day I can work up the energy to try some dna test and confirm the totality of my background.
