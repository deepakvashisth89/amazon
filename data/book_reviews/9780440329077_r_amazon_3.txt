William Manchester was a noted newspaper reporter, biographer and historian.  Yet, in the late 1970s he's disturbed by dreams of his younger self as a Marine sergeant in WWII.  What disturbs Manchester is not so much reliving combat experiences, as a sense that the young sergeant is disappointed in the soft, chubby sedate man of letters he has become.  It's as if the young sergeant is asking what have you done for the past 30 years to make this war time experience worthwhile?So, Manchester sets out to visit the various battlefields of the Pacific war - Guadalcanal, New Guinea, Tarawa, the Philippines, Peleliu, Saipan, Iwo Jima and finally Okinawa where his combat experience started and ended.  He's disturbed by what he sees.  These places liberated by American blood are now the stomping grounds of Japanese businessmen.  Did we free these locales from Imperial Japan only to cede them over to commercial Japan?  While some sites have war memorials and are little changed, others have been turned into resort islands, housing developments or commercial centers.What was it all for?  It is mostly through his encounters with grateful locals and by experiences intended to exorcise some of his war time demons that Manchester is able to reconcile his post war life with his time in the Corps. And by the end of his stay at Okinawa, the young sergeant comes no more.The one thing that keeps me from rating this book at five stars is ironically Manchester's gift of story telling.  He is able to craft such vivid encounters at places like Guadalcanal, Tarawa and other battlefields, that you would think he was there.  He was not.  His combat experiences came at Okinawa and were in themselves sufficiently horrific. The reader must either know this in advance or finds out only if one carefully reads the notes at the end of the book.
