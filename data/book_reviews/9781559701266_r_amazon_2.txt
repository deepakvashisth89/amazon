When the Grand Senior Warden at the time, MW Jeff Williamson, loaned me his copy of “The Temple and the Lodge” by Michael Baigent and Richard Leigh, it wasn't far into the first few chapters before I decided it was worth owning my own copy. The work itself was a continuation of the author’s research in writing previous works, “The Holy Blood and the Holy Grail” and “The Messianic Legacy”, which I have not read.The subject matter supposes to examine the possible historical connection between the Knights Templar during the Crusades and modern Freemasonry. It does not answer this question, but in the process of examination does much more -- it explores the influences of and on both of them. The author’s are non-committal as to when, where, and if  the two meet, but it is not for apathy or lack of effort.The style is broad, taking turns at being a mystery novel, a court case, a historical journal, and very often, a genealogy of linage and ideology bordering in severity with the Book of Numbers. At other times, especially early on, the narratives strayed near the very edge of every plausibility, based on a litany of “could haves” and “what ifs”. But it was never for too long, or pushed too vehemently; caveats were given about the credibility of sources, and honesty was kept with representing opposing interpretations.The story begins with their research in Scotland and the events thereof, backtracks to the Templars, and ends with the American Revolution, but the heaviest weight of it is spent addressing the myriad influences of and onto early Freemasonry, many of which I was previously unfamiliar, such as Jacobinism, the Rosicrucians and the Royal Society. I have been often told that little documentation exists for Speculative Freemasonry before 1717. After reading this book with its well-cited sources, holding that opinion myself is no longer possible.A compelling case is presented that the British commanders, nearly all Freemasons, forfeited the War with Britain that resulted more by default in the Independence of the Colonies. But like elsewhere in the book, this was done without attempts to guide the reader too rigidly along a set narrative. On the contrary, the authors categorically reject the notion that Freemasonry is consistently the same institution over time. It is simply not factual or even possible to be monolithic in nature, agenda, or even to what degree there is an agenda at all other than general humanistic ideals and values. But it does allow for honest instances of intrigue without the sort of conspiratorial, blanket condemnations found elsewhere.The drawing out and untangling of interwoven, and even seemingly contradictory personal connections reveals a depth of research that seems to plumb, primarily or secondarily, a thousand biographies. It seems the intent was to be exhaustive in both sources and speculation as to their implications. That does not mean, however, the authors were all-knowing on every aspect of every subject touched. Four examples come to mind. The first two are understandable if no one among the authors or editors knew French or Greek.A label of a skull was found in Paris with the inscription “Caput LVIIIm”, being made up of the word for “head” in Latin along with the Roman numerals for 58. The footnote to this passage (on page 79) suggests this relates to the Inquisition’s reference to a legend of the Eleven Thousand Virgins because the letter “m” looks like the astrological sign for Virgo, rather than the preponderance of myth being that the Templars worshipped the head of John the Baptist. What is unrealized or omitted is the simple fact that the letter “m” is used in French after numbers the same way we use the letters “th” after “4”, “5”, “6”, et alia, namely to designate ordinal numbers.With regards to the term “arche-tekton”, the authors manage to get it right and wrong at the same time. The etymological exploration of the “architect” concept (on page 138) misses the subtle nuances of the word “arche” (ἀρχή) in favor of the textbook translation. The Greek word (ἀρχή) can be used as “first” or “foremost”, but with the word for builder or craftsman, “tekton” (τέκτων), is traditionally given to mean “master builder” or “master craftsman”. And these are in the masonic lexicon, sure enough. I will not fault the authors or editors with that.But a closer understanding of the word “arche” reveals much, since it carries with it the concept of “source”. Perhaps the closest philosophical parallel of the term would be that of Aristotle’s “First Cause”. Which brings us back to the book, noting that Plato’s great arche-tekton was that which created the world by use of geometry, conceptually more than similar to the Masonic GAOTU. In other words, it was not understood by the authors that the common translation is misleading, whereas the philosophical concept turns out to be linguistically correct.Two further errors gave me pause, but of such a nature as to question credibility in other assertions in areas I do not have familiarity. The first was to assert (on page 180) that the idiom “the 3rd degree” was of Masonic derivation, rather than in reference to Inquisitorial torture. The second was a quick statement made (on page 261), unqualified except by circumstantial assumption and parroting popular myth, namely that the reverse of the seal on the dollar bill was of direct Masonic influence. We now know that particular design was conceived by non-masons, and it is no secret that such symbolism was and is still used by many fraternities concurrent with or imitating Freemasonry.And here I must note that one such society was the Bavarian Illuminati, the mention of which is strangely absent from a book that otherwise endeavors to glean every legend for a kernel of truth. Then again, the end of the book seemed a bit rushed where it could have continued, eager for a simple closure to a complex work.My final judgment of the book’s historical merit mirrors the book’s conclusions on the subject matter itself -- decidedly undecided, or more accurately expressed in modern terms, “it’s complicated.” And I don’t know by what specific reasoning, if any, RW Williamson (now the Deputy Grand Master) thought I ought to read it -- here was a seasoned mason recommending a book on Freemasonry written by non-masons -- but I must concur it was at least worth the value for spent time and attention.Read moreRead less
