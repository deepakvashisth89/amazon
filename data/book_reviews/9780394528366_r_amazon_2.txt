Power doesn't just corrupt, it also reveals.Caro wants to show the nature of legislative power, including legislative genius.This book covers the 2 terms that Johnson spent in Senate, until he became VP to Kennedy. As a Texan, he was a member of the conservative Southern faction of the Democratic Party, which was powerful, but a minority. Nevertheless, he made it to the leadership in and of the Senate. And then he turned into a champion of civil rights legislation. The forces for good in him were revealed. But we must wait for that forever. First he had to tame the Senate.How did LBJ achieve the unlikely, the leadership in the Senate, without seniority, without social standing, without education? Just talent for political games, energy, will power, restlessness, ability to read people and make use of them, ability to handle the levers of political influence. Not to forget: an enormous ability to get things done that nobody did before, like becoming the first president from the South.Caro has the habit of sidetracking. He will insert short biographies of other people who play an important role, be it as mentor or opponent. We get Senator Richard Russell from Georgia, who was instrumental in LBJ's career. 'The closest thing to an honest politician.' A formidable opponent to civil rights legislation. A gentleman racist. A superb manipulator of legislature. Great teacher for LBJ, who used all his skills as professional son to weasel his way into this daddy's good graces.Caro is very clear which side he takes. He roots for civil rights and New Deal. He describes in minute detail how the filibuster was used to block legislative initiatives that the old men from the South did not want.As a contrast to obnoxious Dick Russell, Caro introduces a man called Leland Olds, a Newdealer of the best, most competent kind, head of the Federal Power Commission. Olds opposed deregulation of the natural gas business. LBJ needed to prove his own usefulness to Texas oil money by getting rid of Olds. (All criminal gangs demand that kind of thing sometimes from their members, something like a loyalty test.)LBJ passed that test. His next step led him into national celebrity status as head of a Korea war 'preparedness' commission, thanks to daddy Russell.  He was found out as a fraud, but too late to stop his meteoric rise. He became Democrat whip after just 2 years in the Senate, at age 42. Nobody else had applied for the job. It was a 'nothing job' that he turned into something.By age 44 he was Senate minority leader, by 46 majority leader. His first stroke of leadership genius was the decision to let the Democrats support the foreign policy of the new Republican president against his own party's hard liners.Caro's main theme is the double talk skills that LBJ needed to cultivate in order to become a serious contender for the presidency: a southerner with his feet in the conservative Democrat camp, who makes himself acceptable to the liberal faction. Conflicts and contradictions were not reconciled, but ignored.I find this third volume the most interesting, so far. Caro's revulsion by his hero is most ambiguous here. There is more admiration mixed in with the disgust.The picture of the US during the time is not pretty. Racism and corruption raise the question what in the world the romantics of American exceptionalism were or are proud of.Caro debunks a myth that LBJ had managed to cultivate, namely that he had not been a bigot and a racist. There are enough sources to prove otherwise, and yet he became the liberator. Caro sees the groundwork for that shift in LBJ's young life experience: picking cotton as a child, doing road work as a boy, teaching poor Mexican kids as a young teacher. LBJ had a gift of empathy, hidden inside his vulgar crudeness.My respect for the American political system would have been severely damaged by this book, had I been a novice. The political parties were essentially dysfunctional. The level of Congress corruption was breathtaking. (Has it changed?) I come away from this monstrous book of over 1000 pages with a kind of awe at the monstrosity of that institution, the Senate. I even take away an impression that Nixon might have been a cleaner man than Johnson.Caro's organization of his huge material is excellent, as is his narrative pace: artfully slow.
