This is a fascinating compilation of anthropological observations of mythical and religious practices, taboos, and ceremonies from around the world. They are gathered together to try to answer a single, central question: how did the curious myth of the Golden Bough develop in a certain way in a certain place. To go about answering this Frazer looks the world over to see if he can find a progression of superstition and religious development (he is a Modernist who assumes evolution and bottom up progress) that will explain this one isolated case. Modern American readers beware! This is written in an old style of literature. We have all been trianed in, and raised on, the five paragraph essay where we start with a main idea, list the main arguments in order, then a conclusion or resolution and summary of the ideas presented. Nothing belongs in teh essay that does not support the main idea. This is a linear writing style but it is not the only one. The Golden Bough follows an older European style marked by digression. Frazer intentionally develops his argument very slowly with each new idea leading, not to the next idea in his causal chain, but to a digression which takes you in a direction tangent to the main point. Don't worry, he will always take you back. You must read it as you would take a meandering walk with a friend in pathless park. The walk is the main thing, the destination is almost secondary, and yet, all walks will come to an end. I think. I got tired of this about halfway through (it's a ~600 pg book) and quit.
