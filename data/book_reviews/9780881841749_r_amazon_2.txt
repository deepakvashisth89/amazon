This is an eloquent account of a long and tragic period in our history as it relates to one family and their struggle to save the only thing left to them after the Great Depression, and the manner in which the various members of the family view that struggle and the indifferent fate that moves them ever forward.The writing is lyrical (“The stars were windy and brilliant, and one enormous planet burned down along the west.  It was dark with no moon, but the white patches of everlasting gleamed out disk-like in the grass” page 122), but time was difficult to keep track of:  how much time passed with the family struggling to pay off the mortgage, and also survive, during this period as well ashow much time was passing without rain.  The narrator said things like, “I wished to God it would rain,” but left the reader unaware as to whether it had been a couple of months or several years.  Some chapters began with time markers such as, “in May,” or “by June,” but they were interspersed by other chapters with no references to time so I was unsure whether it was the same year or another year.  At one point, a new chapter pointed out that 10 years had passed.  This lack of a firm timeframe seemed to enhance the feeling of confusion and ambiguity.  Possibly these were techniques used by the author to make the reader feel just as apprehensive and disquieted as the characters, but I would have liked to have some way to mark the passage of time.Despite these small distractions, this is an engaging account of a devastating period in the history of our country as experienced by the fictional Haldmarne family who find themselves seeking love and acceptance as they struggle for a toehold in their quest for financial stability while attempting to navigate the maze of injustice in the form of banks, mortgage companies, tax authorities, landlords, commodity exchanges and, ultimately, the brutal, capricious weather.While addressing such major themes as poverty, race, class, sexism, mental illness and the precariousness of farming, itself, Johnson handles each subject so deftly and with such grace, and the themes are woven so deeply within the storyline that it feels nothing but genuine.  And interspersed between the bleak facts of life on a 1930’s farm on The Great Plains, the coming of age of the three daughters, and the Haldmarne’s struggles against poverty, are middle daughter, Marget’s, breathtaking observations on the natural world surrounding the family farm.This major environmental event of the 1930’s which is, most likely, no longer even discussed in schools, followed upon the heels of the Great Depression, killing thousands of people and destroying and displacing many thousands more due to the eight-year drought and the subsequent phenomenon which came to be known as the Dust Bowl.As Churchill said in 1948, “Those that fail to learn from history, are doomed to repeat it."Despite all we learned about the causes of this devastating weather phenomenon, there is no guarantee this natural disaster will not be revisited upon us.  In fact, due to our arrogance, foolishness and the exorbitant waste of our precious and limited supply of water, we could bring this same disaster, or worse, again upon ourselves or our children.Indeed, Johnson’s book, ‘The Inland Island’ in 1969, put her in the category of early environmentalists along with others such as Rachel Carson.We owe a debt of gratitude to Ms. Johnson for not only bringing this haunting tale to future generations in the hope this tragedy will not be forgotten, but also for her ability to remind us of the value of human dignity by allowing us into the lives and hearts of the Haldmarne family, with such a beautiful and evocative story.This book should be required reading in high school.Read moreRead less
