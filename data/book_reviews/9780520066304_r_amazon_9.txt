Peters points out that there was never a brutal, fear-generating horror of an organisation called The Inquision as it is popularly represented and understood. The term `Inquisition' is derived from the latin and has the same root as `inquire'. Inquisitors were those who led inquiries.The first part of the book covers the Roman legal system which I initially thought superfluous, but I then realised how useful it was as background information as the thorough, documented system was adopted by the Catholic church and used for its inquiries.Peters then points out the facts of these inquiries, including the infamous Spanish Inquisition. Even though monarch-controlled and relatively brutal the sum of death-sentences over 250 years came to an average of about one per month. I feel obliged to point out that in Spain now there are about 40 murders per month.By looking at the facts, Peters allows us to see and understand what heppened in historical perspective - that's roughly the first third of the book. He then goes on to show how the myth of The Inquision evolved and how the myth turned into popular (and extremely stubborn) history.
