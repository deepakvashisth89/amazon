This book is a must have for any student of the Custer debacle.  First it was the initial published  record of the seminal and ground breaking (pun intended) archaeological work carried out by authors Fox and Scott on the battlefield. In fact their work founded an entirely new field of archaeology known now as Battlefield Archaeology, or more the more politically correct name Conflict Archaeology.That it was their first published work on the battlefield is important for two reasons. First it includes a very large pocketed map the size of old highway maps that shows and this is important, all the numbered markers on the field. In addition it shows all the location of all the battlefield relics discovered during the archaeological excavations carried out that year. More digs were conducted over the next ten years or so but no new map of this scale has ever been printed.  In fact their subsequent works, refer to the grave marker numbers and do not provide the necessary reference map to allow the reader to place the marker on the battlefield.I had devoured all their subsequent works, Archaeology, History and...; Archaeological Perspectives on...; They Died with Custer, and Uncovering History. NONE of them include such a map but all refer to the marker numbers as if every reader possessed the map, Figure 9 of the first book. It is out of print now, so good luck finding one. ANY serious student of the battle needs this map.A few caveats though. Subsequent works have pointed out that the initial map references might have included errors which put some of the markers on the map in the wrong places. Greg Michno's Mystery of E Troop includes a detailed discussion of this and figures and maps illustrating the errors.Another reason you should have this map is it amply demonstrates, not intentionally mind you, but inadvertently, how the school of thought has changed about what happened during the battle.In this book the Author still subscribes to some of the old ideas about what took place based on the placement of the markers on the field. That is something that today was dicey at best. Accurate maker placement 13 years after the battle was a pipe dream. However, archaeological digs since 1984 have show that the marker placements aside from the nearly 40 odd spurious markers was actually fairly accurate. US Park Service capriciousness in moving markers willy-nilly across the landscape for any number of reasons without recording the initial positions has added to the confusion.The author shows that at the time he subscribed to some of the ideas put forth by the likes of Kuhlman and others as to the trooper dispositions during the battle that have since been discredited by a more thorough examination of the archaeological data that the author himself collected and analysed.Today the thought on what took place have been thoroughly revamped in light of what the archaeology data has added to the historiography of the battle.In my opinion, anything written on the battle before 1984 was a combination of: fantasy, wishful thinking, cultural, individual and racial bias, faulty interpretation, faulty and leading interview techniques and the homogenizing effects of "group memory."For nearly 100 years what we thought we knew about the battle was based on "the losers" that is the white man's version of events colored by their desire to make a debacle into some heroic last stand against insurmountable odds. The only eyewitness accounts, that is those of the Native American participants was dismissed as lies. This was due to the white man's inability to understand that the Native American saw things through a different set of cultural lenses. They knew nothing of Cavalry tactics. They didn't measure time the same and nearly everything they remembered was from their singular personal perspective. They didn't fight as units but as individuals and saw events through their unique set of cultural filters.So if you are a serious student of the battle, if you've read the other works of Scott, Fox and O'Connor you need this book and its Figure 9 to get the most out of the other works.
