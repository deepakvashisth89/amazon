It is a rare thing for a work of fiction to be remembered in history, and even taught in history classes. This book is one of those rarities, right up there with "Uncle Tom's Cabin" by Harriet Beecher Stowe. The Jungle is a novel about a Lithuanian family who moves to America to be part of the American dream, only to have their dreams, and even their family stomped into oblivion by the big corporations. As a result of this book being published, President Theodore Roosevelt opened a federal investigation into crimes perpetuated by the meatpacking industry, and eventually led to creation of Pure Food and Drug Act which requires inspection of meat and other food products.Jurgis Rudnos is the protagonist of this story, who finds work in the Chicago Stockyards where cattle and hogs are butchered. The author, Upton Sinclair, was trying to make a point about the conditions of workers....of being taken advantage of, paid low wages, of being sold into slavery....just every human rights violation that one can think of is probably in this book. But along the way, Sinclair also tells the reader about the disgusting conditions of the meat packinghouses and the depths to which the business owners will stoop to earn a dollar. Hint: it's pretty horrifying.There are 2 versions of this book: the "sanitized" version which was published first and which made headlines around the world for its expose of human rights violations and the nasty packinghouses. Sinclair had this version published because (as is widely suspected), the packinghouses actually suppressed the publication of the original manuscript. The original manuscript was found in the 1980's (long after Sinclair died) and so far as I kn0w, is only available via Kindle, but this is the version worth reading, in my opinion.The sanitized version of the story is actually told out of order; the original manuscript puts events back in the way that the author intended, and includes some details that the author was forced to scrap for publication. The details that the author scrapped actually made the story better and helped explain certain events in the story that were left out of the 1st and subsequent printings. I won't say I enjoyed this book, because of it's nature, but let's just say that it was very eye-opening. I am giving this book 4 stars because the last 50 pages or so turn rather preachy and while I understand the intent, it got to be a little too much.
