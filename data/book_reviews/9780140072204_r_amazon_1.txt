B. Kliban’s The Biggest Tongue in Tunisia is one of those books that appears, at first glance, to be a collection of cartoons. This is a deception. It is actually a field manual for surviving reality once reality has removed its trousers, climbed onto the roof, and begun shouting at the moon.Kliban’s genius was not merely that he was funny. Plenty of people are funny, in the same way plenty of people own knives. Kliban knew where to insert the blade. His cartoons have that rare and sacred quality of seeming completely idiotic for one glorious second, and then suddenly revealing themselves as tiny philosophical depth charges. You laugh first. Then, if you are unlucky enough to possess a reflective mind, you realise the joke has followed you home.This book contains the sort of humour that civilisation pretends not to need while secretly depending on it to keep from collapsing into spreadsheets, municipal signage, and tasteful beige despair. It is absurd, deadpan, slightly feral, and unmistakably intelligent. Kliban looks at ordinary life and seems to say: “Yes, yes, very impressive. But what if the entire enterprise is being run by cats, lunatics, and men with improbable tongues?”And one must concede the point.The drawings are deceptively simple, but the timing is lethal. Every caption feels as though it has wandered in from another dimension wearing a hat. The result is not merely a joke book, but a peculiar little scripture of the comic grotesque: part vaudeville, part Zen koan, part ransom note from the collective unconscious.If you require humour to be neat, topical, moral, uplifting, or accompanied by a small laminated certificate explaining what it means, this may not be your book. Good. Not every door should open for the villagers.For the rest of us, The Biggest Tongue in Tunisia remains one of the great comic relics of the twentieth century: ridiculous, savage, innocent, depraved, and somehow wiser than many books with footnotes and tenure.Highly recommended.
