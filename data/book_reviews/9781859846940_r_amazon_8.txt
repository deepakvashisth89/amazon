Ghada Karmi's book, "In Search of Fatima," is a poignant, heart-wrenching account of her exodus as a child from Palestine in 1948 with her family, and of then going to England. As a teenager and young woman trying to integrate into a foreign culture, she struggles with her identity, shuns her Arabness and tries to "become English" for many years, only to be hit by shock, after the 1967 Six-Day War, at British support for Israel and her own painful awakening of identity. She then embarks on the reverse route: becomes a political activist, works with the PLO, lives in Syria and Lebanon and practices medicine in the refugee camps, only to give all that up, in turn, when she hits the disillusion of realizing how corrupt and useless the leadership is.She returns to England, feeling very lost and bewildered, and each of her two siblings is similarly lost. Her sister, Siham, leaves to the Arab world soon after graduation, marries an Arab, has four children then gets divorced years later and returns to England. She is back to struggle with issues of identity, having taken the opposite tack to Ghada and never fit into English culture to begin with. This is an interesting difference as Siham arrived in England as an older teenager, while Ghada was a child: it fits the notion that children tend to integrate much faster, at least in their younger years, before the cycle of adulthood begins and they start to question their true identity. Ghada's brother marries a Danish woman and lives in Denmark but on the outskirts of Danish society, unable to integrate. Ghada herself, who marries an Englishman in her youth partly out of defiance and the need for acceptance and integration into what she perceives is "her" English culture, finds her marriage soon disintegrating, and has no further luck with her personal life even when she painfully attempts to return to her Arab roots and goes to live in Syria and Lebanon. She finds that Arab men predominantly view her as "foreign" and "available," she is hit on in a lowly, physical way by married men and she has no marriage prospects. She feels despairingly that, for the most part, Arab men tend to view women in two spheres: those they marry and those they have affairs with, and that the two never intersect.Back in England, sad and lonely, she tries to reconcile her feelings towards the two cultures. The book gives a very powerful interplay between the coldness, frigidity and apathy of English culture in its relation to Palestinians at the time, her sense of complete non-belonging in it, and the narrow-mindedness, corruption and cruelty of Arab culture that hits her, on a political and personal level, causing her indescribable anguish and despair. She is left feeling utterly homeless.Finally, she comes back to Palestine, 50 years later, to close the circle, and it is heartbreaking... She visits the whole country, now Israel, then goes to her old house in Jerusalem, to find Jewish inhabitants in it. Her description of the sense of smallness and defeat she feels is overpowering. She ends by saying that the exodus of internally displaced Palestinians, those who left one of part of Palestine but ended up in another, is only material and temporary: she feels they will regroup and make a comeback. But for her, she says, it's a different kind of exile, one from which there is no return.This throws open the whole question of identity and its irrevocable link to physical location. Why else would so many millions of people who are forced into exile around the world still feel that their new countries are not their home? Even more astounding, this feeling sometimes exists among people who migrated as very small children, with barely a memory of the country they left behind, such as Ghada in this case.One feels, reading through the book, a profound sadness and horror at a loss so marked, so wretched, and so condoned by the world. We re-live the entire extent of the Nakba, the Catastrophe, experiencing continuous waves of shock at how a crime of such magnitude could have been perpetrated: making the inhabitants of an entire country homeless within a few weeks, never to return, to be pushed into refugee camps, poverty and exile, scattered to the ends of the earth under the watch of the world who couldn't care less, while others simply move into their houses, renovate, change the street names and pretend it all belongs to them - because of a claim that they lived there three thousand years ago.Read moreRead less
