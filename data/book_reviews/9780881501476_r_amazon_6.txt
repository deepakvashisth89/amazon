Much of what I said in the first paragraph of my review of Dover 2 applies here too. Dover and MacGregor do their double act as usual. Characterisation is otherwise deft, if incidental.Dover and MacGregor may be the same as before, and as they ever will be, but the plot is different. This is one of the joys of the early Dovers - seeing Dover apply his methods, such as they are, to varying situations, and witnessing the diverse outcomesHere there are poison pen letters, the crime that Dover is sent to solve, but also baby trafficking, and an apparent suicide that MacGregor thinks, to Dover’s annoyance, may be murder.The reader is expected to spot the villain and possibly even work out how they did it, even as Dover is attempting to fix, with the minimum of effort and according to a timetable convenient for him, the poison pen letters on the first person who meets his pre-conceived ideas. The interest is in anticipating when the scales will fall from his eyes. Then it seems that Dover may be right after all ...  Perhaps there will be a final twist? If so how will it play out and can Dover use it to his advantage?Readers also get to amuse themselves with a serviceable locked room mystery. There are however a couple of misfires.It is difficult to make a joke of backyard abortions and baby trafficking, and Porter wisely does not try, but rather than hinting at tragedy, which might be equally misplaced in a book like this, Porter settles for callousness. This mirrors Porter’s failure with the issue of sectarianism broached in Dover 2.
