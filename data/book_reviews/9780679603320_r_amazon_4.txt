POSITIVE: Dumas' writes with his dramatic flair, intricate plots, and rich character development, often blending adventure with historical elements. His use of dialogue and suspense, in addition to complex storylines with multiple twists, keeps readers engaged, making The Three Musketeers timeless classic. Well-developed characters are the main part of honor, justice, revenge, loyalty, and friendship storyline mix in The Three Musketeers.NEUTRAL: Many previous translations. Some not much improved from the Anonymous original translation, some smoother, better and modernized. Four of them deserve some attention:- 1846 William Barrow -- considerable toning down of the original (also known as a "bowdlerization" effect) for the UK and the US. While The Three Musketeers was not originally intended for children and teens, for some time the Barrow's version has been published as a children/young teen book;- 2006 Richard Pevear -- excellent and the translation brings a fresh and more meaningful experience;- 2014 Will Hobson -- very similar to Pevear. Some sentences even more clear; and- 2018 Lawrence Ellsworth (a pen name for Lawrence Schick) -- my impression is very good one, even excellent. The work now appears alive, even more theatrical and humorous (in addition to the already excellent and more realistic translations that Pevear and Hobson achieved).NEUTRAL (PHYSICAL): Nice hardcover edition from Pegasus Books. Attractive dust jacket. Consistent imprint on a good quality and somewhat creamy paper. 2018. About 790 pages (not counting the intro).COMPARE (a random paragraph; ch. 33):Anonymous (the original translation):"Then only D'Artagnan remembered the languishing glances of Kitty, her constantly meeting him in the antechamber, the corridor, or on the stairs, those touches of the hand every time she met him, and her deep sighs; but absorbed by his desire to please the great lady, he had disdained the soubrette. He whose game is the eagle takes no heed of the sparrow."William Barrow:"Then did D'Artagnan first recall the languishing glances, the smiles, and stifled sighs of Kitty, whenever he had chanced to meet her; whilst, in his absorbing wish to please the titled lady, he had neglected the abigail. He who chases the eagle takes no heed of the sparrow."Richard Pevear:"Only then did D'Artagnan remember the languishing glances Kitty gave him, the meetings in the antechamber, on the stairs, in the corridor, those brushings of the hand each time she met him, and those stifled sighs. But, absorbed by the desire to please the grand lady, he had scorned the soubrette: he who hunts the eagle bothers not with the sparrow."Will Hobson:"Only then did D'Artagnan remember Kitty's languishing glances, the meetings in the antechamber, on the stairs, in the corridor, the way she brushed hands whenever they met, her stifled sighs. Absorbed by his desire to please the great lady, he has disdained the soubrette. He who hunts eagle pays no mind to the sparrow."Lawrence Ellsworth (Schick):"Only then did D'Artagnan remember Kitty's languishing looks, their encounters in the antechamber, the corridor, or on the stair, the way she brushed his hand every time they met, and her deep sighs. Absorbed in his desire to please the grande dame, he'd ignored the soubrette. One who hunts the eagle pays no attention to the sparrow."Note the minor language "toning down" in Barrow's translation: the word "soubrette" (a saucy, coquettish, intriguing maid-servant) is replaced with the (milder) word "abigail" (a lady's waiting-maid) in Barrow's translation. Somewhat awkward use of possessive "OF" in "the languishing glances of Kitty" or "stifled sighs of Kitty." where most English speakers today would deploy "'S", as in "Kitty's languishing glances" (Hobson) and "Kitty's languishing looks" (Elssworth).NEGATIVE: None.OVERALL: 5/5.
