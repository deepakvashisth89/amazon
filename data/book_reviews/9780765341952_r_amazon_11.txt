As someone deeply engaged with studying the possibilities of ‘real’ psychohistory (my PhD was on this topic), I have long awaited a copy of Donald Kingsbury’s Psychohistorical Crisis. Asimov’s Foundation trilogy is of course an SF construct – hence a ‘mythical’ psychohistory with no connection to the ‘actual’ form of psychoanalytic psychohistory that originates with Freud’s first psychoanalytic network. As a retired professor of mathematics from McGill University, Kingsbury is the only author of a ‘Foundation’ novel to grapple with some of the issues raised by statistical (i.e. stochastic) long-range models of human affairs implied by the ‘Seldon Plan’. The novel may well therefore be challenging for some. These issues include a) those of predictability in the social sciences, b) determinism vs. freedom of action and c) the function of ‘secrecy’ in institutions seeking to effect long-term historical change – and ‘freezing’ these changes once they have been successfully created. Since this novel appears to have been written outside the ‘canon’ approved by the Asimov estate, there are significant name changes. Those well acquainted with the original Trilogy will, however, have no trouble linking the background and events of this novel with the ‘metahistory’ of the canonical series. Particularly poignant for me is the chapter entitled The Martyrs’ Cache – which harks back to the tragic events (for the Second Foundation) at the conclusion of the third novel of the Trilogy. Suffice it to say here that the ‘fifty martyrs’ of the Second Foundation get their revenge. I might have given Psychohistorical Crisis 5 stars, but I found the characterisation rather weak – will humans living 80,000 years from now really be so much the same as we are at present? What about the ‘posthuman’ acceleration of the present? The possibility of an AI ‘singularity’? Or will collapse intervene to terminate our current scientific-technological culture? If it does, will not acute resource depletion seriously constrain any possibility of a technological renaissance in the future?  I also have trouble dealing with the ‘all-human’ Galaxy, though I’m happy to treat the ‘dynamics’ of the novel as a ‘world model’. Any serious consideration of human psychology is lacking in ‘Asimovian’ conceptions of psychohistory – and it is the absence of any ‘macropsychological’ parameters that seriously constrain the ecological validity of ‘real-world’ stochastically-based models. Nevertheless, the novel has real relevance for our own time. I particularly liked the concept of the ‘fam’ – I guess that within 15-20 years at most the I-Phone will have shrunk to the size of a microchip and will be capable of being embedded within the neocortex.
