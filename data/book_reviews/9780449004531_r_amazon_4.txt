First write a short story (all the time making sure it will be published  in The New Yorker or Playboy); if it works, write another one, using the  same character or characters; when you have written three or four of these,  start thinking about grouping them together in book-form (remember: publish  and republish your work as much as possible); then write a couple of  cementing 'chapters' and offer it to the public as a novel. This is how  John Updike has written (among other things) Bech is Back - his second book  about a Jewish-American literary novelist prone to writer's block. The  advantages of using the compositional method described above are clear:  instead of that heavily programmatic, overdetermined, obsolete thing we  call 'plot', one gets instead a sequence of snapshots, or a gallery of  pictures. We get a book that has obviously evolved organically over time,  pushing out roots into only the most fertile soil. We loose old-fashioned  unity of design, but we do not miss it. This is writing like a cubist: the  by turns judicious and whimsical assembling of fragments of truth, rather  than the facile pursuit of an impossible illusion of coherent 'wholeness'.
