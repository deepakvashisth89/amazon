“Peace is the perspective found in patterns” (29) is the quote from the Refuge by Terry Tempest Williams. This quote can imply many things. This paper delves into interpreting this quote by doing a close reading on chapter BALD EAGLES with a chapter that proceeds it - CANADA GEESE - and the one that follows it - RED-SHAFTED FLICKER. The paper will especially try to answer the following questions focusing on human psychology, the logic of thought processes, and the human relationship with nature: What links these three chapters? Why do they appear in the sequence that they do? What makes them resonate, i.e., how does each pick up and echo the others? Is there progress or development of the themes and ideas across the three of them?In the chapter CANADA GEESE, Mother of Terry, Diane said “I used to think the life of a monk was a selfish life. I don’t believe that anymore” (192). The moment in her life that changes her perspective on the monk’s life is very likely to be the diagnosis of her cancer. She might have thought the monk’s life was selfish because they primarily focused their life on their enlightenment with religion. But after the diagnosis of cancer, she seems to realize that the way to peace, the perspective found in patterns, is focusing on herself first like the monk is doing it through their religion. The monk’s lifestyle also aligns with wild geese: “Wild geese are my favorite birds. They seem to know where they are from and where they are going” (192). Their migration ends with silence. Monk’s life and goose migration align with Thomas Merton’s quote: “Silence is the strength of our interior life.… If we fill our lives with silence, then we will live in hope” (193). However, human life is seldom in silence. People try to go against natural phenomena - like surgery on Diane’s cancer instead of just accepting it.The next chapter BALD EAGLES, talks about the Mormon religion which might link to the monk and their peaceful, silent life from the previous chapter. As with many other religions, the Mormon religion has its own format of practice. Terry admits that some people argue the origin of the Mormon as controversial but during the time of turmoil - the diagnosis of cancer to her mom - Terry “felt the presence of angels” through praying which was recommended by President Monson (197). As another example of peace, the perspective found in patterns, bald eagles appear to resemble and remind of the monks from the previous chapter: “Twelve bald eagles stood on the ice of Great Salt Lake, looking like white-hooded monks” (201~202). They have patterned therefore peaceful life: “Eagles on ice, cleaning up carp: beak to flesh. Flesh to bone. They whittle carrion down to a sculpture, exhibited in a bleak and lonely landscape” (202). Such an organized life contrasts with Terry and her mother’s life in the chapter - like many other people, they sometimes do unplanned and extravagant shopping for celebrations.Besides the contrast between nature and humans, this chapter is particular because there’s a scene that shows the typical human psychology when people face extremely tough facts like the cancer of a family member in this chapter - not telling the truth to the patient. According to Dr. Smith’s examination of Diane’s cancer, “She doesn’t have much time.” but “After the examination, he came back out and said things looked better than he thought, that the tumor he had felt in Hune was gone, and that the others felt smaller” (199). Even though Diane wasn’t told that her cancer wasn’t getting better, she still knew that she still had cancer. Not surprisingly, her attitude is the same as her daughter - denial of cancer. “I think I have denied having cancer for years. It’s a survival skill. You put it out of your mind and you get on with your life. I mean, you have momentary flashes of what is real in periods of crisis and you face them, but then your mind seems to leap over the illness. You forget you were ever sick, much less that you are living with a life-threatening disease” (200). Such denials resonate with the quote from Devra Davis’s book When Smoke Ran Like Water “Denial is one of the strongest of human emotions” (39). It seems that it’s not a coincidence that Professor Handwerk made a class schedule to read the Refuge after When Smoke Ran Like Water. It resonates with the importance of juxtaposition matter not only in chapters within one book but also in the sequence of reading many books.However, as the story - and her cancer - develops next chapter RED SHAFTED FLICKER starts with the end of denial and acceptance: “I see them digging my mother’s grave” (204). It seems Diane is still denying her cancer as she asks “You mean I have cancer?” Dr. Smith, but it might be unintentional at this moment because she is even too weak to get to the door and has bad cognitive senses as the following quotes show: “I heard the words but was incapable of internalizing them. I only saw John’s face and could feel what was being said through him” (205). Christmas Eve toast seems to be not only a holiday tradition but also the family’s acceptance of Diane’s near death. People shared the story of their love for Diane. It is a well-organized holiday with a silver cup of cranberry juice, praying, sitting in a circle, sharing toast prepared by Brooke, and exchanging gifts. “Everything appeared normal - knives and forks ticking on china, platters of food being passed, more water being poured - until one by one” (209).
