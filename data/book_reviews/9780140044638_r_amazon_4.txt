"The Cockatoos" is a collection of six novellas and short stories. (Despite the blurb on the back cover, I do not think any of them really qualify as "short novels"). The theme running throughout the volume is that of ageing; most of the stories deal with an elderly or middle-aged married couple, often trapped in a dull or loveless marriage but not one which is so unhappy as to provoke them to take any active steps towards divorce. The main exception is "The Full Belly" set in Athens under the Nazi occupation, although even here the principal characters, a pair of sisters, are elderly.In my view the best of the stories is "The Night the Prowler". (Yes, that is correct- it's not "The Night of the Prowler" but as in an unfinished sentence: "The night the prowler.......). After supposedly being raped by an intruder into her family home, a young woman named Felicity goes through a disturbing change in her personality, breaks off her engagement to her fiancé and becomes a "prowler" herself, breaking into homes in her middle class suburban neighbourhood and carrying out acts of vandalism. A sudden twist in the story, however, suggests that Felicity may not have been raped at all, implying other, even more disturbing, motives for her strange behaviour. In this story Patrick White displays considerable skill in conjuring up an unsettling atmosphere, hinting at the strange undercurrents which may underlie a seemingly mundane, everyday existence."A Full Belly" is an effective evocation of a family's struggle to survive hardship and starvation in wartime, but unfortunately, none of the other stories are of the same quality. The novella can be a difficult literary form to get right, and can end up either as an overlong, flabby short story or as an underdeveloped novel, and in this collection White seems to fall into both traps at different times. The opening entry "A Woman's Hand" (also the longest in the collection) struck me as falling into the second category. It tells of a retired couple, Harold and Evelyn Fazackerley, who discover that Clem Dowson, an old acquaintance and schoolfriend of Harold, is living near them, and of their disastrous if well-intentioned attempt to engineer a romance between Clem and a female friend, Nesta. White introduces numerous themes the decline without fully developing them - Harold and Clem's schooldays, Harold and Evelyn's friendship with an upper-class English couple, Dudley and Win Burd, Harold's time in Egypt working for Dudley's business, the mysterious decline in the Burds' fortunes, Nesta's past as the companion of an Italian princess. The reader is left wanting to know more about all these topics, and it seemed to me that the story would have worked better as a full-length novel.If "A Woman's Hand" is an undercooked novel, then the title story "The Cockatoos" and "Sicilian Vespers" are more like overcooked short stories. The term "Sicilian Vespers" originally referred to a bloody uprising against French rule, later dramatised as a Verdi opera, but White's story is about nothing more dramatic than yet another retired Australian couple, on holiday in Sicily, where they make the acquaintance of a younger American couple. "The Cockatoos" deals with a small outback town and the events which transpire when a flock of cockatoos descends upon it; again an old couple with a strained relationship are at its centre. I found both these stories overlong and meandering without any real point; they seemed to peter out rather than coming to any definite conclusion. (The same could be said of some of the other stories in the volume). Even when there is some dramatic development, White does not always make the most of it. For example, in "The Cockatoos" a man is shot dead, an incident which in many stories by other writers would be the main focus of the narrative, but here it is mentioned almost as if in passing, with no real analysis of the killer's motives or the consequences of the deed.Patrick White clearly has many admirers, not least the Nobel Prize committee which voted him Australia's only winner of the Literature Prize, so I was disappointed not to be more taken with this collection.
