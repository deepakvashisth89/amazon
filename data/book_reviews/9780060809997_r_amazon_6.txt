Review: The Detective Wore Silk Drawers by Peter Loveseywith lychee black tea and sweet sesame mochi, because Victorian bare-knuckle boxing requires perfume, sugar, and something chewy enough to survive Sergeant Cribb’s questionsBy random tea lottery, I settled down with lychee black tea and a plate of sweet sesame mochi. This was a pleasingly odd combination. The tea had a floral fruitiness over a proper black-tea base — elegant at first, then firmer than expected. The mochi was soft, sweet, elastic, and faintly treacherous if bitten too confidently.Perfect for a mystery set in the world of Victorian prizefighting.Peter Lovesey’s The Detective Wore Silk Drawers was published in 1971 and is the second novel in his Sergeant Cribb series, following Wobble to Death and preceding Abracadaver. It sends Sergeant Daniel Cribb of the Metropolitan Police into the brutal, sweaty, illegal world of nineteenth-century bare-knuckle boxing.That title alone deserves applause. The Detective Wore Silk Drawers sounds like music-hall innuendo, a police report, and a Victorian laundry scandal all at once. Lovesey knew how to choose a title that winks without collapsing into farce.The story begins with a corpse in the Thames — headless, battered, and apparently connected to the underground fight game. Cribb decides that the way into the mystery is through infiltration, sending someone undercover among the fighters to identify the dead man and expose the killer.As a retired library assistant, I admire a mystery that knows its setting is not wallpaper. The bare-knuckle boxing world is not just scenery. It is motive, method, class system, business model, and moral atmosphere. Men fight for money while gentlemen watch. Violence becomes entertainment. Respectability pretends not to recognize its own reflection in the blood bucket.That is very Victorian.The lychee black tea suited the book because Lovesey is doing something similar: refinement over bruising. His prose has wit and polish, but the subject is fists, betting, bodies, injuries, and exploitation. A genteel surface sits above a hard business. The mochi, meanwhile, became a metaphor for the investigation: soft-looking, but resistant; sweet, but requiring work.Cribb himself is one of the great pleasures. He is no flamboyant genius scattering deductions like confetti. He is patient, dry, shrewd, practical, and very good at letting other people underestimate him. His assistant, Constable Thackeray, gives the series its useful friction: loyal, puzzled, occasionally exasperated, and forced into situations that would make any sensible officer reconsider clerical work.Lovesey’s gift is historical reconstruction with comic intelligence. He does not treat the Victorians as wax figures under glass. They sweat, gamble, cheat, posture, moralize, lie, and go to bed with sore ribs. He has a fine eye for the absurdity of English respectability — especially when it hovers near illegal sport with a betting slip hidden in its glove.My private catalog card would read:Lovesey, Peter — The Detective Wore Silk Drawers — Sergeant Cribb — Victorian Mystery — Bare-Knuckle Boxing — Thames Corpse — Class, Violence, and Disguise.And the cross-reference:See also: Never Trust a Gentleman Who Enjoys Watching Poor Men Bleed.Verdict: The Detective Wore Silk Drawers is a witty, lively, well-built Victorian mystery that follows Wobble to Death with another wonderfully eccentric historical sporting world. Lovesey turns bare-knuckle boxing into a sharp detective arena, full of bruised bodies, hidden motives, class hypocrisy, and period detail worn lightly. Cribb remains a delight: dry, observant, and much more dangerous than he looks.Best read with: lychee black tea and sweet sesame mochi. The tea gives perfume over strength; the mochi gives chew and surprise.
