Gogol's account of the Zaporozhian Cossacks and the Polish-Cossack war of the 17th century is a story of a man and his people who know nothing except war.Taras Bulba has a family, including two sons who are studying at a university. However, their visit to home is taken as an opportunity for father and sons to do some bonding by embarking on a journey to the Sech, which serves as a gathering place for Cossacks who, seemingly, are itching for a fight. So much so that a flimsy excuse for war is seized upon as cause to take up arms against the Poles, even though the Cossacks have a peace treaty with them.The religious backdrop for this sequence of events is drawn rather starkly by Gogol, as the Cossacks take umbrage at the slightest rumor of offense against their orthodox faith by the Roman catholic Poles and serves as sufficient justification to roam the countryside and kill as many innocent Poles as possible.Gogol paints this picture in nationalistic terms, with the Cossacks portrayed as defenders of the Russian nation (he curiously downplays their Ukrainian heritage). These people have warfare so deeply entrenched in their souls, that they seem barely human. The pauses they take from killing Poles are used to engage in violent anti-semitism.The traitorous actions of Bulba's son Andriy in the pursuit of the woman he loves is the only deviation from the barbarous primary story line. However, this is thinly sketched out. Andriy betrays his people and his family for a woman, who we didn't even know he was aware of until far into the story. But this serves as the emotional fulcrum of the story and you know this betrayal will end with a tragic faceoff between father and son.As Robert Kaplan puts it in the forward, these are a people who are not receptive to enlightenment.
