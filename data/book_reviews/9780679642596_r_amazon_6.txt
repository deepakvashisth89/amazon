John Irving is a popular and contemporary writer, probably best known for his novel entitled "The World According to Garp" that won the National Book Award in 1980 and that was made into a feature film.  Mr. Irving has written several other novels (his thirteenth "In One Person" 2012) perhaps the most notable "The Cider House Rules" that was also made into a film and for which Irving earned an Oscar for "Best Adapted Screenplay" in 2000.  Perhaps little less known by most, John Irving was a competitive wrestler for over twenty years until the age of thirty four; he continued in the sport as a coach until the age of forty seven and in 1992 was inducted into the wrestling Hall of Fame in Stillwater, Oklahoma.John Irving's writing is variously complex and can contain lengthy sentences and liberal use of the semicolon; the latter the subject in his novel of some ridicule when the New York Times uses it for its feature headlines.  The sentence structure, however, adds to the easy flow of dialog and comfortable nature of the discourse which should be attractive to a wide audience.  In the course of writing his novel, Irving decided on capitalizing the dialog attributable to Owen Meany; ostensibly to suggest the irritability of his voice.  One may or may not appreciate this device.  I found that in today's electronic media world the capitalization of words invokes "LOUD SHOUTING" rather than "strangled emphatic falsetto" blared through ones nose. The reader will have to judge for himself as to the effectiveness of the suggestion.The story in main is about one Paul Owen Meany, Jr. who is born with two unfavorable physical defects - a very small bodily frame and a high raspy and nasal voice.  As the story develops, the reader is brought to the realization that Owen Meany seems to possess some otherworldly traits specifically attributable to some religious notions.  As the story opens, we learn that Owen is befriended by one of his classmates one John Wheelwright and that during a typical little league baseball game, Owen inexplicably hits a ball, which he has never done before, that strikes John's mother in the temple killing her.  From this point onward, Owen and John become unlikely best friends and John becomes witness to the extraordinary events surrounding the life of Paul Owen Meany, Jr.John Irving writes his story with a main stream theme.  He then plies to this stream meandering tributaries whose relevance at times is strained and often enough contains endless boring content.  To his credit, however, Irving brings the reader back to the main just before exasperation sets in.  This technique, however, adds hundreds of pages to the composition, most of which if left out would make no difference to the tale and would probably improve the reading experience (I suggest the screenplay does exactly that).  For instance, the reader is subjected to the reading of several psalms (hymns) - as though we were to sing along perhaps or at least hum a little?  Although Irving makes no claim as a "religious" person, he chooses a particularly dopey religious premise for his protagonist in the story - an immaculate conception and the second coming of the prophet, wow!  Perhaps it was all just tongue-in-cheek, you be the judge.  In a further departure, Irving takes us through a litany of political ruminations that depict the state of affairs surrounding the Vietnam War - did I really need to rehash the Iran-Contra Affair? What did that have to do with anything?When you get to the characters in the novel you are certainly surrounded with a peculiar assortment, not the least of which is John Wheelwright.  Even Irving tries to describe him as a "non-practicing homosexual" meaning "he doesn't always know what he is".  I think it's more than that though.  Here is a guy that is probably retarded - though that seems to contradict his scholastic accomplishments but how many people do you know that would allow themselves to be mutilated? Wheelwright shows little emotion, not even after his mother is killed or about losing a digit or about anything, really! Then there are Owen's parents - can anyone really believe that they believe Owen was immaculately conceived? Then there is John Wheelwrights father. No one would conceive of "The Lady in Red" ever having any reason to have a "fling" with such a loser - a man who is frightened into his faith by a seamstress dummy!All in all, I thought the novel was just okay, not one of Irving's better works.  There were a lot of things, some of which I mentioned, that detracted from the work.  I was disappointed because I liked Irving's novel "Cider House Rules "and it seemed different in its structure compared to "A Prayer for Owen Meany".  Perhaps it was the religious premise and the political musing that spoiled the work - you will have to be the judge.Of my ratings "Forgettable, Pleasurable-Not Memorable" and `Memorable" I would rate this work Pleasurable but not memorable and recommend you consider it for your reading list.Read moreRead less
