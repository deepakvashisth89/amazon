There are plenty of people who will blast off at me when I state that I'm doing this review when I'm only about 2/3 through the book. Trust me; that's enough to know what I think of it. Whether I'll finish it, or just skip to the end, is the question. Another question is whether I downgrade my review to one star (I doubt I'll be upgrading it to three) if I DO make it to the end. I doubt it; I probably won't want to waste any more time on it by that point.Anyway.....It started out well enough, with a suspenseful tone set as we find out that someone referred to only as "the watcher" is keeping our protagonist in his sights, with obviously evil intent. But within two chapters, we have cartoonish characters, people who don't act like real people speaking dialogue that just doesn't -sound- like it's spoken by real people, and cliches that I can't believe anyone is using anymore. It's as if the book was written by a 1970s preteen who is trying to write the way they think grownups speak.Let's talk about the cliches first. It got to the point that I went back over the pages I'd already read and marked them (the cliches, that is) with a pencil just to see whether there really were as many as I'd thought. Here is a sampling of some of the snappy, terse dialogue (and please note that the copyright date on the book is 2001):- One cop asks another "How's it going, Frank?" The reply: "Another day, another dollar."- Regarding a death: "It must have happened in the wee hours." (Do cops really talk like that? Anywhere?)- One cop talking to himself: "'Put a fork in it, Gil,' he told himself. 'It's done.'"  (An expression that always irritated me in any case, because I never really knew what the heck it was supposed to mean.)- The protagonist's sister is described as "a computer geek for some high-tech company." I guess it's too much to hope that someone in a corny novel can be a technology professional without automatically being dubbed a "geek."- There are constant references to a curse that is supposedly responsible for all sorts of tragedies and disasters, and every time our lady thinks about the curse, she gets "shivers down her spine." Eek.- "Something wrong?" Anna asked a colleague at one point. The response was "No way." Why "no way?" Why is that - still - the stock negative answer? Why not just "No, nothing" or "Not at all?" I can only guess it's because "no way" was how teenagers answered questions negatively in the 1980s. Annoying.- Someone asks "Who's that guy by the ticket windows?" The reply: "The managing director of the museum. Ivar Gregor. He's having a cow."Oh WOW, that's really original, isn't it. It gets better: the first person then replies, "A Russian cow?" and they both burst out laughing as if it's the funniest thing they've ever heard. To me, it doesn't even make sense (maybe it would have if the other person had retorted, "Yeah, a Mos-cow!" but our author's imagination didn't manage to come up with that).- "The exhibit cases stood out like sore thumbs." Haven't heard THAT one lately.- The sentence "The plot thickened"  - another one that deserves no points for originality - was used twice in three paragraphs, once in a character's head and once by another character out loud. Maybe we were supposed to believe that he read the other guy's mind. I think I actually said out loud "You already said that!" when reading it the second time.- The cop is concerned that his daughter is running around with a boy he doesn't like. Oh BROTHER do we get a lot on that topic. But still on the topic of cliches, at one point he thinks "Dollars to doughnuts she'd gone to the beach with that boy." Another expression that the author insisted on dragging out of retirement.- The cop expressing suspicion: "I smell a rat." Two sentences later he starts a sentence with "Hoo-boy."- The other cop, a minute later "beamed" and said "We have a criminal mastermind here." Beamed? Doesn't that mean smiled brightly and with pleasure? Isn't that a bit much under the circumstances? Not to mention the corny "criminal mastermind?" (If there's another death in the book, I'll bet you "dollars to doughnuts" that one of the cops will trot out "homicidal maniac.") If this guy doesn't turn out to be some kind of impostor, I'll be disappointed; he couldn't be less professional for a detective.- Our protagonist, Anna, has had yet another terrible shock. How is her reaction described? "Anna, as pale as if she'd seen a ghost...." Really, was this written by a twelve-year-old?- "If he had to watch Tebbins pace like a whirling dervish, which Tebbins had just begun to do....." That's awkward enough; for one thing, dervishes WHIRL, they don't pace. But there's more; less than a page later she drives her point home when the other cop says "I've never been locked up with a whirling dervish before." So subtle.- The protagonist's sister is asked "You can account for your whereabouts after you left the museum?" and so help me, she replies, "You bet your sweet bippy I can." Oh - my - God.- Anna tells the detective that her sister would never harm her that way. His well-thought-out response: "That's what they all say."- The cop is at Anna's front door, and he pounds on it "loud enough to wake the dead."- "Jackson believed that troubles come in threes" - yeah, why not? - and a minute later, when discovering that several pieces of equipment were malfunctioning for no apparent reason, he muses that "apparently they had gremlins tonight."- One character, supposed to be a stuffy intellectual but obviously in need of a thesaurus, refers to the police as "cretins" FOUR times in about three pages. (All I could think of was Niles Crane, or maybe Charles Emerson Winchester III.)So much of the dialogue was just bizarre. She goes to a colleague to break the news about a theft and says"Ivar, listen carefully. You need to call the police."He looked blankly at her. "Did someone break something?"Why would they call the police because someone - broke - something?Here's a mention of the scene where a body was found: "Students who had so little money that they never used air conditioning, which meant that determining the time of death was going to get real chancy. The body had been warm when he'd arrived on the scene, but so had the house. And the lack of air conditioning was no clue to anything at all." Can someone explain THAT to me? Was the lack of AC going to make it MORE difficult (or "real chancy," as the professionals would say), or less? As I see it, that last line in particular makes no sense, or at least contradicts the first one.Just a few more points. WAY too much time is spent on one of the cops and his problems with his teenage daughter. The dialogue between the two cops (who are from two different jurisdictions so I'm still not quite sure why they're both on the case) is absurd, with their constant references to which of them is "Holmes" and which is "Watson" - how do those jerks ever solve a real crime? One of them, so odious that you really hope he's actually the criminal, is described as a "Poirot wannabe" and "twirls his mustache" about four dozen times in the book. By the end, he has been forced to get rid of the mustache so he can't "do" Poirot anymore; instead, he acquires a rumpled raincoat and starts saying "Oh, just one more thing, sir....." because...well, I don't have to tell you why. And everyone still takes him seriously, as if he WEREN'T acting like a kid constantly playing dress-up in superhero costumes.Also, the entire plot centers around a "Mayan curse" concerning a tomb that was disturbed during some engineering works. Everyone - police, the museum personnel, the press - talked about the curse as if it were something that was documented and that thousands had witnessed, like a hurricane. The seriousness with which it was gone on about, over and over, defied belief. As did the final showdown with the killer. I admit that I'd pegged the wrong person as the perp, but the killer's entire motivation was so out-there that it was impossible to take seriously. (And no, the book wasn't intended as a satire or spoof. It was clearly meant as a serious mystery. I've read hundreds of these things and I can tell the difference.)There's more, but I've already spent more time on this than it deserves.Read moreRead less
