This review contains spoilers.This, Bingham’s first published novel, has been much admired as providing perhaps the first realistic depiction of police interrogation methods, and also one of the best. Julian Symons called it ‘wholly original’ and said that ‘although [it] has been flattered by a good many imitators it remains supreme of its kind.’The story is told in the first person by the title character. He tells an expedient lie to the police in the course of a murder investigation. He is caught out, and covers his retreat by resorting to further lies, prevarications, and selective truths. The police play him by timing their revelation of information to him purely with a view to compromising him. There is a statement, then a revised statement, and finally a second revised statement. Each reveals the falsity of the previous one.The police, a double act of inspector and sergeant, are initially ostensibly polite, but their sincerity is feigned and they become progressively more intimidatory. The inspector leads the questioning and the sergeant acts the impartial witness / mediator. On occasion when Sibley questions the inspector’s approach, the inspector appeals to the sergeant as to his reasonableness. The sergeant duly points out to Sibley that it is he, Sibley, who is being awkward.This is indeed remarkably well put down, but it occupies only a part of the novel. The whole might be titled ‘Michael Sibley, An Autobiography’. It relates Sibley’s life in his own words from his school days to his marriage. As Sibley is a dull, insignificant, self-absorbed, unenterprising, and weak individual, the book is, considered as a whole, correspondingly dull and uninteresting. Sibley spends most of his time doing what he thinks people want him to do and otherwise taking the line of least resistance. He is aware of this, but he seems incapable of changing, alternating between self-justification and self-reproach. One finds oneself in whole-hearted agreement with Sibley’s bête noire, the dreadful Prosset, when he says to Sibley, ‘Fundamentally, you are not a bad-natured sort of chap, I suppose. But that is all I can say for you. You are weak and dull. Dreadfully dull, Sibley. You are lacking all wit … A worthy plodding lump of a suet, that’s you’. Bingham skillfully brings out Sibley’s character, but in a first person narrative this creates problems. The self-described thoughts and pre-occupations of dull people make for dull reading.Sibley is brought to trial but is acquitted. The case against him is not strong, being circumstantial and based mainly on his own lies, which can be as easily explained by his own weakness of character as by his guilt.Sibley apparently has pretensions as a writer of fiction. At the end of the book he seems to be making a living at it. Given his total lack of imagination I find this highly implausible. It is one of the odd  - and not very satisfactory - aspects of the book that it ends with Sibley living in a bucolic happy ever after land that is every Englishman’s dream, thanks to his abilities as a writer, a couple of modest legacies, and a loving wife who is the model of dutiful domesticity. It is all very unlikely and unconvincing.Some readers have noted that we have only Sibley’s word for it that he is not the killer and have suggested that he may in fact be so and that his story is another dissimulation, one that cannot be disproved. This is arguable, and indeed appealing, but it really is difficult to imagine Sibley murdering anyone, especially not in the way in which the murder is done (in a violent confrontation) rather than the way in which Sibley envisaged it (pushing the unsuspecting victim off a cliff).
