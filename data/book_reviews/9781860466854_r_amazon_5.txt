When white blindness strikes down all but one human soul, you would be forgiven for thinking you have heard this story before.  Why read another descent into horror, a hellscape that veers toward nothingness, which asks if humanity is a mistake best forgotten?  In these times of bewildering cruelty, is this a book for those despairing of the daily headlines?As if the content were not difficult enough, Saramago does not use most punctuation.  Perhaps in sympathy with his afflicted subjects, he removes the guideposts of grammar and dialogue, ensuring only the determined, with some circling back, persist.Despite all this, or because of it, the answer is: YES.  A thousand times, YES.[SPOILER ALERT, but not really.]We are introduced to the nameless, initial victims of sudden blindness – and one who is not – in an ophthalmologist’s office.  Each is trying to improve their sight, and the doctor tries to help.  They succumb to the blindness of patient zero, who visits the clinic while they are present, and all are struck down within hours. They are immediately quarantined in an abandoned asylum by an unidentified health ministry.  The only doctor in the asylum is the ophthalmologist, now a victim himself, accompanied by his seeing wife, who feigns blindness to remain with her husband. In the filthy whiteness of the sanatorium, they and many others who follow must fend for themselves.At first, food is dropped off by the military guarding the outside world from the blind.  One day, the food and running water ends, without explanation.The white horrors of the asylum deepen, until it is hell itself, especially for the women.  A group of blind thugs takes control of all remaining food, led by a blind man with a gun.  Everything becomes transactional.  These are exceedingly difficult pages to navigate, and yet I was transfixed by the women’s fight to exist as human beings.These women are the heroes who keep everyone striving.  I say “striving” because there is no hope. What drives them forward is something elemental — the need to survive, certainly, but always, with  insistence by the doctor’s wife, even as the memory of what it means to be human fades, with what dignity and humanity are possible under the circumstances.Our group from the doctor’s office leaves the asylum and joins the post-apocalyptic hellscape outside.  In Saramago’s hands, however, the desperately hungry blind people are not zombies, no matter how many zombie films were released subsequent to this book.  His empathy for their plight never lets us forget they were once human with sight.There is an old woman on the first floor of the building of the girl who wears dark glasses, who has been reduced to killing and eating raw rabbits and chickens.  She comes across our wandering band and, craven as she is, comes to understand she has fallen, and soon chooses to depart a life where she has become less than human.There is the coming together of the young woman with dark glasses and the old man with the eyepatch, finding love, if that is what it is, while the world decomposes.In a church where a mass of blind rest, reminiscent of War of the Worlds, the doctor’s wife is startled to see the baroque statues and images of Jesus and the saints with white bandages over their eyes.  God cannot see them, and they are alone, which drive the assembled devout out of the church in a panic.The doctor’s wife is the only person with sight, and she bears witness to the full scale of the calamity.  Finding a little food after an exhausting search, knowing the others will die without it, her spirit heaves, but the dog who licks tears sustains her.Throughout, the doctor‘s wife is the only one who sees it all, as if she has been selected by the gods to determine if our species is worth saving.  She stands as a mountain on whose slight shoulders the fate of humanity somehow rests.  Human in every way, both in her empathy toward strangers and her capacity to overcome the horrors of humans fallen into abject white darkness, she joins my pantheon of our greatest characters of literature.Many on here ask:  what is the point?
