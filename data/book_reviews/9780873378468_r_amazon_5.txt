I'm an attorney. I've used this book. I've recommended specific forms from this book to non-lawyers. (Hey, it's cheaper and easier than Bender's for the simple stuff. e.g. Amazon sellsBender's Forms of Discovery: Complete Library (Bender's Forms of Discovery, 1-16)for 4 figures.)Should *you* purchase this book?If you're an attorney, sure. If you have the training and experience to recognize whether a particular form is sufficient for a particular purpose in your particular jurisdiction, then these forms can be convenient. If you have had the good sense to avoid law school, should you purchase this book? These forms could be dangerous to use. The problem, of course, is that it is literally impossible for a book to engage in the analysis required to determine whether one of the forms in this book will meet your particular need(s) in the particular jurisdiction(s) involved.The authors devote three whole paragraphs to the question "Do You Need a Lawyer." They, of course, don't give a yes or no answer. They don't even provide an algorithm you can apply to your situation to arrive at an answer. Because they simply cannot do so. They do maintain "[c]reating a solid written agreement - especially where a lot of money or property is at stake - will occasionally mean obtaining the advice of a lawyer." I think that understates the problem. Drastically.For example, chapter 4 deals with landlord-tenant forms. Landlord-tenant law is ridiculously local. Hyper local. It doesn't vary just from state to state, it changes from city to city. The forms you need to use in New York City may be completely different from the forms you need to use in Albany. Never mind the differences between New York and California.Another example, some of chapter 10 is generally applicable because it deals disputing credit card debt - which is governed, in part, by federal law. But you may have state law rights that exceed the federal protections. If you have 10k in credit card debt, would you want to risk it?Read moreRead less
