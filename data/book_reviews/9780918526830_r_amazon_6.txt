Li-Young Lee is an amazing poet who has the capacity to bring his readers on a sorrowful journey with him. His use of imagery allows one to see the roses, to taste the fear, to smell the dirt, to feel the cold, and to hear the cries. He is not afraid to be open and bare his soul to anyone who will listen. The experience of watching his father being taken away and dealing with that loss as a child is extremely personal, yet he writes from a perspective of one who is thankful for the strength it brought him. Though he is a contemporary American poet, one can also see the similarity between confessional poetry and his desire to express emotions and situations that some might believe are too personal to share. The reoccurring theme of memory reminds me of William Wordsworth and Samuel Coleridge because they too believe that “the characters survive through the telling, the teller survives by his telling; by his voice brinking silence does he survive (“Furious Versions”). He chastises those who would rather forget his/her struggles as if it is not a part of who he/she is because “[a]fter all, it was only our life, our life and its forgetting” (“For a New). He also believes in a connection between all things in this world. His experiences have the ability to impact someone else’s life; therefore, we are all connected somehow. The fact that he mainly uses pronouns to identify his characters instead of names suggests the possibility of his struggles and emotions being universally shared. When reading about how even as a child he noticed other people’s misfortune and struggles, one can see the empathy Lee feels towards others, which again goes back to his belief that we are all connected. In his famous poem “The City in Which I Love You,” he expresses his empathy and connection to other people by stating, “that man was not me; / his would was his, his death not mine.” Even though it was not his pain, he still could feel sorrow for the man’s family.
