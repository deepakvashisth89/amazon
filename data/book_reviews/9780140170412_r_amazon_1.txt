This is a great book and all, but really there is one thing that must be addressed: Alistair Horne has a time machine.I have never read a chronicle that so finely describes a single battle and all the chaotic forces interacting inside it. There's no other explanation. He must have a time machine. Reading "The Price of Glory" brings you inside the heads of soldiers, both infantrymen and generals. You see the macro-level decision making that brought these armies against one another and you have the micro-level heroics of individual German and French soldiers who can turn battles with the toss of a grenade, the stab of a bayonet. The balance between those two story lines - the planning and strategy of the staffs and the on the ground battles between soldiers - is the best I've ever read as well. Horne must have been there.A lot of books covering the Great War walk too heavily on the macro side. For some, like "The First World War" by Keegan, there's too much to cover to get into the intricacies of trench warfare. They describe the horror, but not the detail. "The Price of Glory" describes in depth the tactics of trench warfare, how the trenches were arranged and conquered, even specific battles for dugouts or trench lines. For those that don't know, it was not simply one German trench staring at one French trench. Ribbons of forward and defensive trenches ring strategic fire points, command dug outs, and overlapping fields of fire. Horne draws the defensive and offensive arrangements in great detail.Horne also does a fine job bringing alive the main characters of this battle: obstinate Falkenhayn (the German war chief who presided over the battle plans, without allowing much discussion at the general staff), resilient Petain (roused from the bed of his mistress to command the armies at Verdun), and the air aces fighting in the sky above Verdun (one of whom would hop out of his plane and drive full steam to Paris for a night out, returning just before roll call in the morning). Meanwhile on the ground Horne tells of the heroics of Sergeant Kunze (who captured Fort Douaumont and 50 odd French soldiers by himself after accidentally being blown into the moat), the German scouts sneaking into French lines at the start of the battle, and Lt Col Driant who designed the elaborate Verdun defenses and died firing into a crowd of advancing Germans (just after yelling "they haven't hit me yet.") and the doomed defenders of Fort Vaux (survived for a week with no food or water, surrounded by Germans). Only Driant's meticulous preparations kept the French alive long enough to resist the initial surge, during which is was estimated 80,000 shells landed in an area measuring 500 by 1000 yards.Things that stuck out to me was the insanity of the French's attacking strategy, which could be described simply as: attack no matter what. It was widely assumed blind valor could overcome bullets. Petain was known chiefly by the soldiers he commanded for valuing their life and relying on blistering artillery bombardments rather than suicidal frontal charges. I was also struck by the intricacies of trench warfare - how a few soldiers sneaking through lines could compel surrender and advance rapidly. Finally, the obedience of the German army to follow orders was what got it into trouble. No one questions Falkenhayn's plan - which was basically to "bleed the enemy white" and to cause catastrophic losses. Generally when you empower one person to make huge huge decisions you either get fantastic results (Steve Jobs) or terrible results (Caligula). The Battle of Verdun was the latter. Falkenhayn's plan was asinine, and though the execution was admirable, it made no strategic sense for a country fighting a two front war. The general staff obeyed, and the Germans paid for it.Unlike Biff Tannen Alistair Horne has made good use of his time machine. The writing is fine, and doesn't read like most history books from 1961. There is a great balance between the generals and their maps and the soldiers and their bayonets. This book will teach you about the meat grinder of Verdun - it basically takes you inside the butcher shop. I'm glad you developed your time machine, Horne. Use it wisely.Read moreRead less
