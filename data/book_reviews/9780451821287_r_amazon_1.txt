This movie tie-in begins with the novelette Cycle of the Werewolf.  The novelette is problematic.  It started out life as a horror-themed calendar that was, frankly, a poor idea that could not be realized.  The novelette was a compromise, featuring some bizarre vignettes and illustrations by Bernie Wrightson that would have gone in the calendar.  The impossibility of this idea cannot be more evident upon reading the book:  There is no way they could have fit the vignettes, illustrations, and calendar grids in one package.  A terrible idea.But an intriguing book, nonetheless.  This is the book that got me hooked on werewolf fiction despite its flaws.  How?  I give credit to the illustrations.  You know how a picture is worth a thousand words?  It's true.  Those illustrations add so much to the vignettes.  And you have Stephen King's blueprint:  Even though there's not much there, you can read this book and easily fill in the blanks about how this story would have looked full-length, fantasizing about what the characters said and did in-between full moons.  An utterly incomplete story replete with flawed, lazy writing, but a fun read all the same.And then there's Silver Bullet.  Silver Bullet, to me, represents a high-water mark and low-culture mark in the Stephen King phenomenon.  Seems like a contradiction, right?  Hear me out:  This movie represents how bankable King was to the American people and how hopelessly desperate Hollywood was to take advantage of that bankability.  I mean, truthfully, how many other abandoned calendar projects can you think of that ended up getting made into movies?  Silver Bullet is proof that Hollywood would latch onto anything that had King's name on it in 1985.  The movie failed at the box office, and, though a staple of cable TV (especially in October), is largely dismissed by just about everyone.  This is unfortunate, as it's a decent movie:  Mediocre by mainstream standards; pretty good by genre standards; but one of the best of the werewolf subgenre.  It certainly doesn't represent the pinnacle of King adaptations (Carrie, The Shining, Misery, The Shawshank Redemption, The Green Mile), but it's also certainly not one of the schlock adaptations either (I think Graveyard Shift is probably the worst of his adaptations).What does the screenplay offer?  Well, when compared directly with the book, the screenplay gives you an excellent picture of what a full-length novel about a small-town plagued by a werewolf could have been if King had done things differently.  There was room for a masterpiece here.It also proves what Roger Ebert suspected 35 years ago:  Silver Bullet was written to be intentionally humorous.  Why?  To begin, King had sold several things to Hollywood by 1985, and he had sold about a half dozen projects to Dino de Laurentiis, who produced Silver Bullet.  All of King's screenplays had been previously rejected by producers.  I suspect he thought this one would be rejected too, and so he just said screw it, let's have some fun with this.  I also suspect he may have been under the weather when he wrote it.  His substance abuse during this time is well-known.  He goes back and forth with identifying Lester Lowe as a Catholic priest and then a page later calling him a Baptist reverend, as in the novelette.  Plus, the werewolf and other characters sing the Rheingold beer jingle several times.  Regrettably, the beer jingle is only sang once in the produced movie.  Regardless, de Laurentiis bought the humorous screenplay and produced a mostly-faithful adaptation.Perhaps the best part of this book is the foreword written by King.  King's forewords on his re-released materials are always golden reading, and this one is no different, chronicling the improbable journey from a busted calendar project to a Hollywood movie.  That foreword alone makes the purchase worthwhile.  Five stars.Read moreRead less
