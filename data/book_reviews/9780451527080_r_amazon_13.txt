In his day, Sinclair Lewis was much more famous and successful than his contemporary F Scott Fitzgerald. So it's an odd reversal of fortune that the latter is now so much better known.To my mind, Babbitt works as a companion piece to Lewis's 'Main Street'. But whereas Main Street satirises small town America, Babbitt focuses on urban life. Without giving too much away, the titular character George Babbitt undergoes a crisis in confidence about American society, and the question is whether he can resist the conformist pressures that seek to reclaim him and turn him back into a model citizen. In following Babbitt's journey, Sinclair skewers the banality of the 1920s American dream - its limited notions of success, its unthinking drive for more. There are themes that should still resonate deeply.In order to get his dialogue right, Lewis apparently spent months studying how contemporary Americans spoke. Much of it will sound weird and archaic to modern ears, but for me that was a plus.Overall, Babbitt is a window into the social fabric of a great country one century ago: the fashions, the preoccupations, the fears.
