William Manchester's opus on General Douglas MacArthur is worthy of any and all acclaim it's garnered. It is a timeless piece that should capture the imagination of even the most pedestrian layman with a bit of historical curiosity. 1000 pages of small print and not a word wasted or an irrelevant anecdote.MacArthur, his father and his grandfather lead interesting lives. Manchester sets the tone of expectation weighing on MacArthur from an early age. His father casts a long shadow as the hero of Cemetery Ridge from the pivotal Civil War battle of Chattanooga at age 19 and ultimately rising to be a 4 star General.Douglas MacArthur's life is stuffed with ambition, contradiction and accomplishment. He is the highest ranked graduate of West Point at the turn of the century and one of the 3 highest ever and yet 25 years later he dramatically changes the education. He is heroic and fearless in battle earning 7 silver stars in WWI and yet still feels the need for a bit of embellishment. He is one of the greatest strategic commanders of all time and yet he failed to prepare Bataan and Corregidor for defense in the early battles of WWII.American high schools teach three things about MacArthur:  that he lead the ground troop in the Pacific during WWII, that he oversaw Japan after the war and that he swung the Korean war back from near defeat with his brilliant surprise attack at Incheon behind enemy lines.But there is so much to this man. He is born in the 19th century and remains a 19th century aristocrat throughout his life but becomes a thoroughly modern general that learned more from the wasteland of the trench warfare of the Great War then just about anyone. He saw the potential in airplanes and the importance of mobility and flexibility over fighting for fixed space.Having fought brilliant campaigns he completely reverses course and becomes an equally adept diplomat and administrator in Japan working 7 days a week for 5 years to implement policy after policy that lead to both economic recovery and a sustainable democracy. The how's of his battle leadership and oversight of Japan are fascinating and lead at least for me to a revised and much more admiring opinion of MacArthur. He has faded from history much more than his towering accomplishments deserve.This is really 3, 4 or 5 books. MacArthur in the Great War took at least 7 bullets through his uniform. He lead from the front and he is a genuine hero. MacArthur between the wars was far busier than most of his potential peers. His military actions against WWI pensioners protesting in Washington was excessive and needlessly violent. Yet he also reformed West Point and banned much of the brutal hazing. And much more. MacArthur of WWII is worth multiple studies. He had the least resources of any area commander. He had the lowest casualties, the best ratios, always did what was least expected and delivered victory after victory (84 successful amphibious landings!). His role in Japan is no less spectacular. Korea and then his termination by Truman are again another story. In all he is there at almost all key points of US 20th century history.By the time of his funeral at end of the book I was truly thinking that I too would miss the man. He spoke so well. I think I've said "did you know he did.." at least 20 times in the last few weeks to listeners equally surprised as I was. You cannot believe it's all one man both the good and bad. He's a fascinating mix of accomplishment, staged drama, ego and so often misunderstood or resented. Manchester ties it all together. It's still fresh (40 years later) and probably even more important to appreciate what he did and how he did it. He begged LBJ to stay out of Vietnam on his deathbed. He would have seen the folly of the most recent wars and their equally poor civil administration.  If I could give it the 6 stars that the General wanted I certainly would!Read moreRead less
