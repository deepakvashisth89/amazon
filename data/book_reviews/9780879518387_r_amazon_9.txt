This is a valuable work of reference for those trying to understand the Bronte story, but apart from the introduction it consists only of original letters devoid of explanation or narrative, and so is not a good way to learn about the Brontes for the first time.  Juliet Barker is to be commended for providing us with Bronte material in it's original form, complete with spelling mistakes and errors without any of the tidying up that others have done.  She has also traced each letter to it's original where possible - not easy as they have been spread all over the world in sales and auctions.  Having the letters follow on in date order with the year at the top of the page is very useful for research too.However, this is only a selection of letters, she says, as she has selected them for their importance, and in some cases they are only presented in part.  This implies a criterion for selection, reducing the value of the book a little for the researcher who might not see the Brontes in the same way as the author, who by her own admission is on a mission to 'demolish the myths', myths, that is, created by other writers, some of whom, like Elizabeth Gaskell, and Francis Leyland lived with and knew the Brontes and their friends.  Even Daphne du Maurier's 'The Infernal World of Branwell Bronte' (which I highly recommend) was written in a time (1960's) much closer in spirit to that of the Brontes, and free from the political correctness that colours our present world view.Barker, who qualified in History, is a stickler for facts, which would be fine if she were not so quick to dismiss the writings of others as 'pure fiction' just because obvious directly supporting evidence doesn't exist.  Understanding the Brontes is about understanding their minds, and their relationships, and what everyone did or didn't know - it is a task for the psychologist, and even the detective, as much as the historian, and in this respect Barker seems to me to sometimes be naive and lacking in insight.  Daphne du Maurier, easily condemned for being also a novelist, seems to me to show huge insight - most of what she says fits the evidence available, which she constantly refers to, though I assume that she is one of those that Barker pours scorn on (in her other book 'The Brontes').  Daphne du Maurier also had reason to know about people like Branwell Bronte, from personal life experience, so she could understand better than most the reasons for Branwell's apparent failure and demise under the influence of drink and drugs.  She was also the one to recognise that Branwell was the key to the whole Bronte saga, not only as a model for the men in his sister's novels, but also because it was he who inspired the girls to start writing about their imaginary lands and heroes in the 'Juvenillia',  Furthermore, he actually created in those childhood stories, which they all continued with into their twenties, many of the main characters of the novels; Heathcliff in Jane Eyre, and the men in Wuthering Heights.  Some letters, from Branwell's friends, stand out as hugely significant to anyone  struggling to understand the real Bronte Story.  Why do two of his friends write of how he read out to them, in the pub, a large part of what they later recognised beyond doubt as 'Wuthering Heights', even though Branwell is supposed not to have known of it's existence?  Did he in fact write it, as some have suggested, or did he come across it and take it with him (unlikely as Emily was protective), or did he write something that Emily later continued with? Emily was closer to Branwell than the others.There are some stark facts that the newcomer to the Brontes should know, but which are not emphasised enough.  While this book contains many letters from Charlotte, and quite a few from Branwell, there are few from Anne and I think none from Emily.  More importantly, no manuscripts have ever been seen for the novels that are claimed to be by Emily or Anne; only 'fair copies' by Charlotte for the publisher.  What do exist are the early writings of all four, the 'Juvenillia', which Barker has also provided us with in another book (they are almost impossible to read in original form even if magnified).  Astonishingly these are said to comprise over 100,000 words; more than all the novels put together, and it is perhaps these I think that provide the major clues to understanding the Brontes.  Why no original manuscripts, except in Charlotte's hand?  Once published, the importance of the original manuscripts would surely have been recognised by Patrick, the father, and by Charlotte, and even Arthur Bell Nichols, the curate she married.
