P.G. Wodehouse was undeniably a very gifted comedic writer.  His plots have more twists than an Agatha Christie novel causing readers to bend through curves they see coming and sometimes that they don't see coming.  The classic Wodehouse charm oozes from "Summer Moonshine", a spirited romp through the English countryside that is just a little too staid and familiar.Sir Buckstone Abbott, Buck to those who know him, is a true English baron - he has the name, the house, but not the money for upkeep, so he rents out rooms to paying customers to make ends meet.  However, after borrowing five hundred pounds to publish his memoirs, he finds himself in debt to one of those customers, as well as to the publisher of the book.  He therefore spends his day hiding from his guests, causing his daughter Imogene (Jane to those who know her) to be in charge of their meals and entertainment.  But when his guests start to have troubles of their own, Buck can no longer hide.  First there is Tubby Vanringham, who was engaged to Buck's secretary, Miss Whittaker, but broke off the engagement when he thought she was two-timing him.  In response, Miss Whittaker is set to sue Tubby for breach of promise.  Then Tubby's brother Joe shows up, smitten by Jane, and sends her secret fiance, the groveling ladies' man Adrian Peake, running (only to return in hilarious circumstances).  Not to mention that the brothers' stepmother, the Princess Dwornitzchek, who wants to buy Buck's home, is soon coming to settle matters and will be most upset to learn of Tubby's troubles.  Throw into the mix an unknown brother-in-law and an interesting array of guests, and Buck has his hands full and not a lot of hope that things will turn out his way."Summer Moonshine" is an enjoyable read, full of the classic Wodehouse humor, dated at times yet still applicable to today.  For anyone who has read a lot of Wodehouse, the plot twists will be familiar - readers know who will end up with whom and that those little twerps who need to be found out will be found out.
