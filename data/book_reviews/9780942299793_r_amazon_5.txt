Guy Debord's The Society of the Spectacle is not a text which can stand on its own. It is a Marxist polemic on the state of late capitalism in 1967. While the work is fabulous it is not rigorously argued, in so far as many of the arguments' presuppositions and Marxist defences are not elaborated. However, I cannot fault it for not being more rigorous. It is the product of a particular time and place and it serves to explore an idea that is, (in one variation or another) I think, essential to understanding the dissemination of ideology in late capitalist society. If you have a competent understanding of Marxism then Debord's sometime's unsubstantiated arguments will be clear and convincing. Without this background I would not recommend this as a text in critical theory. The work is divided into 9 chapters of 221 aphorisms or theses. Each chapter addresses a different aspect of the spectacle. His arguments regarding the manner in which the spectacle functions (socially, individually, environmentally, etc.) are, I think, his most important and compelling. With these alone one could develop a theory of the spectacle based on a coherency theory of truth. However, Debord goes beyond merely explaining the functions of the spectacle; he goes to great lengths to describe its genesis, history, and resolution as well. Despite the failed revolutions of 1968 this text is still an incredibly valuable work for today's society, perhaps now, more than ever.A note on the publication: This latest issue, published by Zone Books in 2012 and translated by Donald Nicholson-Smith, is a fantastic translation.
