Now and then, one runs across a book more impressive for what it attempts than what it achieves. Such a book is The Black Throne, in which Messrs. Zelazny and Saberhagen try to unite in a single narrative the collected works of Edgar Allen Poe.The authors' device to do so is that of parallel worlds through which a gifted individual can travel. (Did you say that Zelazny had already used that device?) In The Black Throne, the gifted individual is Annie, the heroine of Poe's poem "Annabel Lee." As a child, she creates a seaside playground to which she draws her otherwordly soulmate, Edgar Allen Poe, along with his counterpart from our own world, Edgar Allen Perry. As the three children grow, Poe becomes a dreamer; Perry, a man of action.The action whose man he is starts when Annie, now an adult, is kidnapped by criminals of Poe's world. They want to use her power of transference between worlds in an alchemist's schemeto transmute lead into gold. With the aid of a group that wants (or does it?) to thwart the scheme, Perry is exchanged with Poe so that the man of action can prevent the transformation. This, if completed, would destroy the psychic connection among the three childhood friends.As Perry strives to rescue Annie, he experiences a series of ever more harrowing adventures that Poe, now trapped in alcoholic despair in our world, transcribes from his fevered dreams. In the end, no one is quite what he or she seems...In The Black Throne, the authors display enough inventiveness to fuel several novels. At times, sadly, the authors' inventiveness works against itself. In their zeal to incorporate every character and setting of Poe, they complicate the plot so much that only a lifelong fan of Poe can follow it. Furthermore, many of the characters (not least of them Poe himself) are treated skimpily. Some of them behave little like Poe's description of them.Nonetheless, The Black Throne is worth reading for its ingenuity of theme.
