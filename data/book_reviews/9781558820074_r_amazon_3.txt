In my reading life (which I wish was the majority of my life, but alas, bills must be paid), I have lately begun alternating between recent/contemporary books and older/classic books. I've been having some fun tracing the evolution of a few genres, including mystery, by comparing what I read today vs. what mystery lovers read back in the day (1930s-1960s). The conclusion I'm coming to is that yesterday's books relied much more on story and plot, while a lot of today's books seem like a collection of characters in search of a plot. Also, many of the older books seem to rely on DETECTION and sleuthing, while many newer books rely on intuition, coincidence, last-chapter confessions, and so forth. Perhaps it's a manifestation of readers in our psychological age being more interested in people, and what makes them tick, than in matching wits with a sleuth.I had read an article calling Craig Rice "mystery's funnybone," so I was intrigued enough to seek out one of her books. Truthfully, I hadn't heard of her before reading that article. I went into 8 FACES AT 3 thinking it was going to be tough for ANYONE to be funnier than Celia Fremlin. I'd say Rice is amusing and tells a good story with plenty of the mystery conventions in evidence. In contrast, Fremlin is a much, much finer writer, but her plots are often somewhat tepid. Overall I prefer Fremlin but I had a moderate amount of fun reading this book. A nasty old woman, Aunt Alexandria, is murdered in her wheelchair; and, simultaneously, all clocks in the house stop at 3 a.m., an old superstition regarding what happens when someone is murdered.The setting is the Chicago suburbs, and while the local authorities investigate the case, the real investigators are an unlikely, and often drunk, trio: Helene Brand, local heiress and party girl; Jake Justus, publicist and general doofus; and John J. Malone, a not-entirely-savory lawyer. Helene happens to be the next-door neighbor of the murdered woman, and she gets involved in the investigation to protect her friend, who's the victim's recently-and-secretly-eloped niece.The humor here comes from witty (drunken) repartee, and overall the tone is lighter than other drunks-as-protagonists books like Hammett's THE THIN MAN. The story kept me involved (maybe it went on a touch too long), though I feel like there wasn't much detection happening, just a series of mini-capers followed by the occasional observation or epiphany (which would have come sooner, the characters admit, if they weren't drunk all the time). Would I read more by Craig Rice?  I think I would, but I'm not sure I'll actively seek more out.I realize I am probably showing a bit of my own prejudice here. I've never been a person who finds drunkenness or alcoholism funny, so I really had to let that part of myself go, in order to appreciate this book. I'm probably a bit uptight about it so I worked on not letting my biases get too much in the way of the reading experience. I will say that I really enjoyed Helene Brand, with her witty bon mots, her grace, and the way she flagrantly flouts the rules (for example, by orchestrating a jailbreak) while charming the socks off just about everyone.
