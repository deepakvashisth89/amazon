A bit of speculation here.I'm currently reading this antique copy of Thorne Smith's TURNABOUT (1931) (he's the author ofTOPPER).It's the story of Sally and Tim Willows, a young couple (he's 35, she's 28) who've been married for five years. Lately all they seem to do is argue. They have in their house a statue of Egyptian god Ram that contains the deity's spirit. Ram is tired of the Willows' constantly fighting and he's pondering a way to teach them a lesson.I haven't gotten there yet, but what will happen is Ram will cause the Willows' to walk in each others' shoes-- he'll switch bodies on them. Now Sally, who's terminally bored at home, will find out what it's like to commute to Tim's miserable ad agency job, and Tim, who thinks his wife is spoiled and foolish to complain about her life of freedom, will discover what housewifery is actually like. The kicker is, after they learn how to function in their new roles, Sally will impregnate Tim!But I'm wondering if playwright Joseph Kesselring, who completedARSENIC AND OLD LACEin 1939, read this passage from TURNABOUT. To set it up:There's a party at the Willows' place and as usual Tim is laying low. Their friends come over, swallow copious quantities of potent potables and swap partners for a bit of necking. Although quite lit up himself, Tim notices Sally and a big guy named Carl Bentley are missing. He goes into the kitchen and finds them kissing. Carl has his back to Tim, who bashes the interloper's head with a rolling pin. It's "murder most foul" to quote Sally quoting the Bard.Realizing that Carl is very dead, Tim says..."...What are we going to do with his nibs?"Sally took a sip and considered this in silence. From the lounge came the clatter of the radio and the hubbub of many voices."Bury him," she said at last. "That's what they do with bodies. They bury them."Mr. Willows regarded the erstwhile Carl Bentley distastefully. "We'd have to dig a regular Panama Canal to tuck that body in," he observed.
