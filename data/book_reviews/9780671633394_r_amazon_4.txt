Mr. Hayes interviewed me for this book in 1987 and I can only speak to the accuracy of those things he interviewed me on and my time working at Karisoke Research Centre as well as those things told to me by Dian Fossey and others.As far as my own small part and interview much of the information was not accurate.  My conversations were tape recorded so I am not sure what happened.  The book was completed after his death so that might explain some of the inaccuracies or perhaps my part in the history of it was so small as the accuracy of details was not that important or significant.  Interestingly, Farley Mowat, who wrote Woman in the Mists, had more accurate details of my time at Karisoke than did Mr. Hayes and Mr. Mowatt never interviewed me.Mr. Hayes does give a more accurate view of Dian and the effect many years of isolation, disappointment, and sometimes her perception of betrayals had on her.  I truly liked Dian but hated her behavior at times that was often erratic and seemed to border on madness and even cruelty.  One day she could be pleasant and the next day it seemed as if dark clouds descended on her head and everyone was her enemy.  It was her behavior that probably posed the greatest risk to her research and reputation in her later years at Karisoke.  In some ways her death helped prevent that and gave her a martyrdom status which history and the media capitalize on.Dian's love and dedication for the mountain gorillas clearly comes through in the book and the sacrifices she made especially in the early years.  There were few comforts, communication was limited to mail and food delivery twice a week that the porters brought to camp.  Her accomplishments were unparalleled.For those of us who got the rare privilege to be at Karisoke during the life of Dian Fossey it was the experience of a life time.
