I have wanted to read this book for years.  I was only 10 years old when it was published in 1973, but I was certainly aware of it when I was a teenager.  I’m actually glad that I waited until this advanced age to read it.  What resonates with me now is much different from what would have resonated in my 20’s, 30’s, and 40’s.  This would be a good book to read once a decade as our perspectives naturally change with time and experience.Fear of Flying is the story of Isadora Wing, a well educated poet and writer.  She has been married to her second husband for 5 years and finds their relationship lacking in several ways.  Having been in analysis herself for years, it is no surprise that she marries an analyst and is frustrated with his demeanor and behavior. At an analyst’s conference in Vienna, she becomes attracted to another analyst and has a fling.  She eventually decides to leave her husband and travel through Europe with the new guy.  Of course, things are never as they seem and she realizes that Adrian has his own self-serving agenda that does not include her.  Now alone, Isadora must examine her choices and her desires so that she can decide her next move.Author Erica Jong became famous for her idea of the “zipless f***.”  This is one of those ideas that I label as good in theory and impossible in practice.  Basically, the ZF occurs between two strangers who have consensual sex free from ulterior motives, power plays, guilt, or remorse.  Good luck with that!  As Ms. Jong points out, the ZF is “rarer than a unicorn.”But Fear of Flying is about so much more.  The title is both literal and figurative.  Isadora is afraid to fly on an airplane.  She is also afraid to “fly” on her own without a man and to ‘fly”in the face of conventional norms for women in that era.  There is a wonderful section toward the end of chapter 12 where Isadora reflects on her first marriage.  She talks about feeling torn between her duty as a wife (a duty she sees as being subservient to the needs and wants of her husband) and her own self-preservation.  She wisely chooses herself but feels like a traitor.  Isadora concludes “I was not a good woman.  I had too many other things to do.”  Powerful stuff.I gave this four stars only because I thought the beginning was a little slow.  I had a hard time getting interested in the book.  The novel really picked up for me when Isadora begins to reflect on her previous relationships and her role in them.  It was then that I was really struck with both Ms. Jong’s humor and her ability to get to the heart of problems that still plague women 47 years later.Read moreRead less
