The author of "Kaffir Boy", at the behest of fans, wrote a follow up titled "Kaffir Boy in America".  Essentially it is a book to catch us all up on what happened to him once he left South African soil and reached America.  He came across trials and tribulations right away in the form of culture shock, his inferior tennis skills and his sub-par South African education.Without a doubt this book lacks the potency of its predecessor.  While "Kaffir Boy" was a hard hitting book giving the readers insight into the lives of the Black South African under the suffocating grip of apartheid, "Kaffir Boy in America" was about how an immigrant adapts to American life.  There were no life threatening situations, no starving, no beatings, no suicide contemplations or being dipped in human waste.  There was a lot of compare and contrast and a LOT of his opinions.Now we get to understand Mark’s political, social and religious beliefs.  You may agree with some, or all of them, or you may disagree with some or all of them.  I, for one, did not care to read many of his opinions on how the world should be and how mankind should be.  Not that I found his opinions repulsive, it's just that I didn't pick the book up for that.  I never considered Mark Mathabane a political or religious leader, nor did I read either of his books for his political or religious views.  I read them for the vivid and in-depth chronicling of life in South Africa under apartheid and subsequently his life in America.I found myself quite disappointed in Mark halfway through the book.  He came off as indecisive, noncommittal and unable to deal with challenges.  You may be reading this review thinking: “He faced and overcame all kind of hardships in South Africa and he was committed to make it to America.”  Yes he did and yes he was; which made his behavior in America all the more surprising.What more I learned about Mark is that his deepest and most fruitful relationships were forged with White people.  How do I know that?  Because he made sure to mention the race of many of the people who made an impact upon him.  For someone who claims that we all need to coexist and live in harmony, why does he mention race so much?  Why does it matter what the race is of those you dated, or those you bonded with?  As for his most interpersonal relationships being with Whites instead of Blacks, I’m not saying that this is good or bad, but as a Black reader I did glean a certain implication.  “You, Oh Black Boy or Black Girl, if you want to succeed in this life, you will need to make some strong bonds with White people.”  That may not have been his message at all but it comes through in the writing.  He heavily relied on Stan Smith and his family up until Stan was like a surrogate father.  He unabashedly went to the Stan Smith well over and over to help him through his financial woes.  When mentioning the Black women he dated he only mentioned they were attractive but with the White women he dated he had deep bonds with them up until he married one.  Then there was his German neighbor that he connected so well with and others that helped him throughout his life.  The fact that he had these relationships and he benefited so much from them is not a problem with me, I just found it ironic and a bit perturbing that he had so few healthy and strong relationships with Blacks.This is not to say the book was devoid of any good.  He made some astute observations about American culture that I agreed with.  At times I thought he was a little too critical considering his plot in life had improved astronomically by virtue of being in America.  But he did recognize America’s virtue above the situation he’d come from.  Furthermore, we did get an update about his family and the seemingly good path they were now upon because of his success."Kaffir Boy in America" is like most movie sequels: not as good as the first.  The book just lacks that “it” factor that the first had.  He readily admitted in his preface that he only wrote this book because of so many requests from fans to find out what happened to him and his family after he came to America.  So what you find is that he ends up having some of the problems that many Black Americans have except with many many more opportunities.  He also has the problems that many immigrants have except with much more support and many many more opportunities.  The book is far from shallow.
